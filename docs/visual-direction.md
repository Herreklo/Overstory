# Visual Direction — A Living Style Guide

*An open exploration of the visual identity for future games and worlds built with The Lab.*

## The overall vision

We want to create beautiful, distinctive, stylized 3D worlds that feel alive, inviting, and full of character.

The visual style should balance **artistic expression, atmosphere, readability, and practical production**. It should feel intentionally designed rather than generic, overly realistic, or obviously procedurally generated.

A handcrafted appearance is appealing, but the assets themselves do not need to be handmade. Through procedural modeling, reusable systems, AI-assisted creation, thoughtful shading, and strong art direction, we want to explore how far automated production can go.

The goal isn't to reproduce any existing game's aesthetic. It's to discover our own visual identity through experimentation.

## 1. Visual character

The direction we're currently most interested in could be described as **illustrative, stylized 3D with expressive shapes and exceptional lighting**.

Qualities worth exploring:

- **Strong silhouettes and shapes:** Buildings, people, vegetation, and objects should be recognizable and visually appealing without excessive detail.
- **Illustrative simplicity:** Clean geometry, deliberate proportions, controlled textures, and an artistic rather than photorealistic appearance.
- **Handcrafted character:** Subtle irregularities, appealing shapes, and materials that feel thoughtfully designed, even when generated automatically.
- **Selective detail:** Richness where it matters, balanced with calm surfaces and visual breathing room.
- **Warmth and personality:** Worlds should feel inhabited, varied, and capable of expressing joy, excitement, intimacy, melancholy, tension, and everything in between.
- **A cohesive identity:** People, buildings, nature, lighting, and interfaces should feel like parts of the same world.

We are interested in experimenting with cel shading, soft toon shading, painterly materials, illustrated textures, and selective outlines. None of these techniques is mandatory. The visual result matters more than the particular rendering method.

## 2. Lighting and atmosphere

**Lighting could become one of the defining strengths of the visual identity.**

The lighting in *SEASON: A letter to the future* and *Firewatch* is especially inspiring.

We want to explore how lighting, color, atmosphere, and composition can make relatively simple 3D environments feel extraordinary.

This includes beautiful natural daylight, long shadows, warm interiors, soft ambient illumination, atmospheric depth, changing weather, and distinctive times of day.

Color should be expressive without becoming excessively saturated or artificially cheerful.

Importantly, the world must look good under ordinary conditions—not only during a carefully composed sunset. Dramatic lighting should enhance a strong visual foundation, not disguise weaknesses in it.

## 3. Architecture and worldbuilding

We're attracted to the idea of **worlds assembled through intelligent visual systems**.

*Townscaper* and *Tiny Glade* are particularly interesting references for their procedural approaches to architecture, visual harmony, and the feeling of carefully composed environments.

Buildings could be assembled from reusable components such as walls, roofs, windows, doors, trims, and decorative elements.

But the goal isn't simply efficient asset reuse. It's to create systems that produce variety while maintaining a coherent aesthetic.

A generated town should feel like a place with character, recognizable neighborhoods, different architectural identities, and the visible traces of its history.

The same philosophy could apply to interiors, furniture, nature, streets, music venues, businesses, and larger cities.

Ideally, places should also change visually as the simulated world develops.

## 4. People and characters

Characters are especially important because The Lab revolves around individuals and their lives.

We want stylized people with recognizable identities, expressive proportions, and believable movement.

The character reference previously shared—with elongated proportions, sculpted shapes, and an illustrative appearance—is an interesting starting point, but not a fixed direction.

A modular character system could provide variation in body proportions, faces, hairstyles, clothing, age, and personal style while maintaining visual consistency.

Animation should help communicate individuality through posture, movement, gestures, attention, and social interaction.

Characters need to look appealing both from a distance and in close-up, without requiring photorealistic faces or extremely elaborate animation.

Their appearance should also be capable of evolving with age, circumstances, and personal history.

## 5. Camera and scale

The world should support several levels of observation and interaction, potentially through a point-and-click system with a movable, rotatable, zoomable camera.

**World scale:** Landscapes, cities, towns, districts, roads, and geographical relationships. Strong composition and recognizable forms matter more than individual details.

**Neighborhood scale:** Streets, buildings, public spaces, visible activities, and moving residents. A useful scale for observing the world and interacting with locations.

**Personal scale:** Interiors, individual people, objects, conversations, and activities. More detail, stronger character expression, and closer observation.

Ideally, these views should share a coherent visual identity, even if different levels of detail, scene representations, or transitions are necessary.

A perfectly seamless zoom from an entire continent to a person's face is not a requirement.

The emphasis is on creating a satisfying, intuitive way to explore the world at different scales.

## 6. Interface and information design

The interface should complement the world rather than dominate it.

We want to explore interfaces that can communicate complex relationships, histories, choices, organizations, and world systems in an elegant and understandable way.

Different games may need very different interfaces. A music manager, detective game, and world explorer shouldn't necessarily share the same screen layouts.

However, they could share a coherent approach to typography, colors, icons, motion, composition, and information hierarchy.

The UI should help make the depth of the simulation accessible without turning every experience into a spreadsheet.

## 7. Production philosophy

The intended toolchain is:

- **Blender + AI assistance / Blender MCP:** Procedural modeling, asset generation, modular systems, characters, and animations.
- **Unity 6:** World assembly, materials, shaders, lighting, animation, cameras, interaction, and rendering.

The ambition is to avoid manually modeling individual assets wherever possible.

Rather than generating thousands of unrelated objects, we want to explore **systems that produce coherent families of assets**, with reusable geometry, materials, proportions, and variation rules.

Consistency, editability, performance, and visual quality are more important than generating large quantities of content.

The style should be developed through actual game-engine experiments, not judged solely by beautiful concept images.

## 8. Visual references and inspiration

These games represent qualities worth studying, not styles to combine literally or copy.

| Reference | What interests us |
|---|---|
| **Townscaper** | Procedural architecture, harmonious shapes, color, playful composition |
| **Tiny Glade** | Handcrafted appearance through procedural rules, environmental charm |
| **SEASON: A letter to the future** | Exceptional natural lighting, stylized realism, illustrative materials |
| **Firewatch** | Color, atmosphere, sunlight, environmental depth and composition |
| **Röki** | Simplified shapes, illustrative 3D environments, restrained detail |
| **Mythwrecked: Ambrosia Island** | Warmth, character, playful colors, cohesive environments |
| **Sable** | Graphic illustration, outlines, simplified geometry |
| **COCOON** | Lighting, composition, clarity at elevated camera distances |
| **art of rally** | Landscape simplification, scale, sparse but distinctive environments |
| **South of Midnight** | Expressive character proportions, sculptural and handcrafted qualities |

References from illustration, animation, architecture, film, photography, and physical miniatures may prove equally valuable.

**These references communicate what we appreciate, not what the final result must look like.**

## 9. Things to be careful about

A few risks are worth remembering:

- Creating attractive concept art that cannot realistically be reproduced in a game engine.
- Relying on dramatic lighting to make otherwise generic assets appealing.
- Generating visually inconsistent assets through independent AI workflows.
- Adding detail everywhere instead of designing a clear visual hierarchy.
- Choosing a rendering style that only looks good from one camera distance.
- Developing characters whose appearance or animation becomes disproportionately expensive.
- Making every future game follow one aesthetic even when its setting or gameplay suggests something different.
- Mistaking technical graphical sophistication for artistic quality.

These are considerations, not restrictions.

## 10. What we're ultimately looking for

We want a world that is enjoyable simply to look at and explore.

A world that can be beautiful when viewed from far away, interesting at street level, and expressive when observing individual people.

A world that can feel warm and playful without losing the ability to portray serious or emotional situations.

A world whose visual identity is recognizable because of its **shapes, colors, materials, lighting, characters, and composition**, rather than because it imitates a particular game.

And, ideally, a world whose appearance reflects what has happened inside it.

The visual direction is not decided. It should emerge through exploration, experimentation, comparison, and iteration.

**The aim isn't to find the easiest style to generate, or the most technically impressive one. It's to discover an artistic language that feels distinctive, beautiful, enjoyable, and achievable—and that makes the simulated world worth experiencing.**
## 11. Open questions

To be settled by look tests, not by discussion.

- **Two kinds of view.** A world view (an illustrated map at city scale, a live neighbourhood seen from above) and scene views (conversations, gigs, matches, labs, forests: bounded, walkable, with a staged camera). Does one shading approach work for both, or does each need its own variant of it?
- **Characters.** The most expensive part of the look, and the hardest to generate for thousands of people. How simple can faces and bodies be while still reading as individuals from a distance and in close-up?
- **History as a pillar.** How visibly should places and people carry what happened to them: ghost signs, later extensions, wear that follows an owner's fortunes, posture and clothes that follow a life?
- **Animation.** How far can a small library plus procedural layers (posture, gesture, gaze, distance) carry individuality, and does stepped animation suit the look?
- **References.** For each reference, what we take and what we leave, recorded after each look test.
