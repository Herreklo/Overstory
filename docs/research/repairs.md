# Repairs: who fixed what, how, and how it looks

Research for the damage and repair layer every hard surface shares (`docs/foundations.md`, layer 6; `docs/stone.md`, The history layers), October 2026: who repaired steps, walls, paving, kerbs, quays and fronts in Norwegian and Nordic towns from about 1870 to 1989, with what, and how the repair looked and aged. Oslo is best documented; Stavanger is thin. Claims from search summaries are marked (secondary); claims no source confirmed, and our own arithmetic, (unverified).

## The bottom line

- Repairs come in two modes. **Campaigns**: planned, scoped, done by the works department or a hired mason, every few decades. **Patchwork**: reactive, local and cheap, made with what was at hand, often laid over earlier patches. A thing in 1989 carries both.
- A repair follows its cause, and most causes act on a stretch, not a part: frost heave, a washed-out bed, a bulging wall, a pipe trench. So repairs come in runs. One odd part alone needs a one-part cause: a broken stone, a rusting rail post, an impact.
- Reused stone hides a repair; new stone, another stone or another material shows it. Towns kept and reused their old stone.
- Materials follow the era: lime, then lime and cement (about 1930 to 1965), then cement; concrete from about 1890; asphalt patches from the 1920s. In Oslo after about 1960 asphalt patching replaced the upkeep of stone, until a reaction around 1980.

## Who repaired what

**The municipality.** Oslo's road department laid and kept the carriageways; from 1843 the town took the pavements over from the owners, who had built them to no common standard (Roede). Kerbs were the town's, 30 cm wide from about 1870, a size kept so spares were always in stock (Roede). Budgets swung: after Oslo's 1899 crash, maintenance was cut and almost no paving was laid from 1900 to 1910 (Roede). Edinburgh kept a squad for its setts until the 1980s, then filled with tarmac and lost the skill (secondary).

**The utilities.** Each dug the street on its own schedule. An Oslo caption of 1860 complains that the waterworks, the gasworks and the sewers each broke up the setts just after the paving crew had laid them (Roede); trams and telephones joined them later (Roede). Stavanger got its waterworks in 1865 to 1866, after the fire of 1860; a gasworks ran from 1866 to 1963 (secondary); the town's electricity came on in December 1909 (secondary). Telephone cables were the state telegraph's (Televerket from 1969).

**The harbour.** Quays were the harbour authority's. Stavanger's Strandkaien was a 1930s structure, strengthened in 2005 (secondary). Little else was found.

**Private owners** kept their own steps, yard walls, fronts, yards and private lanes, from pride to neglect by money and by whether they lived there or let; the old harbour streets of the 1930s and 40s were poor (`docs/research/house-changes.md`). Cheap work used cement: a 1939 cabin's stone steps were bedded in coarse cement (secondary, forum). Housing cooperatives got their own law in 1960; the board kept shared stairs and yards, partly by residents' *dugnad* (secondary).

## Materials by era

- **1870 to 1900.** Local stone and lime mortar; Iddefjord granite for kerbs, setts and pavement slabs from about 1870 (Roede). Sett joints filled with sand, bitumen or cement mortar (Roede). Norway's first cement works began in 1888 to 1893 (sources differ; secondary).
- **1900 to 1930.** Lime render usual (1890 to 1930; ifi.no). Concrete pavement slabs from 1909, 10,000 m² a year in Oslo by 1917; tarred gravel pavements from 1908; small setts in arcs from 1910; asphalt on Oslo's carriageways from 1922 (Roede). Concrete pavements scored in squares show in 1949 photos (Halden).
- **1930 to 1965.** Lime and cement render (ifi.no). War and after: rationing and short materials (`house-changes.md`); cement was what was at hand (unverified for street work).
- **1965 to 1989.** Masonry cement normal (ifi.no). Asphalt over setts as heavy traffic rose; narrower and concrete kerbs tried in the 1970s and 80s (Roede). Cement slurry and plastic paint over lime render (an Oslo front, 1987; secondary). From about 1980 stone came back, often badly done (Roede).

## Partial repairs and how they age

- **Asphalt over a trench in setts.** A straight-edged strip, black when new, greying as the binder oxidises and wears off the stone (secondary; years unverified). Patches fail faster than the surface round them; the damage reaches 0.5 to 2 m beyond the cut, and a cut takes 20 to 56 % off a pavement's life (secondary). The edge cracks and the patch settles.
- **Setts relaid in a trench.** With their own stones, the repair is close to invisible; with new stones it shows: worn and new setts look very different (Halden). In Edinburgh a second rock type in a street appears only where a repair used new or recycled setts; streets were laid from one quarry (BGS 2018).
- **A step**: reset, swapped, turned, indented, screeded in the hollow, overlaid in concrete, or recast (`docs/research/stone.md`).
- **A wall.** A bulged section rebuilt, in new or depot stone. Cement pointing: denser than the stone, so the stone beside it decays instead; often wider than the joint and smeared (secondary). Light mortar used in spot repointing breaks a dark-pointed wall's harmony (NPS Brief 2). On roads, old dry-stone walls got a concrete cap about 0.4 m high, a concrete wall cast in front, or a concrete footing under rebuilt dry-stone (Statens vegvesen notes, 2005 to 2016; secondary).
- **Render.** A patch is in its era's render: lime, then lime-cement, then cement. Hard cement patches move less than lime and brick and tear loose; cement slurry and plastic paint trap water, which freezes and breaks the render (secondary).
- **A front.** One side reclad or repainted, the rest left (unverified); in a two-family house each owner restores their own floor (`house-changes.md`).
- **Iron.** Cramps and rail posts rust and split the stone (secondary); mended with an indent or a cement dab (unverified).
- **Concrete** ages by map cracks from early shrinkage, paste eroding to show the aggregate (worse under acid rain), rust stains before spalling, and white efflorescence (NPS Brief 15). It greys but stays smoother and more even than stone.
- **Quays.** Spot pointing in dry-jointed walls, a concrete toe against scour, a fallen stretch rebuilt in concrete (UK examples; secondary).

## Traces of work

- **Trench lines**: straight along the street for mains, short cross-cuts to each house for connections (unverified for layout).
- **Covers**: cast iron, some with the town's arms; Ulefos made street castings from 1952 (secondary). Iron stains the setts round a cover (BGS 2018).
- **Kerbs**: rough narrow kerbs of the 1850s, 30 cm kerbs from about 1870, concrete or narrower ones from the 1970s; old setts kept as a three-row gutter when a street was asphalted (Roede). Dropped kerbs at drives (unverified dates).
- **Fixings**: holes, lead plugs and rust runs where a rail, sign or bracket was (unverified).
- **Reused stone**: old greenstone setts mixed with new granite between pavement slabs (Oslo, about 1900; Roede).

## How repairs spread

**Campaigns follow the cause's reach.** Frost lifts a flight, water washes out a bed, a wall bulges along its weak length. The crew fixes what moved. A step reset in a conservation job lifts the whole flight, sets the stones aside and relays them (a London specification; secondary). Repointing is done where needed, but if a quarter to a half of a wall needs it, the whole wall is done (NPS Brief 2).

**Towns kept their stone.** Edinburgh and Halifax store lifted setts and use them in repairs (secondary); Swedish towns kept stores of setts (STEN 2003); Halden relays its streets with their own stone (Halden); Oslo reused old setts in pavements and held one kerb size for spares (Roede). A depot repair is weathered stone: it matches in wear and colour, but may be another stone or size.

**Whole renewal is rare.** Oslo's small setts were expected to last 15 years and many lasted 90 (`stone.md`); a Halden street laid in the 1930s was never relaid (Halden); Ålesund's Aksla steps, built 1885 to 1900, were mostly recast only in 2014 to 2015 (`stone.md`).

**A reset step** is the same stone with its own hollow and lichen, but with fresh joints, a corrected tilt, moss gone from the joints, lichen torn where hands lifted it, and perhaps turned so a sharper back edge became the nosing (unverified).

**What blends a repair in, over 20 to 40 years.** Polish comes in two years (`stone.md`). Film and algae return within a year, faster in shade (secondary). Lichen restarts: at 0.7 mm a year after a delay, a stone set in 1952 carries patches of about 2 cm in 1989 beside 7 to 8 cm on its neighbours of 1874 (unverified). New mortar differs at first and is best left to age; stained mortar drifts apart in a few seasons (NPS Brief 2). The hollow never catches up. So after 20 years a new stone of the same kind differs in form and growth, not in pigment; another stone, or concrete, differs for good (unverified, our reading).

Rules for the generator (starting values for look tests, unverified):
- Scope is the cause's reach: a flight's reset takes landing to landing, or the lower run where water collects; a wall's rebuild takes the bulged length, 2 to 6 m; a sett relay takes a block's width between kerbs.
- A reset reuses the part's own stones (more than 90 %); broken ones come from the depot (same kind, weathered), and only failing that new stone, or concrete in 1940 to 1965.
- Interval for a tended public part: a campaign every 30 to 60 years; full renewal once in 80 to 150. Untended: none, until it fails.
- Spot work touches under a quarter of a part; past that, the whole part.
- A single new part alone in a run needs a single-part cause in the diary.

## Patchwork: the quick fixes

What decides between a quick fix and a campaign: money and who pays, how prestigious the street is, whether the skill is still there, and whether it is an emergency. In Oslo after about 1960 the setts were left and patched with asphalt (Roede); on one later square, new setts were patched with asphalt within a year (Roede). Private steps were mended by their owners with cement; the town's by a lone workman between campaigns (unverified).

Examples, by what was at hand:
- **Asphalt blobs** in sett streets and over a sunken patch of setts (Oslo, documented; Edinburgh, secondary). From the 1920s, common after 1960.
- **Spot repointing** in a lighter, harder mortar (NPS Brief 2).
- **A cement fillet** where a roof meets a wall or chimney, in place of lead: cracks with frost and movement within months to a few years (secondary, roofers' forums).
- **A concrete cap or footing** on an old dry-stone wall (Statens vegvesen; secondary).
- **A cement dab** on a broken step corner, **a cement smear** in a crack, **a brick** in a stone wall, **a stone or slate wedge** under a rocking step, **a timber shim**, **bucket cold asphalt** in a yard (all unverified; plausible, not documented here).

**How patches build up.** A patch weakens its edges, so the next failure starts there; patches cluster and overlap, asphalt on asphalt, cement on cement (secondary, from utility-cut studies). A patch's age reads from its colour and cracking; the newest is darkest and sharpest (unverified for asphalt timescales).

Rates (starting values for look tests, unverified):

- Quick fixes on steps and walls are undocumented beyond conservation complaints; old photos (Oslo Museum, Stavanger's archives) would show them.
