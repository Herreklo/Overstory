# How the town changed, 1945 to today

Research for the dated plan (`docs/living-fabric.md`, Big change): how whole quarters, streets, public works and the underground changed from 1945 to today, in Stavanger first, with Bergen, Oslo, Ålesund and Kristiansund where they add something. Houses are in `house-changes.md`, repairs in `repairs.md`. The best sources are Nils Jacobsen's history of Stavanger's centre plans (*Stavangeren* 1/2016) and the city's heritage plan (2010). Claims taken from search summaries rather than the source itself are marked (secondary). Claims no source confirmed, and our own readings, are marked (unverified).

## The story in short

In 1946 Stavanger planned to tear down its old centre and rebuild it for cars and commerce. It cleared two of four planned areas, slowly, because the town had little money and owners would not sell. The fight over the cottages west of Vågen (1956 to 1957) turned the tide. Oil money rebuilt the edge of town more than the centre, which a one-page plan of 1974 held to its old streets and heights. After 1989 cars left the squares, underpasses were filled in, stone came back, and tunnels and car parks went under ground. The 2014 oil fall emptied the office parks at the edge, not the centre.

## 1. Plans and who decided

- **The 1946 plan.** A wartime competition allowed most of the centre to go; one winning entry was called "Riv og bygg" (tear down and build). Both winners kept only the cathedral, Kongsgård, the Valberg tower, Petri church and a few large buildings (heritage plan). Torleiv Våland's frame plan of 1946, adopted in 1948, gave new access streets from the west and north, a widened square and a straight new main street, Klubbgata (Jacobsen).
- **Carried out in part.** Of four clearance areas, Klubbgata (1948 to 1960) and Olavskleiva (1952 to 1962) were done. Laugmannsgata and the Havneringen, a four-lane road through the 1800s sea houses, never were. Property was expropriated and houses torn down. The Kleiva plan passed in 1952 by a narrow majority, and the 1957 plan that opened its blocks by one vote (Jacobsen). Progress was slow because the town had little money and owners would not sell (heritage plan).
- **The car, then the hold.** The 1960 traffic plan sorted streets into main, service and pedestrian streets; around 1970 the traffic office's logo was a motorway junction. A 1970 plan to carry growth and cars up onto the plateaus was shelved after the architects objected in 1972. Plan 681 of 1974 was one A4 sheet: keep today's streets and houses, fit new buildings to their neighbours (in practice two storeys and a loft), control colours, light fittings and signs. In 1984 it reached the harbour and the Havneringen was dropped. In 2002 a developer got a tower on Holmen by offering "all or nothing" (Jacobsen).
- **Who.** The council, often by one vote, on the regulation chiefs' plans; the national heritage office, the museum and Einar Hedén resisted. Husbanken and the housing cooperatives built the suburbs, after 1965 on land the merger gave (Berge 2014). Oil companies chose sites, and developers pushed in every boom.
- **Elsewhere.** Bergen dropped plans to raze Marken and most of Nordnes in 1974. Kristiansund, bombed in 1940, was rebuilt to a 1941 plan by about 1960 (both secondary).
- **Scars.** Olavskleiva's slope of separate blocks and stairs; Klubbgata's width; blocks by Løkkeveien shaped like the 1946 plan's; sea houses still standing on the line of a road never built (Jacobsen).

## 2. Clearance and demolition

- **What went.** Small wooden houses and sea houses on the medieval lanes, and old merchants' houses on the squares. The causes were traffic, hygiene and commerce. Those who lived on Straen in the 1930s and 40s saw little to keep: draughty houses, privies in the yards, two families to a house (*Stavangeren* 1/2016).
- **Industry.** Canning fell from 60 to 70 factories in 1920 to three producers in 1982. Backyard chimneys and smokehouses came down for collapse risk, infill or sun. One showpiece factory became a car park; others became pubs, banks and shops, and Mobil restored brick factories on Straen (*Stavangeren* 1/2016).
- **Commerce.** Buildings were joined, or bridged over streets, into shopping centres, and blocks of small houses went for bigger shops, flats and public buildings (heritage plan). Sølvberget merged many plots into one (Jacobsen).
- **What it left.** First, a car park. Oslo cleared Vaterland's small houses for one from 1959 and made a park on a leftover plot in 1993 (secondary). Holmen's burned block was a car park for years before its tower (Jacobsen); Jorenholmen was a temporary one (heritage plan). A fire cleared part of Bergen's Bryggen in 1955; a 13-year dig followed, then a museum on the ground in 1976 (secondary). Bare gables and kerb lines must have followed, but no source describes them (unverified).

## 3. Preservation and restoration

- **When.** The 1956 plan for Gamle Stavanger was Norway's first area conservation plan. The law had no means to protect buildings, so the houses were zoned as "open space" until 1965 (*Stavangeren* 1/2016). It covered 35 houses in 1956 (Jacobsen says 54), 79 in 1965 and 154 in 1975 (secondary). The wooden town as a whole entered planning in the 1970s. Stavanger got a city conservator in 1974 and protected its first post-war terraced estate in 2006 (heritage plan). About 8,000 wooden houses of 1700 to 1950 stand today.
- **Why.** Popular anger at blocks of another scale, the national heritage office, and a lack of money. What survived was what was cheapest to keep (`generating-the-past.md`). Hedén said in 1992 that nobody had taught them how.
- **How it looks.** Restored areas read as white houses (`house-changes.md`) on stone and timber ground: the oldest wooden town allows slate slabs, plank fences and stone walls, and forbids asphalt and cast surfaces (heritage plan). Neglected areas, by our reading, show asphalt, concrete and wire at the ground and mixed windows above (unverified).
- **Elsewhere.** After tenants protested, Oslo's Grünerløkka was mostly rehabilitated in the 1970s and 80s rather than torn down, with Husbanken's money (secondary).

## 4. Streets and public space

- **Cars.** Car rationing ended on 1 October 1960, and the national fleet nearly quadrupled by 1970 (secondary). Klubbgata ran four lanes until 1989 (Jacobsen).
- **Pedestrian streets.** The principle is in the 1960 plan. A walking guide calls Kirkegata Norway's first, announced in 1960 (unverified). Øvre Holmegate closed to cars in autumn 2005 (secondary), the cathedral square in 2007 (Lokalhistoriewiki).
- **Squares.** Torget, long the bus terminus, got underpasses in the 1960s and had its market trade restricted in the 1980s. A 1999 competition to bring back the square as it was before the 1960s was finished in 2008 (Jacobsen; Lokalhistoriewiki).
- **Surfaces.** Asphalt over setts after 1960, stone again from about 1980 (`repairs.md`). A 2025 draft guide covers relaying the centre's stone and reusing old setts (secondary).
- **Light and heat.** Mercury lamps earlier, high-pressure sodium from the 1980s, LED from about 2010. The first snow-melting pavement was laid in Västerås in 1969; Tromsø heated a pedestrian street in the 2010s (all secondary). Stavanger: no date found.
- **Universal design.** The law came into force on 1 January 2009. In streets it brings handrails on both sides of outdoor stairs, contrasting step edges, tactile guide lines, and ramps no steeper than 1:20 with a landing every 0.6 m of rise (municipal guides; both secondary).
- **New layers.** Blue lights along the quays by 2008; Nuart's murals from 2001 (secondary).

A large building takes two to three years on site and five to twenty from decision. A clearance runs a decade, plot by plot, and a site often waits years as a car park first. On site: hoarding, excavation, frame, scaffold; tower cranes from about 1950 (Liebherr, 1949; secondary; Norway and posters on hoardings unverified).

## 6. The underground and the vertical town

- **Utilities.** Water from 1866, gas from 1866 to 1963, electricity from 1909 (`repairs.md`). Sewage goes to a treatment plant in rock halls at Mekjarvik; a 1999 permit required every building to connect (secondary). A sewer main runs in a tunnel by the town hall (TU 2017). District heating came late, with licences in 2015 and 2023 and pipes into the centre due by 2026 (secondary). At street level: trench strips and covers by era.
- **Shelters.** The duty came by regulation in 1948 and by law in 1953; Stavanger counts its shelters as Cold War memories, 1945 to 1990 (heritage plan). Buildings over 1,000 m² must have one; municipalities build public ones, in rock where possible. Norway had about 20,000, 600 of them public (SNL, 2020). Building stopped in 1998 and is now required again (secondary). In peace a shelter may serve anything that can be cleared in 72 hours: Odda's sports hall in rock (1972) and a dozen more by 1994, and car parks as the simpler kind (secondary). At street level: a steel door at the foot of a stair, vents, a sign (unverified).
- **Car parks.** The 1960 plan placed car parks above and below ground at the cathedral square, the post office and Klubbgata, where Arkaden now stands. Jorenholmen has a multi-storey one (year unknown). Car parks in rock came late and big: a Bergen hospital's (2014 to 2015) has two 85 m caverns for 345 cars and a 100 m walking tunnel; Ålesund's Aksla (2014 to 2016) holds 940 cars on six floors under the town park, with tunnels in and out and lifts up (both secondary). At street level: portals and ramps in a hillside, lift and stair heads in a park.
- **Underpasses and passages.** Torget's underpasses came with the 1960s plan and were filled in after 1989; the year is unknown (Jacobsen). Oslo opened a metro station under Jernbanetorget in 1966, a rail tunnel under the centre in 1980 and the Vaterland passage in 1988 (secondary). No underground links were found in Stavanger; its shopping centres bridge the street instead.
- **Road tunnels.** Byhaugtunnelen (1987, 638 m, lengthened in 2022) and Bergelandstunnelen (1989, about 700 m) carried the new road in. The Eiganes tunnel (3.7 km) and the Hundvåg tunnel under the fjord (5.7 km) opened on 22 April 2020 (secondary). Of the war, only bunkers too hard to remove stayed (heritage plan).

## 7. The economy's mark

- **Canning.** About 60 factories in the 1930s, 38 in 1960, 13 in 1968, three producers in 1982. By about 1960 Stavanger was a "poor" town with low incomes (heritage plan). It left empty halls, felled chimneys, reuse and a car park.
- **Oil.** The first supply base was inside the town at Strømsteinen (1965); others followed at Dusavik and Tananger, and helicopters took the old airfield's hangars at Forus (Berge 2014). Houses for American drillers went up at Slåtthaug in three months in 1966, and there were 70 oil firms by 1974 (heritage plan). The population grew from 78,000 in 1965 to 132,000 in 2015, and housing became among Norway's dearest (industriminne). On the street: hotels, offices, a new skyline, canning halls with oil companies inside.
- **1986.** The oil price more than halved. Norwegian house prices fell about 30 to 40% from 1987 to 1993, and banks failed from 1987 to 1992 (secondary). In Stavanger an arts festival died (industriminne), yet the platform yard at Hinnavågen grew until 1988 (*Stavangeren* 3/2014). No source describes empty shops or stopped projects (unverified).
- **2014.** Rogaland's jobless nearly doubled in a year. In 2017 office vacancy stood at 14.8% at Forus but 5% in the centre, where rents held (secondary). This bust shows at the edge: half-let glass offices and to-let signs.
