# Stone: how the best build it, and what it does over the years

Research for the stone system (`docs/stone.md`), October 2026: what shipped games and painters do with stone, and the facts of Norwegian steps, walls, paving and quays that the generator reads. Claims from summaries or forums are marked (secondary); claims not confirmed, or our own arithmetic, are marked (unverified).

## The bottom line

- History is drawn from causes, never from texture. A tread's hollow is footfall times years over the stone's hardness (Archard's law); lichen's size is its growth rate times the years since the stone was bare or cleaned; a relaid step is a dated event. Then every mark is a fact, as the trees' bark marks are.
- Minimum information, big shapes first (The Witness: "minimize the amount of noise"; Oga: get the essential point across). Each stone has a real chamfer of its own size near, and its lit line in the shading far off; one value a face, with at most one accent; and detail only where the story is: the lit edge, the worn middle, the damp foot.
- A few stone kinds and a few kinds of piece, laid by real rules: steps rest on the step below, walls bridge every joint and lean back, setts run in arcs. Every piece its own seeded instance, so nothing repeats.
- One stack of history layers, the same for every hard surface (stone, later brick, render, concrete, asphalt): wear and polish, stains, growth, damage, repair, wet. Masks from geometry and facts: occlusion and shade for moss, gravity flow for stains, a footfall map for polish.
- Value painted into the pigment, real relief kept, shade from the light (the town's one shade rule). No scanned textures with light baked in.
- Stone is laid as masons lay it and built as studios build big runs (How stone is built and laid, below): every bed level, only footings and copings follow a slope, a head where a flight rises from a rest; each stone a rigid block cut by planes, its surface from a few shared sculpted faces picked by seed, its chamfers at least 2 px at the nearest view, its joints filled.
- Wet is a state: granite darkens and glosses, water stands in the worn hollows (they are the puddles), and the gloss dries before the dark.

## How games build stone

- **Firewatch.** Rocks sculpted in ZBrush, normals baked, textures made in Photoshop; Olly Moss painted over textures for a more painterly pass, and over screenshots of the blockout; lighting matched to his paintovers with atmospheric volumes. Art judged in the game under different lights: "A beautiful looking flat texture might not look great in game" (Campo Santo blog). Jane Ng's talk confirms it (*The Art of Firewatch*, GDC 2015, captions): "flat shapes with strong distinctive silhouette with abstracted internal details"; rocks almost flat grey, with baked occlusion and drybrushed edges; fog "great at cutting down texture detail"; a few sculpted rock modules reused, "if you only need three rocks don't make a fourth".
- **The Witness.** "Minimize the amount of noise", "simplified reality": the minimum that tells you what a thing is; chipping a cube's edges is enough to read as stone (Antonio, *The Art of The Witness*, GDC 2014, slides 7 and 27 to 36). Slides 29 to 31 take one wall from photograph to final. The earlier wall's joints, 9 to 22 L* darker than the stone, and its light chipped rims round every stone went; the final wall keeps each stone one value and a joint a plain step in value (L* measured on the slides; unverified; `reviews/lt1/port/2b/research-studios-stylised.md`).
- **Townscaper.** The design is in the transitions: what happens where shapes change (a corner, a wall meeting a roof); garden walls appear at corners and where colours change. Ornate detail looks cluttered from a raised view.
- **Sable, Tunic.** Flat shading loses depth; it is won back with light, fog and placement that carries perspective; extra detail density looked wrong (secondary).
- **Naughty Dog.** Layered materials blended by vertex colour (cracks, moss, leaks, wet); height blending, so the second layer settles into the first's cracks; an occlusion blend as free contextual data (secondary).
- **Skyrim's kits.** Players spot repeated detail before repeated architecture; footprints in multiples of each other; numbered variants; glue pieces for transitions.
- **Trim sheets** (Sunset Overdrive): long dressed pieces (kerbs, copings) from strips with bevels in the normals.
- **Disco Elysium.** Hand-painted environment textures, each with its own height, normal and shadow maps (secondary); anything that changes with time drawn in real time, never painted in.
- **Raised cameras.** Riot calmed Summoner's Rift by clamping value ranges and controlling detail (secondary); Diablo IV built its detail around the isometric camera and puddles form in ruts and hoof prints.

## How painters paint it

- **Kazuo Oga** lays a ground layer, then works from large shapes to small; detail in small things painted on top; he studies places on site because photographs lose the atmosphere and the scale.
- **Simon Stålenhag** starts from his own photographs, sketches over them and repaints large, layer by layer, with brushstrokes, no photo texture (secondary).
- **Kawase Hasui**: wet shrine stone in the rain as reflective planes of the sky.
- **Hammershøi**'s greys are not black and white mixed: cobalt blue and other pigments are in them; a final thin grey layer softens the outlines. Grey stone in grey light is a coloured grey.
- **Overcast light** (James Gurney): less contrast, bigger shapes, purer colour, greyer shadows (secondary). Rocks grouped into masses, shadows blocked in first (secondary).
- Our reading, now from primary sources (The Witness's final wall, Firewatch's rocks): each stone face one value, with at most one accent; detail on the lit edge, the worn middle and the dark foot where moss grows; joints as broken accents, never a grid.

## What stone does over the years

**The stone.** Iddefjord granite (Østfold) was quarried from the 1840s, peaking about 1913: light grey, medium-grained, sometimes faintly red, splitting in two directions at right angles; used for kerbs, setts, quay stone and building, and every big Norwegian city got "a generous dose" of it. Bergen used local gneiss and granite; Ålesund's rebuilding after 1904 largely local stone. Stavanger stands on phyllite and mica schist, fissile and poor for steps, so its step stone was likely brought in (unverified); Lysefjorden has light granites and granite gneiss (secondary). Larvikite, blue or green shimmering, went in its lower grades into walls and kerbs. Iron in biotite or pyrite rusts to yellow-brown spots; salt and wet-dry cycles lose grains; in damp climates surfaces stay humid, are colonised and blacken (Gloeocapsa's black stains); greening starts as a shift towards yellow-green on shaded wall areas kept wet by run-off (secondary).

**Steps.** Block steps: each step's front rests on the back of the one below, sometimes rebated; nosings rounded, chamfered or square; treads laid with a slight fall to shed water; over a void, carried on an arch; iron handrails set in holes with poured lead, and when the lead loosens the iron rusts, frost jacks the hole, the stone cracks and stains rust (Historic Scotland). Outdoor rise 12 to 16 cm, tread 30 to 40 cm (unverified). Ålesund's 418 steps up Aksla were begun in 1885 by a town society and built over about 15 years, natural stone set into the terrain, partly slabs; most recast in concrete in 2014 to 2015, some original groups kept. Repairs, each visible: re-dressing; swapping unworn side steps into the centre or turning a step over (some nosings sharper than the rest); a stone indent in a cut pocket; a cement screed in the hollow (common, ugly, speeds decay); mortar infill left distinguishable; concrete overtreads.

**Paving and kerbs** (Oslo's record; for our town unverified). Rounded field stone with a central gutter before the 1800s; hewn stone from the 1840s; prismatic setts (about 20 by 13 cm) from about 1850; granite from Iddefjord once local stone ran out, about 1870. Kerbs 5 inches wide in 1853, 8 in 1870, 12 (30 cm) from about 1880. Granite slab pavements 30 to 42 cm wide from 1865; concrete slabs from 1909. Small setts, 10 cm cubes laid in arcs (bueform, invented 1885), in real use from 1910 and the main town surface from 1910 to 1950: arcs 1.2 to 1.6 m wide, larger stones at the crown, smaller at the joints between arcs, half-arcs meeting the kerb square; expected to last 15 years, many lasted 90. Asphalt from 1922; in 1947 half of Oslo's carriageways were still setts.

**Wear.** Granite resists abrasion five to ten times better than limestone or slate (Kessler 1933, stair treads in use; unverified as a ratio); wear is uneven on mottled stone, and damp lowers resistance. Polish comes fast: granite entrance steps lost 46% of their roughness in two years and about a fifth of their slip resistance in a few. Depth comes slowly: sixteenth-century college steps (stone not stated) worn 75 mm in the centre in 500 years, about 15 mm a century; for granite perhaps 1.5 to 3 mm a century at about 100,000 footfalls a year (unverified). Wide steps wear only in the middle; the hollows hold water and freeze. Wear is read by archaeologists for how often stairs were used, which way, in pairs or single file, and what was repaired (Leiden's Testimony of Stones).

**Growth.** Map lichen (Rhizocarpon) grows about 0.75 mm a year in maritime west Norway and 0.55 in the east (Jotunheimen; secondary), slower under 1 cm across, faster on north-west faces; a step laid in 1850 would carry patches 7 to 10 cm across by 1989 on edges no one treads, one of 1920 about 3 to 5 cm, one of 1960 under 1.5 cm (unverified). Xanthoria grows about 2.6 mm a year, 6 to 7 where moist, orange in sun and almost green in shade, where birds perch. Lecanora muralis makes pale yellow-green rosettes of 1.5 to 3.5 cm on pavements and walls and tolerates town air; Grimmia pulvinata cushions of 1 to 4 cm on wall tops; Tortula muralis is the commonest moss on mortared walls (secondary). On a quay wall: a black band of Verrucaria at the top of the tide, yellow and grey lichens above (secondary); Stavanger's tide range is only about 46 cm (secondary). Trampling kills cover fast (no plot kept half its cover after 200 passes; secondary): nothing grows on the walking line, while moss, grass and shrubs root in the joints and along the side faces.

**Walls.** Dry-stone: face battered about 3:1 (Norwegian roads authority; general practice 1:6 to 1:10), stones bedded tilting back and square to the face, every joint bridged, the largest stones lowest, through-stones, flat slab copings. An undrained wall bulges, its lower part vertical or leaning out; Bergen's municipal walls slide at the top, shift and slip out, and are rebuilt in new stone. Norway made its own cement from about 1890, so mortared and concrete walls are plausible from then (unverified); wall-rue ferns grow only in lime-rich crevices, so they mark lime mortar.

## Why stone in games looks cheap

- Tiling: fixed by a random offset per tile, Voronoi blending or a hashed choice of variants (Íñigo Quílez); stochastic and hex tiling keep contrast that plain blending washes out.
- Every edge chipped the same: wear that does not follow contact and use reads as a filter.
- Bevels only in the normal map: the silhouette stays sharp; a real chamfer with weighted normals shades better.
- Grout too regular: real walls bridge every joint, put big stones low, lean back and carry through-stones.
- Uniform noise: repeated detail is spotted before repeated form.

## Distance and performance

Unity's GPU Resident Drawer instances shared meshes (Forward+ and compute shaders), best when many objects share a mesh: it saves CPU and "slightly increases GPU workload" (Unity). So identical repeats (setts, a library kerb) are instanced; stones cut to fit are each their own shape and merge into chunks (How stone is built and laid, below). Decals with rendering layers add a depth-normal prepass, and Unity recommends screen-space decals on tile GPUs. Far off, walls and flights merge into proxies (Kena merged distant meshes; Naughty Dog's Building Proxy; Unreal's HLOD, a merged mesh under a baked material); octahedral impostors cost memory per unique object.

## How stone is built and laid: the craft (22 October)

Studied before the gully's stone was redrawn (the port's step 2b; `docs/proposals.md` Q14, Q15), because the first build had been made before its craft was known: stones sheared along the rake so courses bent, pillow-shaped wall stones, black joint slots, one-pixel gloss lines, 962,000 triangles for one flight and its walls. Four studies, online only; their notes with every source are in `reviews/lt1/port/2b/` (`research-masonry.md`, `research-production.md`, `research-restraint.md`, `research-procedural.md`). No public studio talk shows a rigid, cut-to-fit masonry generator, or one that lays several stones a tread; the rules below come from masons, engineers and studios' parts of the problem.

### In short

1. **Every bed is level; no course follows the pitch.** "Lay the stones on the true horizontal, whatever the slope" (BTCV). On a slope each course starts on a footing stone uphill and ends under the coping downhill. Through a change of pitch the courses run on unchanged and only their ends move. Footings step once the ground rises more than about 1 in 20; a stair is about 1 in 2.
2. **A stop at every upward break.** Where a flight rises from a rest, a dry wall gets a head and a mortared one a kneeler: a big stone with a level bed that takes the foot of the raked coping and resists its push; the coping carries across the break (BTCV; for a ramped wall's coping, Rivington Part I, 1904, p. 54, and Mitchell 1902, which also set kneelers at intervals along a long rake; `reviews/lt1/port/2b/research-local.md`). Locally, no dry wall beside a lane flight has a raked coping: it has none, the bank running over, or a level slab coping that drops square at each rest; raked copings, with a level-bedded block at the foot, appear only on dressed granite (`docs/references/stone/`, 29, 32, 33, 36 to 40 against 42). So the gully's dry walls step and need no kneeler, and the unsourced knee stone is dropped. Copings step or rake, never on a sloping bed: slab copes are set level and stepped; a raked coping sits on level-bedded stones, stopped at its foot, its joints rebated, joggled, dowelled or cramped.
3. **Steps bear front on back and are built into the wall.** Each step laps the back of the one below by 2 to 7 cm, rebated; its ends go about 15 cm into the wall when they are built together (McKay 1944; EB 1911). At most two stones across a step, never pieced in depth, no running joint up the flight. A rest is two to four large joggle-jointed slabs, or setts inside a kerb.
4. **Bond is a number.** No vertical joint through more than two courses; never four stones meeting at a cross; a minimum lap over the joint below; no stone longer than four to five times its height (DIN 1053-1; Derouet-Jourdan et al. 2019). Measured by the engineers' line of minimum trace: the shortest top-to-bottom path through the joints, longer being better bonded (Borri et al. 2015). Heads take the biggest, most regular stones, ties and runners alternating.
5. **A stone is a rigid block cut by planes.** Split stone breaks along planes, so a few planar faces are true to the material, cheap, and keep a convex block convex (its collision hull stays valid; Embark). Flat beds and square arrises (a rock-faced bed is dressed "full and square" 50 mm behind the face); small chamfers only on exposed arrises; irregularity as a few planar chips, never noise on the edges. Every fitting tool that keeps stones rigid (dry-stone robots, engineering packers) chooses the stone that fits from a set and never deforms one; the game tools that fit by stretching (Unreal PCG's `scalable`, Townscaper) or by rounding the wall to whole stones (Burger) break our rule or our facts.
6. **Detail on a light block from shared sculpted faces.** Unique bakes per stone need virtual texturing, which URP lacks: three maps at 1 mm a texel for one flight and its walls come to about 240 MB (unverified). The studios' answer for big runs is a light chamfered block with tiling or patch textures sculpted once (The Order: 1886's library of sculpted tiling materials; Star Citizen's "med poly"; Gears of War 4's layered materials cooked in-engine): each stone picks a patch, offset and turn by its seed. A texture array rather than an atlas, so mips do not bleed between patches. Library geometry only where a fact puts it (a spall, an indent). Primary sources now confirm the way. Bluepoint kitbashes masonry from one sculpted block kit, so that every column looks "mined from the same quarry" (House, GDC 2019). The Order is 90 to 95% tiling, with up to four vertex-colour layers (Phail-Liff and Vitale, SIGGRAPH 2015). Naughty Dog's rocks share three tiling textures, capped at 1024², with one unique macronormal each (Benainous and Sobota, 2023). Star Citizen's is still unread (`research-studios-aaa.md`, `research-studios-stone-worlds.md`).
7. **The M4 pays for small and thin triangles.** Apple: "Render fewer very small triangles, especially if you're applying MSAA as well" (we run 4×); Arm: long thin triangles span several tiling bins. A 6 mm chamfer is about 2 px wide at 5 m and under 1 px at 12 m, so chamfer strips leave the mesh past about 10 m and the arris moves into the shading. Shadows come from a street-tier proxy (`ShadowsOnly`), not the near mesh. Ready at Dawn used "a tiered system of beveling techniques by distance" with custom normals; Insomniac put a 45° bevel in the normal map on unbevelled trims (Olsen, *The Ultimate Trim*, GDC 2015). No studio gives a bevel in millimetres, so the 2 px floor is our own.
8. **Budgets others reach.** A wall stone at 20 to 120 triangles with its buried faces deleted; a step at 150 to 400, most of it the tread grid that carries the hollow; about 25 thin wall stones a square metre, so under 1,000 to 3,000 triangles a square metre of face (unverified). Peytavie's rock walls cost 132 a stone. Store-bought rock kits run 300 to 1,300 a rock at LOD0.
9. **The faults have known causes.** One-pixel lines and orange rims are specular aliasing on chamfers too thin to draw (Kaplanyan et al. 2016): chamfers at least 2 px at the nearest view, and Unity's core `GeometricNormalFiltering` (Tokuyoshi and Kaplanyan), which none of our shaders calls; the shadow normal bias (0.5) is a second suspect for the rims. Black slots are deep narrow joints crushed by shadow and SSAO: joints filled no deeper than they are wide (the repointing rule), their value above the cast-shadow floor. Specks are sub-pixel glossy facets that bloom enlarges (a Karis average on the first downsample). Wavy nosings are noise on arrises. Mottled treads are high-contrast mid-frequency masks on the walking line. Studios draw joints in the texture or as generated filler, never as slots (Insomniac, Naughty Dog, Bluepoint), and Bluepoint's screen-space shadows block the specular in cracks.
10. **Restraint has numbers.** One value per stone face and at most one accent where a fact puts it; the walking surface the calmest part (The Witness's "minimize the amount of noise"; Riot "clamping value ranges"; Arkane's Mitton, who "hates stones with big uniform joints"). A mark reads as a shape at one-eighth only if it covers about a quarter of the face's short side; smaller, it is speckle. Every mark is band-limited by its footprint and faded to its mean before it falls under 2 px, smallest first; courses and silhouettes never fade. The first numbers, measured on The Witness's slides (unverified): its final wall holds each stone within 1.5 L*, neighbours 1 to 6 L* apart, and the lit wall within about 4 L*; a joint is a value step, or a hairline 1 to 1.5 L* lighter, never a dark line. Sucker Punch built a tool that shows noisy areas in red; no one publishes a threshold.
11. **Detail at the corners of the mass, at medium frequency, edges drawn by light.** Chips go on a wall's or a flight's corners, not round every stone (Antonio). Detail "can't be everywhere in equal quantity", and medium-size detail carries a surface while the big forms come from modelling (Arkane). High frequency is omitted where possible, and edges are drawn with rim light, never dark outlines (Valve, NPAR 2007; Firewatch's drybrushed edges). Distance is quieted by fog and proxies; no studio publishes a fade or LOD distance for stone.

### Laying: the mason's rules for a stair and its walls

- **Courses.** Level beds in every wall, dry or mortared, since sloping beds slide either way. Coursed and squared work shows the level courses plainly; random rubble hides them but keeps level beds. At a change of pitch nothing in the field changes; the footing and the coping change, with a head at the rest-to-flight break (BTCV) and, by the gable's reasoning, a plain joint at the flight-to-rest break where nothing pushes (unverified for stairs).
- **Copings.** Upright, tilted, flat slab or shaped (BTCV). On slopes tilted copes hold each other down; slab copes step, one course a step, with an extra course added where the steps grow too big. A stepped coping one course high tracks a stair only every few goings (a 30 cm course over two 15 cm risers needs 70 cm of run; unverified). At a rest the coping runs level; at the knee a joint falls on the break or one stone carries both tops (unverified).
- **Steps.** Block, tread-and-riser, or spandrel; a lane takes block steps. Nosings slightly rounded or chamfered; a fall of about 10 mm forward. Old steps bedded in mortar; joints about 5 mm in modern work. An 1884 German handbook seats step ends on stone cheeks 15 to 20 cm wide and two to two and a half risers high (secondary).
- **Stones and bonding.** Stones on their natural bed; rubble levelled at least every 1.5 m; headers about two-thirds through, in every course; throughs about every 90 cm in dry double walls (secondary). Norway's road standard V220: bond along the wall and across it, no continuous vertical joints (secondary).
- **Our town, 1850 to 1960** (unverified). Dry walls of split local rock, roughly squared and coursed, largest low, wedged from behind; mortared or concrete-backed from about 1890 (`repairs.md`). Copings of large split slabs or long blocks. Granite steps came from Fevik's hewers from the 1880s, reported in a Stavanger paper in 1889: few large facets, split sides and bed, a pointed or axed top and front, a slightly rounded nosing, plug-and-feather holes on hidden arrises.

Wear and history by masks, a pixel each on a tile GPU: a vertex channel per layer, one interpolant, needing vertices where the mask changes (Aldskogius adds quads only so vertex paint interpolates evenly); a height blend, free if height rides in alpha; a baked chunk map, one fetch; a URP screen-space decal, one to five depth reads; a DBuffer decal, a depth-normal prepass; parallax occlusion, 8 to 32 fetches and possibly a depth write; triplanar, three times every fetch. So vertex fields and height blends; no parallax, no triplanar (our stones carry metric UVs). A dated event (a screed, an indent) is geometry or a mesh decal. A lead technical artist would add: per-vertex history buys density only where a field varies (the treads, the damp foot), never on a riser's back or a buried bed; custom normals are fragile through the pipeline, so import them and never recalculate; unique meshes never instance, so count bytes per kilometre and stream by chunk.

### Generating: how others lay stones by rule

- **Features before fill** (Legakis et al. 2001): ends, heads, the rake, a rebuilt panel's edges and the coping are placed first, then the courses filled between, an occupancy map recording what is taken. Every stone is settled on the unrolled elevation, in integer millimetres, before any mesh exists.
- **Fill by choosing, never stretching.** Draw lengths within range, redraw the last few, and close with one cut stone above a minimum length; refuse a fit loudly rather than squash it. Score a few dozen candidates a slot (forms and flips) by least cut and best bed, as the robots do.
- **Bond as a test** (Derouet-Jourdan et al.): crosses and long straight joints are named faults with a deterministic cure; cap the longest straight joint and measure the line of minimum trace on every sheet.
- **Per-stone ids drive variety in the shader** (Sangers, Substance Designer): tilt, tint and wear from the stone's own id, so a small set of judged forms never repeats.
- **Cheap by what is seen.** Chips to about 10 m; plain stones merged per course to about 25 m; course strips with joints in texture beyond; a slab baked from L0 far off.
- **Pitfalls named by practitioners:** a fixed half-stone offset (running bond); crosses and long straight joints; alignment on long runs; shapes alike in no real order; holes where stones are big for their space; uniform joints; stones "cut and glued" on with no mass; stretched modules; every edge chipped; floating or sunk stones.

### Checks that can be automated

On frames as played (1440p, 4× MSAA, never supersampled), with a stone mask and a mask of the clay around it; thresholds are proposals until a sheet fixes them (unverified).
1. **Busyness at one-eighth:** edge density and high-frequency share of L*, stone against clay (Rosenholtz et al. 2007; Machado et al. 2015, whose edge density best predicted rated complexity).
2. **Value histogram:** lit and shaded stone's 5th to 95th percentiles do not overlap; two or three modes a stone.
3. **Joint darkness:** from a joint id pass, the joints' 5th percentile above the darkest cast shadow on open ground.
4. **Thin lines and specks:** a white top-hat keeps bright features under 3 px; long thin pieces are one-pixel lines; a black top-hat finds slots (Frangi's line filter).
5. **Shimmer:** eight frames on sub-pixel offsets, ꟻLIP between neighbours, stone against clay.
6. **Feature size per view:** each chamfer and joint's width in pixels at each view's nearest distance, flagged under 2 px while still at full contrast.
7. **Contact and hygiene** (Blender): overlapping pairs by BVH; rays from each bed to the stone below (a gap over 3 mm on 30% of a bed is loose); arris deviation; T-junctions; coplanar overlaps.
