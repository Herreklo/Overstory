# Fun

Research, 21 October 2026, on Opus 5.5; Fable reviews it after the usage reset. Øystein: "maybe also do a deep research on fun in gaming". This page asks what the best design thinkers and the evidence say makes games fun. Then it asks what that means for games built on a town of simulated lives that records its history. It serves the standing rule in `CLAUDE.md`: we are making a game, not a walking simulator. Its siblings are `docs/ideas/first-game-options.md` (the six options), `docs/research/playtesting.md` (how we test) and the paper playtests in `docs/playtests/paper-1/`.

Claims read in the author's own words (a paper, an essay, a talk's slides, an interview) are unmarked. Claims from reviews, summaries or abstracts are marked (secondary); claims we could not confirm are marked (unverified). Most books here were read through reviews and their authors' essays, not whole; Gaps lists which. Two helpers gathered the sources, and no page was fetched twice.

## The bottom line

- **Fun is mostly getting better at something that matters to you.** Koster, Cook, Loewenstein and Deterding and colleagues arrive there by different roads. Other pleasures exist (Lazzaro's four keys, MDA's eight aesthetics), but improving is what holds people for a hundred hours.
- **Our world's natural fun is curiosity, and curiosity alone makes a walking simulator.** Lazzaro's easy fun comes from ambiguity, incompleteness and detail, which describes world 9989 well. The standing rule asks for hard fun beside it: a reading that is right or wrong, and pays or costs.
- **The fun nobody else can offer is being right about real lives.** Every reading of a person or a house can be scored against a record nobody wrote. The engine's own readers mark the band a player's skill can climb: 58 per cent right for the base rate, 79 for a reader who sees everything.
- **The biggest risk is arithmetic.** The whole town makes about one notable, knowable event a week, and in 46 of 120 months nothing big happens. Each concept must own an inner loop: the edition, the case, the match, the works. The engine cuts into it as RimWorld's storyteller does, and jumps reach the years where lives move.
- **Consequences must be seen as well as kept.** The record keeps everything; the player sees little of it, years late and spread over many people. Returns to a place, people who remember aloud, and records make consequences visible.
- **Competence must be confirmed soon enough to feel.** Readings get verdicts, rationed so guessing doesn't pay. Obra Dinn checks in threes, Golden Idol says "two or fewer wrong", and a wager is settled by the jump.
- **Failure must be fair and the player's own.** In Juul's experiment, players who failed and then won rated a game highest, and players who blamed the game rated it lowest.
- **Players learn the shape before the content.** The engine writes about 900 sentence shapes, while its chains never repeat. We never show an engine sentence, we sift chains instead of types, and we vary the form of a case.
- **No compulsion loops.** No variable-ratio rewards, appointments, streaks or grind. Jumps and the long save respect the player's time.
- **Measure what players do, then ask.** The Game Experience Questionnaire and lone fun ratings are unreliable. Behaviour comes first; PXI subscales only compare versions. A fourth playtest cell, the world without the game, would test the walking-simulator risk directly.

## 1. What fun is

### Fun as learning

Raph Koster's *A Theory of Fun* (2004; revised 2013) defines fun as the brain's pleasure in learning patterns. A game bores when its pattern is mastered, or when it can't be grasped and reads as noise (secondary). Critics found the argument thin (secondary), and Koster himself notes that players grind longer than a pure learning account predicts (his blog, 2022). A simulated town renews its patterns for decades, which a written town can't. But 2,635 events in ten years can as easily read as noise.

Daniel Cook gives the mechanism ("The Chemistry of Game Design", 2007). A skill atom is a loop: the player acts, the game simulates, feedback comes, and the player updates their model. Atoms form chains, and burnout is a skill with no interesting use left. In "Loops and Arcs" (2012) he set loops, which repeat and build skill, against arcs, which play once and are spent. The town's stories are arcs. The reading must be a loop: the same verbs (look, ask, compare records, infer) applied to an endless supply of arcs from the engine. That is our advantage over a story game, while the verbs stay interesting.

George Loewenstein (1994) treats curiosity as a felt gap between what you know and what you want to know. It needs some prior knowledge and is strongest when the gap seems closable (unverified: the paper's scan wouldn't read). Kang and colleagues (2009) tested it with 19 students and 40 trivia questions. Curiosity peaked when confidence sat near even odds, and curious people paid tokens and waited longer for answers. So the player needs a priming dose (a face met, a name on a shopfront) before a gap can open, and evidence should leave confidence in the middle.

Deterding, Andersen, Kiverstein and Miller (2022) give the strongest synthesis we found: enjoyment comes from reducing uncertainty faster than expected. Competence reduces uncertainty about how to reach a goal; curiosity, about what is going on. It explains why players enjoy improving more than winning. It is theory without new data.

### Many kinds of fun

Nicole Lazzaro (2004) drew four keys from about 45 hours of video of 30 adults at play, coded by her firm. Hard fun is challenge, with frustration turning into fiero, the triumph of overcoming. Easy fun is curiosity and wonder, from ambiguity, incompleteness and detail. Serious fun is play that changes how you feel. People fun includes naches, pride in someone you mentored. Best-selling games used at least three. It is an unvalidated white paper, and says itself that statistics were beyond its scope. Two parts fit us closely: easy fun describes a recorded town, and naches is what the club's youth player, the band's new member and the heir could give.

MDA (Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek, 2004) splits a game into mechanics, dynamics and aesthetics, and replaces "fun" with eight aesthetics: sensation, fantasy, narrative, challenge, fellowship, discovery, expression, submission. Walk and Lantz object that narrative has no proper home in it (Lantz secondary). Its use for us is twofold: name the target aesthetics per concept, and heed its Monopoly analysis. A runaway leader kills tension, and the fixes cost realism. A faithful simulation runs away too (a rich club grows richer, an ageing squad dies out), and "it's realistic" is no defence.

Richard Bartle's four player types (1996) came from a forum debate among about thirty players of one MUD, and he called them stereotypes himself. Nick Yee found the parts of each type don't hang together in survey data. Quantic Foundry's model (Yee and Ducheneaut) has twelve motivations in six pairs, from over 1.25 million self-selected respondents (74 per cent male, median age 23). Competition falls most with age, and strategy holds best (secondary). Its labels describe our likely player: "contemplative" strategy, "self-driven" goals (RimWorld is its example) and a taste for a web of human drama. Strategy is also the one motivation that fell markedly between 2015 and 2024 (secondary).

### Competence, autonomy, relatedness

Self-determination theory says games pull people as far as they meet three needs: competence, autonomy and relatedness (Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski, 2006). In their largest sample, 730 online players, need satisfaction explained enjoyment well, autonomy most (β = .49), then competence (.24) and relatedness (.12). The lab studies were short and used many non-gamers, and Rigby's firm sells the PENS questionnaire, as the paper discloses. Tyack and Mekler (2024) found most games research uses the theory as vocabulary and rarely tests it.

For a slow game it says three things. Autonomy is a real choice of what to pursue. Competence must be shown visibly and soon, or the need starves. Relatedness, in a game for one player, must come from people known over years; whether simulated people can meet it is argued, not shown.

### Flow, and why it isn't the target

Csikszentmihalyi's flow is absorbed focus when challenge matches skill, with clear goals and immediate feedback (from the book, not reread). Jenova Chen (2007) argued for letting players choose their challenge inside play, as in flOw. GameFlow (Sweetser and Wyeth, 2005) turned flow into eight criteria, validated only by two expert reviews.

Flow doesn't explain why idle games or Soulslike games are enjoyed (Deterding and colleagues). Juul's players who failed sometimes rated a game higher, and Lazzaro's fiero needs a fall out of balance first. A game of reading and deciding is a poor engine for moment-to-moment flow. Aim it at the longer loop (investigate, decide, see the result), and design rests on purpose. Chen's idea fits: let the player pick a colder case, a more ruined house, a riskier signing.

### Decisions and uncertainty

Sid Meier called a game a series of interesting decisions, and in 2012 said what makes one interesting (secondary). It trades off things the player values, its answer depends on the situation, it expresses the player's style, and its effects last. Ours last by nature. Our failures will be too few decisions an hour, made with too little to go on. Reading the world exists to inform trade-offs: a choice is situational when its answer depends on who these people are.

Greg Costikyan (2013) argues that games need uncertainty, and lists its sources, among them hidden information, analytic complexity, other people, narrative anticipation and the solver's doubt: can I solve this? (secondary, through Liz England's review). The engine gives us the first four for free. It gives no solver's uncertainty: a deduction with a checkable answer must be built on purpose, or reading never becomes a skill.

Salen and Zimmerman's *Rules of Play* (2003) asks that the result of an action be discernable (seen) and integrated (shaping later play) (secondary). In a persistent world integration is easy and discernability is the problem: criterion 3 from the design side. Of Jesse Schell's lenses (secondary), triangularity sets a safe, small reward against a risky, large one: a sound house or a cheap rotten one. Endogenous value says reputation matters only if it changes what the player can do. The interest curve lets a slow game rest without stalling.

### Failure

Jesper Juul's paradox of failure: we avoid failing, yet seek out games where we will fail (*The Art of Failure*, 2013, secondary). In his 2009 experiment, 94 players tried a small custom game. Those who lost some lives and then won rated it highest, above flawless winners, though only at p = .06. Those who blamed themselves rated it above those who blamed the game. The evidence is thin, but every later source agrees in shape: failure must be fair and the player's own. On a decades-long timeline a setback is enormous, so its scope must be chosen. And a failure the town remembers is content.

### Simulation and emergence

Tynan Sylvester's "The Simulation Dream" (2013) is the source closest to us. Complexity that doesn't reach the player's mind is worthless: Ultima Online's ecology was eaten by players before anyone saw it work. Players read intent into simple events (apophenia), so the game co-authors the story with them. Model the least that supports the stories you want. In RimWorld he kept the cast small, made events long and overlapping, and let disasters wound instead of end, so a collapse becomes an arc that can turn (2016 interview).

This pulls against our rules. RimWorld wants players to invent; criterion 4 says they must not be misled. So apophenia must be steered toward true readings, and the player's story treated as a hypothesis the record can test. Section 5 builds a game on that.

### The brain at play

Celia Hodent's *The Gamer's Brain* (2017) puts usability first, because perception, attention and memory are limited (secondary; her talk notes read). Decades of lives overflow working memory, so the game must carry the player's memory: a journal, the paper's archive, accounts with sources. She defines dark patterns as design that exploits cognitive biases for business gain, and asks studios not to punish players for leaving.

## 2. Fun in the genres we are weighing

### Managers

We found no primary account of Football Manager's pull from its makers. The mechanisms can be read from the game. Ability shows as ranges and scouts' judgements that narrow as a player ages and with a better scout (secondary). A click is a day, a match a week, a save decades. Researchers describe players' attachment to "their" club as social identity, and friendships with footballers they never saw play (secondary). Its press conferences are the warning: players have long called them repetitive (secondary), and templated exchanges are the first thing players learn to skip.

Crusader Kings makes the dynasty the player: when a ruler dies you go on as the heir. Crusader Kings III's stress makes personality a cost on decisions (dev diaries, secondary). Both fit a life and its heirs. Ranges that narrow with exposure are the right shape for reading people.

### Story generators

Will Wright built The Sims as a space of possibilities and left the stories to players, keeping Sims abstract so players filled the gaps (2000, 2004). Richard Evans, who wrote The Sims 3's AI, names the limit (Evans and Short, 2014). A shy Sim hosting a party was torn inside, but the animation could only answer the door or not. An inner conflict the screen can't show doesn't exist for the player.

Tarn Adams: give actors motivations and let them go; players fill in the blanks (2008). "Losing is fun" was there to stop players reloading after a disaster, and failed fortresses stay in the world as ruins (2014). In Boatmurdered, forum players each ruled a fortress for a year and wrote it up (secondary): a place handed on with a record per reign. RimWorld's storytellers pace the incidents: Cassandra on a curve of rising tension, Randy at random (Ludeon devlog, 2013). Caves of Qud picks an event first and rationalises its cause, then scatters history as biased fragments the player collects in a dated journal (Grinblat and Bucklew, 2017). Its unreliable accounts fit our perceived information; its invented causes would break our rule that history is fact.

Generated stories land when the player sees the cause and knows a small cast by name. Events last and overlap, something human is at stake, the past leaves a mark, and the player can retell it. They go samey when variety doesn't change play and detail feeds nothing back.

### Mysteries and deduction

Return of the Obra Dinn confirms fates in threes so guessing doesn't pay (secondary). Andrejs Kļaviņš found in testing The Case of the Golden Idol that all-or-nothing checking drove players to guess; the fix says when two or fewer slots are wrong. Scenes hold only needed clues and chosen red herrings, and several paths lead to one answer. The makers couldn't judge their own difficulty, so they needed five to seven rounds of silent observation. Outer Wilds began as Alex Beachum's thesis (2012): exploration answers the player's own questions, so there are no set objectives and no aimlessness. Sam Barlow layered Her Story's clips so any order of discovery satisfies (2016).

The aha has evidence behind it. In Danek and colleagues' magic-trick study (2014), 41 per cent of solutions came with an aha, and happiness was its strongest part. But the aha also comes with wrong answers (Danek and Wiley 2017, secondary). For criterion 4 this matters most: an aha from our screen will be believed and remembered even when it is wrong.

### Role-playing games

Larian approached Baldur's Gate 3 as a dungeon master would, and let nearly anyone be killed (secondary). Disco Elysium lets you fail, even never solve the case, but failed checks open other paths (secondary). Josh Sawyer disliked percentage checks because they invite reloading (secondary). In Pentiment he gave too little time for every lead, had people remember for years whom you accused, and named no true killer (2022). Three devices for felt consequence: a scarce budget, a memory per person, and no take-backs.

### People fun with simulated people

In Versu (Evans and Short, 2014), characters hold several judgements of each other at once, remember the reason ("rude at the dance"), pass them on, believe others' reports and disagree. That is evidence without verdicts, built as a system. Shadow of Mordor's Nemesis enemy remembers you, carries the scar and returns with a shared history (de Plater, secondary). Wildermyth's maker warns that a generated character acting out of character breaks trust (abstract, secondary). Shadows of Doubt went first person because top-down cut players off from its people; reviews fault its repetition once case patterns are learned (secondary).

Simulated people are interesting when they remember visibly and want things that clash with what is expected of them. They judge each other fallibly, act when the player doesn't, and are few enough to know by name.

## 3. The other side

### Boredom, frustration and false choice

Boredom is a pattern used up or never grasped (Koster), burnout a skill with nothing left to do (Cook). Jon Hopson (2001) adds that when rewards stop, frustration is sharp, so reward rates should change gradually.

Fendt and colleagues (2012) gave about 150 readers a branching story, the same story linear but acknowledging each choice, or linear with little feedback. Acknowledgement came close to branching on most measures of agency, yet readers still felt the story wouldn't have changed. That was one reading. A town with a recorded history invites comparison, and an illusion of choice wouldn't survive it. Acknowledge every choice, and let some change what follows.

### Empty abundance

Kate Compton's "10,000 bowls of oatmeal": each bowl differs mathematically, and none differs to the eater (GDC 2017, secondary). Her test is whether anyone would write fan fiction about a generated thing. Emily Short adds that variety which doesn't touch the mechanics is decoration players learn to ignore (2016). No Man's Sky recovered by adding purposes and people, and Starfield repeated whole points of interest (both secondary). Dwarf Fortress is the counter-case: crude to look at, but its generation feeds a permanent record players retell.

The paper playtests' panels found our own oatmeal. World 9989's events fall into about 900 sentence shapes, and the commonest 25 make 18 per cent of all events. Every band show is at the Anchor. Thirty years move how a person chooses by about 3 per cent. Yet the chains never repeat: Sarah Lane loses a place in Pirate Radio to an 85-year-old who dies four months later. The content is fresh and its surface samey.

### Slow consequences and passive play

The panels counted what a player could see. About 54 events a year are both knowable and notable, one a week across the town. In 46 of 120 months nothing of notability 2,000 or more happens anywhere, and 76 per cent of events are never the cause of another. The systems panel's verdict: the engine feeds the outer loop and starves the inner one.

Games make late consequences felt with a handful of devices. Pentiment jumps years with visible change, and its people remember aloud, as Nemesis enemies do. Nemesis and Dwarf Fortress leave marks on bodies and places. Caves of Qud keeps a chronicle, Crusader Kings passes costs to heirs, and Frostpunk and New Vegas sum up a span at its end. Some Frostpunk players felt its closing judgement didn't match their run (secondary): a warning against summaries that moralise.

### Compulsion, and the player's time

Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013) call a pattern dark when it works against the player: grinding, playing by appointment, paying to skip (secondary). Hopson showed that variable-ratio rewards give the highest steady rate of play, which is what drives loot boxes. Zendle and Cairns (2018), with 7,422 players, linked loot-box spending to problem gambling (secondary). We won't use variable-ratio rewards, streaks, daily logins, timers that punish leaving, grind or paying to skip. The fiction's clocks (the inquest, the deadline, the season) are rules of the game, visible and fair.

Serviss (2013) asks that every action serve the core experience and that downtime after a failure stay short. Players accept a permanent failure they chose: about 141,600 beat Baldur's Gate 3's single-save mode against some 1.2 million failed runs (secondary, in `playtesting.md`). Jumps are our main tool: the player skips the months where nothing happens.

## 4. Measuring fun

`playtesting.md` designs our programme. This section adds what the measurement research says about fun itself.

| Instrument | What it measures | Evidence | Use here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Game Experience Questionnaire | seven factors, from immersion to negative affect | A review of 73 papers and a validation with 633 players found no support for its structure (Law, Brühlmann and Mekler 2018, secondary) | No |
| PENS | competence, autonomy, relatedness, presence, intuitive controls | Held across two games with 447 players (Brühlmann and Schmid 2015, secondary); free for academic use, commercial use needs permission | Only if licensed |
| PXI (Vanden Abeele and colleagues 2020) | five functional scales (ease of control, goals and rules, challenge, progress feedback, look and sound) and five psychosocial ones (meaning, curiosity, mastery, autonomy, immersion; list unverified) | Built with 64 experts over five studies; an independent pre-registered check with 1,518 players mostly held it, immersion weakest (secondary) | Yes, to compare versions |
| Intrinsic Motivation Inventory | interest and enjoyment, competence, effort, pressure, choice | Widely used, not made for games | The enjoyment scale as a short check |
| One "fun" or "play again" rating | | Not validated; Valve uses ratings to compare builds over time | Only to compare builds |
| Biometrics | arousal, attention | Show arousal, not whether it's pleasant; physiological peaks often missing from what players say (Tan and colleagues 2014, secondary) | Not at our stage |

Three rules follow. Questionnaires compare versions; none measures fun on its own. Five or six players find problems, while telling two prototypes apart on a questionnaire takes about 64 players per arm for a medium effect, by standard power arithmetic. And what players do outweighs what they say (Valve, 2009): thinking aloud disturbs immersive play, so for long sessions we watch in silence and ask over the replay, as `playtesting.md` decides.

## 5. What this world can offer

| Fun | Here | Kind | Nearest game | Why only here |

| Right when the town is wrong | The player's own eye against an account or the Courier | fiero | Paradise Killer, Papers, Please | Accounts have tellers and can be wrong in the record |

The first row is the core. Every player invents stories; RimWorld relies on it. Here the invention becomes a hypothesis the town settles later. The engine's numbers make that a good game. A reader who sees everything is right 79 per cent of the time, so people never become certain, and Kang's curve says curiosity lives near the middle. The witness reaches 64.6 and the base rate 58, so there is a band a player can climb. The cleanest fair mystery in the reading packs is a trap for a false join. Grace Shaw bought her boat a year after the town learned about the till, and she paid for it with ten years of saving. The player who resists the obvious link wins. Criterion 4 stops being a guardrail and becomes the challenge.

## 7. Levers for each concept

*The club.* Aim at challenge, ownership and pride in players found.
- Own the week, not the match: the secretary's job of getting eleven to the Mudbowl or forfeiting (the experience panel's "fielding eleven"). The match resolves in a minute.
- Scouting as ranges that narrow with evenings spent; readers of the town pick the better side.
- Promises remembered per player, returning seasons later.
- The board's safe target against a promotion push that risks debt.
- Traps: the core plays as well on the page, the squad ages out, and press-conference repetition.

*A death in the town.* Aim at the aha and fiero: hard fun on an easy-fun stage.
- Commit, then confirm in groups (threes, or "two or fewer wrong"); the record shown after scoring.
- Questions as acts others hear of; a clock to the inquest; evidence that decays.
- A case is a sifted chain, never a type; some end in an honest "?".
- Chen's choice inside the fiction: a fresh case or a cold one.
- Traps: a natural death can feel a cheat, and the supply is four deaths in ten years. Pentiment could name no killer because its game had teeth; ours earns fiero by scoring against the record. The obituarist hybrid widens the supply.

*The restorer.* Aim at discovery, competence (dating a window), expression (what to keep) and ownership.
- Inspections rationed; hidden condition as facts; a misreading costs money, and the bill comes years later.
- Owners and tenants with lives and opinions; works that change a street others live in, as when a band loses its loft to flats.
- A restorer's name as endogenous value: it opens doors and owners.
- Traps: a decorating toy, and an economy that belongs to the engine. Øystein found it "a bit boring" alone, so it fits best as a thread inside a stronger game.

*The band.* Aim at people fun, naches and the fiero of a gig that goes right.
- The first command with a cost (the month's rent, a promise); a refusal with its reason reads as a person deciding.
- Gigs that can fail: an empty room, a member who doesn't come. Today they nearly always go well.
- The one not picked returns, in the front row or a rival band.
- Play a member instead of the manager, so the engine's member decisions become verbs (narrative panel).
- Traps: a manager with no verbs, gigs that template, and the music.

*The paper.* Aim at the hard fun of judging sources, the stakes of printing, and a weekly rhythm.
- The edition as the clock; column space as the trade between the safe story and the scoop with one source.
- Sales against trust; corrections as failure the player owns; the archive keeps every mistake.
- The obituarist hybrid for supply: deaths, arrivals and weddings feed one desk.
- Traps: the page wins, there is a lead a week, and a true story can be cruel. Rule 8 holds, and consequences are shown without a verdict.

*A life and its heirs.* Aim at relatedness, ownership, regret and pride across generations.
- A dated goal per act, so Juul's model holds: the mortgage due in March, a parent's care.
- Evenings as the budget, as in Persona; the missed evening as the cost.
- Succession as the act break; the heir inherits the family's accounts and tests them against the town (the narrative panel's family story as a cold case).
- A wager before each jump.
- Traps: the weakest game of the six, people who barely change, and little to master.

## 8. What the playtests should measure

### The marks, read as kinds of fun

The paper round's marks already carry most of what the research asks for (`docs/playtests/paper-1/method.md`). Read them this way:

| Signal | Kind of fun | Read with care |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `curious`, and needs about people and places | easy fun, curiosity | Curiosity about the rules is confusion |
| `engaged`, pull rising after the player's own decision | hard fun, autonomy | Pull that rises only after reveals is story, not play |
| Surprise: a delight | fair surprise | Check it against the record: a false aha counts against criterion 4 |
| Surprise: a cheat | failure the player blames on the game | Juul's lowest-rated case |
| Predictions right above the base rate | competence; reading pays | Only in the lives-on arm does it count for the world |
| The stakes probe names a person | relatedness, ownership | Naming only money or points means the format carries it |
| `bored`, `would quit` | burnout or noise | Ask which: nothing left to learn, or too much to grasp |

Six codes would add to the think-aloud and the replay commentary:
- questions the player asks unprompted ("why did she stop coming?"), Loewenstein's gaps;
- "I knew it" and "I was wrong", the aha and fiero, each checked against the key;
- "I should have" against "that's unfair", Juul's blame;
- "again" or "same as before", sameness noticed;
- goals the player sets themselves, autonomy;
- reversals in the retelling, where up to three made a story compelling in Kreminski and colleagues' study (`playtesting.md`).

### The world's fun, apart from the format's

The lives-on and lives-off arms already measure what the world adds to a game. They can't show whether the game adds anything to the world. A fourth cell would: the same lives, with no goal card, read as an observer would. That is the walking simulator, built on purpose as a control.

| | Game on | Game off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lives on | the concept | the world alone: the walking simulator |
| Lives off | the format alone | (not needed) |

The calibration game, a loved loop at the same text fidelity, sets the format's ceiling. A concept passes when marks fall in both directions: when the lives go off (the world matters) and when the game goes off (the game matters). Curiosity, predictions above the base rate and stakes that name people should fall without the lives. Engagement, pull and deliberation should fall without the game. If engagement holds with the game off, the concept is a walking simulator that happens to read well. Agents can't feel fun, so these are hypotheses for Øystein and his testers.

### For people at the greybox

- Behaviour first: the next case taken, minutes played past the end, a return after a week, the town retold to someone else.
- Prototypes ranked against each other, never rated alone.
- PXI's goals and rules, progress feedback, curiosity, mastery, autonomy and meaning scales after each session, compared between versions.
- The same introduction for every tester.

## What we should do

Ranked by what each buys for its cost.

1. Write each concept's target fun on its card before anything is built. Name two or three of MDA's aesthetics or Lazzaro's keys, the inner loop the game owns, and the fail states the player owns. An hour per card.
2. Make the reading loop the shared core of every concept: commit, then confirm in groups against the record, shown after scoring. The wager and the census in `playtesting.md` are its first forms. It is the fun only this world has.
3. Make consequences seen: the return as a ritual, people who remember aloud, records with makers. Test it at every jump.
4. Design failure in, per concept: elastic, the player's own, kept in the town.
5. Give the sifter (request 7) freshness rules: chains before types, repeats folded, a case's form varied, some cases ending in "?". Run Compton's fan-fiction test across seeds before trusting a generator.
6. Add the fourth cell and the six codes to paper round 2 and the greybox.
7. Use PXI subscales for people, behaviour above all; drop the Game Experience Questionnaire and lone fun ratings.
8. Write the line against dark patterns into the design docs: no variable-ratio rewards, appointments, streaks, grind or paying to skip.
9. Back the panels' engine requests with this research: arcs that turn, a decision held for the player, more people or a lighter tier of lives.

## Gaps

- GDC Vault was down. Koster's "Revisiting Fun" (2024), Meier's "Interesting Decisions", Sylvester's RimWorld talk, the Outer Wilds talks and Fåhraeus on Crusader Kings II are known from abstracts and reports.
- Books read through reviews and their authors' essays, not whole: Koster, Juul's *The Art of Failure*, Costikyan and Schell. The same holds for Salen and Zimmerman, Sylvester's *Designing Games*, Rigby and Ryan's *Glued to Games*, and Hodent.
- Loewenstein's 1994 paper was a scan that wouldn't read; its claims are from memory.
- Quantic Foundry's own posts were blocked; their age and decline findings come through the press.
- No primary account of Football Manager's pull, and no developer reply on Shadows of Doubt's repetition.
- Whether simulated people can meet the need for relatedness has no evidence we found.
- The paper round's analysis isn't written yet, so this page predicts its findings without them.
