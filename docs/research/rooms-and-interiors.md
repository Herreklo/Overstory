# Rooms and interiors

Research for the Interiors row in `docs/systems.md`: Stavanger's rooms from 1870 to 2020, with 1989 in most detail, and how studios build interiors cheaply as places and as evidence. It builds on `fronts-windows-and-signs.md` (the room behind the glass), `scenes-and-sound.md` (Shadows of Doubt, Merrell), `history-in-things.md` (interior traces) and `docs/living-fabric.md` (stuff layers). Written 29 October 2026; nothing here is decided. Unmarked claims are primary, read this session; (secondary) is a summary, wiki, excerpt or talk report; (unverified) is unconfirmed. Oslo's museum flats stand in where Stavanger has none.

## In short

- **A room is layers with dates.** Paper over paper, furniture from a marriage and a move, things from parents. Style dates the making, never the owner, so every thing carries made, acquired (when, how, from whom) and used by whom.
- **1989 has numbers.** A video recorder in 5% of old people living alone and 58% of young families; Agder and Rogaland heated 52% by panel heaters, 27% by a stove. These are defaults and checks; an exception comes from a fact.
- **Old houses held many households.** Three flats and 13 people in one Øvre Strandgate house in 1910, one flat in the cellar.
- **Rooms show little and invite stereotypes.** Openness and tidiness read, warmth does not, and viewers fill gaps from age, sex and race. Few facts make a sparse room. A young couple's restored house reads as an old person's unless it shows new work and new things.
- **The shell is fitted, the contents are kits.** Each person has an inventory that moves house with them. Different facts beat more variants against sameness. A layout is stored once seen.
- **Light from our own field.** Probe volumes leak through thin timber walls, so each room gets a small seeded light grid. Keep the cut at the door; try a cutaway for one chosen house.
- **First test.** 108's stue in grey clay from the couple's facts, beside the same shell furnished from an old widower's; cold readers must say who lives in each.

## The subject

### Homes by type and money

- **The old wooden town house.** Stue, kitchen and *kammers* over two or three small floors, often a shop in the plinth (Stavangeren 1/2016). Øvre Strandgate 64 in 1910: a cellar flat of three; on top a canning foreman's family of four in three rooms and a kitchen, no bath, no electric light (Digitalarkivet). Straen in the 1930s and 40s: two families and lodgers where two adults live now, privies in the yard. In 1946 the town had 800 shared kitchens and 200 cellar flats; from 1956 the council stopped letting its Gamle Stavanger houses to families with children (Bergsgard 2005).
- **The best room.** A merchant's *finstue* held paintings only, the family photographs hung in the everyday stue (Nyrud 2010). An Oslo washerwoman of 1950 kept her dining room for show and slept on a divan (Norsk Folkemuseum). Stavanger's 1950s row houses were new in putting a dining place by the kitchen (Bergsgard 2005).
- **Villas and workers.** Breidablikk (1881 to 1882), an Eiganes villa, came to the museum with its contents in 1972; its ground floor is largely as lived in. The canning workers' house at Øvre Strandgate 90 is furnished to about 1920 and 1960 (DigitaltMuseum).
- **Post-war and oil.** 2.7 people and 4.1 rooms a dwelling around Stavanger in 1980 (NOS B 249). In Agder and Rogaland in 1988, 16% lived in houses built before 1921 and 70% owned (NOS B 892). After 1986 real prices fell about 40% nationally (secondary).

### 1989 in numbers

Shares of households with a TV / video / dishwasher / PC / car, 1989 to 1991 (NOS C 65):
- alone at 65 to 85: 93 / 5 / 7 / 0 / 25;
- alone under 65: 85 / 26 / 16 / 5 / 56;
- a couple with a child under 7: 99 / 58 / 59 / 14 / 69.

In 1988 a bath was missing in 2% of homes in Agder and Rogaland. It was missing in 14% of homes built before 1921 and for 11% of old people living alone (NOS B 892). So a missing bath belongs to an old person in an old house, not to the year.

### What a room holds

Norsk Folkemuseum's Wessels gate 15 is the best dated set. It has flats of 1879, 1905, 1935, 1950, 1965 ("Teak, TV and Teenager"), 1979, a 1982 bedsit and a Pakistani family's 2002 home. Bonytt's archive of 16,000 photographs (1968 to 1989) holds real 1980s rooms. Inherited: a chest, a clock, the Bible, photographs; no survey of a room's ages was found. Workplaces are thin. Grocers shut at 17:00, and at 13:00 on Saturdays (1985; secondary). No source shows the inside of a shop, café, office, the fish market or a workshop in 1989.

### What a room says, and what it falsely suggests

Gosling and colleagues (2002): observers of offices and bedrooms read openness and conscientiousness best (secondary), and sex and race stereotypes carried part of their accuracy. In *Snoop* Gosling misjudged an age by several years (secondary). Rules:
1. Style dates the making, never the owner.
2. Residue only from what people do: "reads late into every night" gives a lamp by one chair, a stack, a worn arm.
3. A thing that implies a person, habit or event is a fact or absent: a toy claims a child, an ashtray a smoker.
4. A shared room shows the household.
5. A pale patch or nail holes only from a removal.
6. A room kept as the dead left it needs a "kept since" fact.
7. Tidiness has causes besides character.
8. A room is never asked to show warmth or honesty.

**108's danger:** its auction and inherited pieces, rag rugs and pelargoniums read as an old person. The truth shows in new paint and joinery, antiques refinished rather than worn in place, a video and hi-fi, and no medicine or chair worn by the window. **107's empty years:** unheated, boarded, mould above about 70% humidity behind furniture and on linoleum's jute, lifting seams (Bygg og Bevar); dead plants, a stopped clock, mail. What the heirs took leaves marks only if their visit is an event.

The look: in Harriet Backer's *Blue Interior* (1883) the colours shift with the light outside (SNL). That is VD2 indoors, the shade taking the window's colour by day and the lamp's at night.

## The craft

| The Sims 4 | rooms move with walls and contents; footprints, deco slots; dirt and breakage decay (secondary) | footprint, slots and state on every part, each state with a cause |
| Gone Home, Tacoma | a density of objects, the least story cut first; doors and taps work or have a reason | an era catalogue; every affordance with a cause |

| Disco Elysium | 193 painted 4K squares with height and normal maps | paint that answers the light |

| Firewatch | two real lookouts photographed; what Henry brings stays (secondary) | rooms gather what people bring |
| TLOU II, RDR2 | clean and dirty versions of one space (secondary) | one shell, dated dressings |

| Spider-Man 2, Dredge | 32 fake rooms with variants (secondary); a dock as a panel (unverified) | a small set; a panel where a room is not play |
| Shadows of Doubt | one new furniture piece rearranges the city (devblogs 36, 37) | a versioned catalogue, layouts stored |

**Kits, facts, sameness.** Walls, heights, openings and depth are the house's plan, fitted by functions as the front is, so the window's room record and the real room agree. The kit sits one level down: families of real era variants (stoves, panelling, doors, furniture), each judged once. Sameness comes from chance picks, class presets and templates. Its cures, strongest first, are different facts, dated layers, variants, then the machine check and the find-the-house test. Layout follows activity (Fu 2017, about 2 s a room; Fisher 2015), solved by a seeded optimiser with fixed order and iterations, with no learned step in the game.

**Light on the M4.** APV is baked in the editor, and its finest default spacing is 1 m (lower is not documented). It allows four layer masks against leaks, and Unity says its fixes cure "often not all" of them. Proposed: a seeded SH grid per room (about 8×8×4), filled on entry from each window's sky and the lamps, judged against an APV bake (cost unmeasured). One real light per room at night, exposure fixed per pose by day, the window a bright painted shape.

**Street to room.** Keep the cut at the door (`play-craft.md`). Try a cutaway for one selected house at the raised views. Its walls are clipped by a cut height and a camera-facing flag, with thick caps. Every other house keeps interior mapping. A Sims-like build mode needs exactly this seam.

**Clutter.** Unpacking has about 29 items a room; a 1989 stue perhaps 150 to 400 with books (estimate). Proposed: at most 150k triangles, 300 renderers in 60 draws or fewer, two or three material families, well inside the eye-level ceilings (`docs/building-blocks.md`). Books are one fitted mesh per shelf, and nothing small casts. Over-detail beside clay reads as noise.

**Years.** A room has four clocks. The shell changes over decades (a stove's flue hole), the surfaces over years (paper over paper, a picture's pale ghost only as a fact). Furniture changes by arrival and departure, small things by the day. Wear follows a use map from habits, and dust gathers where no habit goes.

## Three first tests

In grey clay, cheapest first, each still looked at from the four views before any reader sees it. The interior works each test needs are authored as fixture facts before building.

1. **One wall at three dates.** 107's stue wall and stove in 1960, 1975 and 1989. First paper over paper; then the 1965 window and a panel heater. Then three empty years: light only through the boards' gaps, mould where the sofa meets the outer wall. *Passes if* cold readers order them, name a change between each and call 1989 empty, naming nothing the record lacks.
2. **Light and window agree.** 108's stue with block furniture, by day and at blue hour. Our light field stands beside an APV bake; from the street stop, interior mapping stands beside the real room. *Passes if* nothing leaks, the field reads like the bake, both views show the same back wall and lamp, and `tools/check.py` times it inside budget.
3. **Who lives here.** 108's stue in 1989 from its household's facts alone: a couple of about 30 who restored it in 1985, both working, with named heirlooms and auction pieces. The control is the same shell furnished from an old widower's facts, with no hand-tuning. Each blind reader sees one room and writes who lives there, their age, what happened lately, and what is unsure. *Passes if* 108 reads as young people in a house recently done up, never old, and the control as an old person alone. Nobody may name a person, habit or event the facts lack.

## Whose facts

By FC1: the engine decides that and why, our rules how it looks, the seed which exactly. Already filed: homes (15), goods (17), small acts on things (20), records (9), care (13), works (14), where (28), rites (31), dated intervals (33); taste stays ours (33).

Candidates for the engine:
- **Things that carry a relation** (extends 17): a gift, an heirloom, a friend's photograph, with giver, year and occasion. Only the engine knows who mattered to whom.
- **An estate after a death** (beside 24): who inherits the things, whether they clear the house, what they keep. 107's room is the heirs' decision.
- **Interior works** (extends 14): a kitchen, a bathroom, a room restored, with year, payer and reason. It dates every surface.
- **Households that split and merge** (extends 15): a divorce, a parent moving in, a lodger. Each changes who sleeps where.
- **Habits that mark rooms** (extends #22): smoking above all; nicotine on a ceiling claims years of it.

Ours, by the pack and rules:
- the room plan, and surfaces by fit-out year;
- who sleeps in which room, by rule from the members and their ages, until a story needs it;
- furniture families by era, money and cohort, and the survey's defaults;
- ordinary inventory from money and life events;
- the layout, stored once seen;
- wear, dust, damp and fading, and lit rooms by the hour.

Scene state: tonight's table, the TV on, curtains drawn. Texture: a wallpaper's pattern within its family, ornament order, spine colours, never an object that implies a person.
