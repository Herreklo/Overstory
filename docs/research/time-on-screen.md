# Time on screen

How to show one place across years so that change reads, and is read right. `history-in-things.md` covers the marks on things: wear, stains, repairs, layers. This study covers change itself: how the same place is shown at different years, how the passage between them is shown, and how a viewer sees what changed, when and why, without being misled. It serves criteria 1, 3 and 4 in `CLAUDE.md` and the plan's step 4 (the corner in 1935, 1960, 1989 and 2020, dated by cold readers). It also serves the decision recorded in `docs/town-system.md` (For Øystein, 2): years pass differently in each game, from a week to generations, and each game picks how a jump is shown from what is found here.

Written on Opus, October 2026, from four research passes run side by side (art and film; photography, maps and conservation; games; perception science) and the project's own pages. Unmarked claims come from the maker's own words or the paper itself, read in full. Claims from reports by others, wikis, abstracts or search summaries are marked (secondary). Claims no source confirmed, and our own readings, are marked (unverified). The shared web-search budget ran out partway through all four passes, so the later sources were reached only through known addresses; what could not be opened is listed under Gaps.

## The bottom line

- **The eye finds a change only where something jumps.** In the flicker studies, a grey blank of 80 ms between two photos made people take about 11 seconds to find a marginal change, sometimes more than 50. Without the blank they found it in under a second (Rensink, O'Regan and Clark 1997). A cut, a dissolve, a slow fade or a time-lapse all hide the jump. An aligned toggle in place, with nothing between, shows it.
- **Hold everything but the year.** Rephotographers match vantage, lens, day of year and hour, so time is the only variable (the Rephotographic Survey Project). Any change of camera, light, season or tier swamps the real change, and memory widens a remembered frame by itself (boundary extension). The renderer must take the fabric's year and the light's moment as separate inputs.
- **People see three or four changes at a time, and the meaningful ones first.** Central changes were found more than twice as fast as marginal ones of larger area. In VR, observers found at most three or four changes however many there were (Martin and colleagues 2023). The history budget per view (one, two, three) fits across a jump as well.
- **A cut invites a cause.** Viewers fill in an unseen causal moment across a cut as readily as one they were shown (Brockhoff and colleagues 2016). A jump from an event to a later state claims that the event caused it. That is a lie unless the record holds the link.
- **Two states hide the path; a third shows it.** Third View added a third survey to the nineteenth-century and 1970s pairs, and Vergara works in dated sequences. The corner's four years let readers see reversals (stone, then asphalt, then stone again) that a pair would flatten.
- **A real place in any year is mostly older than that year.** In remote sites the survey found change "often less than expected"; Bryggen's medieval cellars stand under eighteenth-century houses; the period sets that read true carry each object's own timeline (*Here*, 2024). A frame where everything is of its year is the commonest period lie. Grading an era by its photographs (sepia for the 1930s) is another.
- **Readers date pictures badly and parts well.** Untrained people dated colour photos to the decade 26 per cent of the time against 20 per cent by chance (Palermo, Hays and Efros 2012). The dated part (a sign, a lamp, a window) carries the year; the jump carries the order.
- **Conservation already has the conventions for honesty.** Phase plans give one colour per period, a dashed line for what is gone and no colour for what is undated (English Heritage). The charters ask that new work be identifiable on close inspection and that every reconstruction say how sure it is (Burra, London, Seville).
- **Jumps cost the memory of people.** Pentiment's playtesters forgot an act-one character who did not appear in act two, and the team could not see it because they knew everything (Sawyer). Majora's Mask cut its loop from a week to three days, partly because a week would be hard to remember. A person or object must recur across a jump to be recognised after it.
- **The architecture needs four things.** The drawing is a function of the record, the date and the view. Shaders take the date as an input instead of baking years since reset. Each part's life is stored as dated intervals. And a query returns what changed between two dates, with cause, weight and precision. That one query serves the transition, the change budget, the highlight, the cause view across years and the cold readers' answer key.

## 1. Art: one place held still

### Here

Richard McGuire's six-page *Here* (*RAW*, 1989) opens on the bare corner of a room. Each later panel shows the same spot at another time, out of order, and most carry small insets from other moments, each labelled with its year (Wikipedia; Columbia's comics exhibition; both secondary). McGuire told Publishers Weekly the idea came from moving into a flat and wondering who had lived there. His first version split each panel down the room's corner, one half running backwards in time and the other forwards. Seeing Windows software, with many frames open at once, gave him the rest. He kept a running timeline from newspapers, letters, maps and photographs. The 2014 book widened it to 304 pages with the book's gutter as the room's corner. Reviewers noticed small things changing across decades: wallpaper, palette, a chair, a fold-out bed (secondary). The grammar is one fixed viewpoint and an anchor that never moves. Every window carries its year, windows from other times sit inside the base year's picture, and chronology is broken so that rhymes across time carry meaning.

Robert Zemeckis's 2024 film kept the fixed camera. Production designer Ashley Lamont said the team first fixed the constants: the house across the street, the window, the fireplace, the door, the camera. They built two identical sets matched to the millimetre, because cross-fades reveal any difference, and gave each kind of object its own timeline: sofas, armchairs, televisions. Cinematographer Don Burgess said every scene had an exact day and hour, with the room's light pre-programmed for overcast, rain, snow and season. Critics were split. Berardinelli credited the changing décor and the house holding still across seasons, but found that jumps of a day and of centuries look alike (secondary). Round and Fitch observed that the book keeps several eras on nearly every spread, so the reader keeps comparing, while the film uses its insets mostly as passing transitions (secondary). What we take: constants first, light from the date and hour, a timeline per object. What fails: comparison reduced to a transition, and jumps whose size cannot be gauged.

### The same view, dated

Jörg Müller's *The Changing Countryside* (1973) is seven large pictures of one Swiss landscape from one viewpoint, each dated to the day, from 6 May 1953 to 3 October 1972 (German Wikipedia; secondary). Critics credit its sober detail, used in schools for decades, with teaching generations to care about built heritage. It carries two warnings. The season and weekday rotate from picture to picture, so a viewer must separate the season's change from history's. And it argues a thesis (everything sacrificed to profit, in Müller's words) that a reader can take for causal history.

Hiroshige's *One Hundred Famous Views of Edo* (1856 to 1859) carries a year-and-month seal on every print and orders the set by season (secondary). Monet held one motif while light and air changed, then reworked the canvases so the set held together, and showed them together (secondary). A 2023 study tied his and Turner's hazier, whiter palettes to rising air pollution, so the air itself is a dated fact (secondary). Nicholas Nixon has photographed the four Brown sisters once a year since 1975, always in the same order (secondary). In *Smoke* (1995) a shopkeeper photographs his corner at 8 every morning; a friend finds the albums all alike until told to slow down, and then finds his dead wife crossing the street (secondary). Sameness is the frame that makes a small difference legible, and the payoff is recognising someone known.

### A room across years

*Citizen Kane*'s breakfast montage compresses a marriage into about two minutes at one table, the costume changing between cuts and a waltz darkening each time; Welles credited Wilder's *The Long Christmas Dinner* (1931), ninety years of one family's dinners in one room (secondary). Chris Ware's *Building Stories* (2012) lets a Chicago brownstone think in its own script and keep tallies of a century of tenants (secondary): a place can have a voice and a ledger beside the pictures.

### Museums

Norsk Folkemuseum's Wessels gate 15 is the nearest precedent to the corner: an 1865 Christiania tenement rebuilt at the museum, whose flats show 1879, 1905, 1965 and 1979 off the main stair and 1935, 1950, 1982 and 2002 off the back stair (the museum). The 1950 flat is a laundress's whole household, collected in 1957 (secondary), so it carries the older things people kept (unverified). Aarhus's Den Gamle By lays out districts by period, and its page names what marks the district of 1900 to 1927 at street scale: pavements, street lamps, public telephones, colour advertising. Bergen's Bryggen keeps fifteenth-century cellars under houses rebuilt after 1702 (secondary): the oldest fabric survives low down, and what shows is younger than the plan.

## 2. Photography and maps

### Rephotography

The Rephotographic Survey Project (1977 to 1979; Ellen Manchester, Mark Klett, JoAnn Verburg, later Gordon Bushaw and Rick Dingus) returned to 122 western sites photographed by the nineteenth-century surveys. It matched the camera position, framing, time of year and time of day, and logged camera and map data for whoever came next (a founder's account, Blue Sky Gallery). Two findings matter. Cities, mines and reservoirs made the big changes, but in remote places change was often less than expected. And the old photographers' viewpoints were so consistent the team could often predict where they had stood: the framing is itself a fact about what someone chose to show. Strict repeat photographers log station, date, hour, lens, camera axis and sun azimuth (Christian Science Monitor, 1985; secondary).

Third View (1997 to 2000) went back to 109 sites a third time and recorded what the first survey had not: interviews, sound, video and artefacts, published as a database (Nevada Museum of Art; secondary). Three points show that change between two dates is not a straight line. Klett and Byron Wolfe later set old photographs into new panoramas at their true place: Ansel Adams's 1940 pine on Sentinel Dome stands dead in 2002 (secondary). Camilo José Vergara has photographed the same buildings in Harlem, Camden, Detroit and elsewhere from the same spots since the 1970s, and says an empty lot means something only as part of a sequence (Metalocus, quoting him; secondary). When the Library of Congress built a viewer for his 18,000 photographs, its researcher rejected a two-image slider as too limited and chose a timeline beside a map (Segal's paper).

### Then and now, and long exposures

Sergey Larenkov and Jo Teeuwisse blend 1940s photographs into present views so soldiers fade into modern streets (secondary); a critic noted that stacking every layer of a place turns it to grey mush (secondary). *Dear Photograph* and the Flickr group "Looking into the past" hold an old print up at its spot and photograph both (secondary): the print's edge keeps the old image an object, which is honest about what is old. Daguerre's *Boulevard du Temple* (1838) shows an empty boulevard but for a man having his boots blacked, because the traffic moved during the exposure (secondary). A view averaged over time keeps buildings and loses people, and a street drawn without its people reads as deserted.

### Satellites and maps

Google Earth's Timelapse (2021) covers 1984 to 2020 by computing one representative pixel per place per year from about 24 million images: each frame is a synthetic year. Google Earth's historical imagery mixes dates within one view (secondary), and Street View's past captures exist only where a car once drove, in its season. Norway's Norge i bilder holds about 4,000 aerial projects from 1935, the corner's first year, comparable side by side or by a transparency slider, each with its date and maker. Riksantikvaren's SEFRAK lists about 500,000 buildings from before 1900, each mapped and photographed. Historic towns atlases map growth by phase, "the current state of research" (the International Commission for the History of Towns), and the British atlas marks whether its information is vague or precise (the Historic Towns Trust). No Norwegian town is in the Scandinavian series.

## 3. Conservation and archaeology: original, altered, lost, guessed

English Heritage's phased plans of Kenilworth Castle (2015) give each period one hue, warm for the twelfth century through to cool mauve for modern work. Buildings that are gone are dashed outlines labelled "Site of"; walls nobody has dated stay uncoloured. The plan says when, gone and unknown at a glance. Edward Harris's matrix (1973; *Principles of Archaeological Stratigraphy*, 1979) orders deposits by superposition: what lies over is later, and a cut is later than what it cuts. Work on standing buildings added that gravity takes parts away, so the oldest parts left are the ones that resisted time (Wikipedia; secondary). Norway's NIKU practises the same as *bygningsarkeologi*. A building's phases are an ordered graph of what lies on or cuts what, and our dated record is that graph already.

The Burra Charter (2013) separates preservation, restoration (to a known state without new material), reconstruction (with new material) and adaptation. Changes should not distort the evidence or rest on conjecture (3.2); reconstruction should be identifiable on close inspection (20.2); new work readily identifiable as such (22.2). Its note on new work warns that imitative solutions can mislead the onlooker. The London Charter (2009) asks each visualisation to tell users whether it shows the existing state, a restoration on evidence or a hypothesis, and how uncertain it is (4.4). The Seville Principles (2011) ask that it always be possible to tell what is real, and that reconstructions show their levels of accuracy (4.2). Under historical rigour (5.2) they ask for every phase, decline included, never an idyllic past of seemingly new buildings. Byzantium 1200 scores each reconstructed element on ten steps from pure imagination to surviving original, and a Spanish version of 2022 colours the steps from grey to dark red (parpatrimonio). The Extended Matrix (Demetrescu, CNR) gives every reconstructed unit its chain of provenance back to the evidence.

Real buildings carry the acts that erased their periods. Hampton Court stands half Tudor and half Wren because the rebuilding stopped in 1694 (secondary). Stavanger Cathedral's restoration of 1866 to 1874 stripped its seventeenth- and eighteenth-century detail and is now criticised; Nidaros's west front was rebuilt from one engraving, partly on guesswork (both secondary). Such acts are events with dates and authors. Forensic Architecture (Goldsmiths) puts every source of an event on one clock and one model and keeps each source visible; the German newspaper taz objects that it takes sides under a claim of objectivity (secondary). It fits "evidence, not verdicts" only while the model is offered as an argument.

## 4. Film: the cut, the dissolve and the date

A match cut carries the eye across a jump by keeping a shape or an onlooker in place: in *A Canterbury Tale* (1944) a medieval falcon becomes a wartime plane with the same actor watching both (secondary). In *Zodiac* a time-lapse of the Transamerica Pyramid rising marks the years: a landmark under construction is a clock that is also a fact (secondary). *Once Upon a Time in America* places each of its three periods with one prop or song; recut into chronological order for its American release, it failed (secondary), so out-of-order time works if every jump is anchored. In *Cinema Paradiso* the returning man finds the cinema demolished for a car park beside a fountain that has not moved (secondary). Since the 1970s almost all film transitions are plain cuts, and the dissolve that once marked a change of time is a weak convention (Cutting 2011). Viewers mark new events at breaks in action; a jump in time with the action carrying on barely registers (Magliano and Zacks 2011).

For *The Irishman* each decade was graded like its film stock (secondary). For *Once Upon a Time in Hollywood* more than 75 shots removed later billboards and buildings (secondary): subtract what is newer, keep what still stood. Four errors recur.

1. **The grade as era.** It dates a scene by its photographs, not its world, and in a simulation would claim the 1930s were brown.
2. **Everything new.** A period street built only from things of that period. No production designer's statement of the rule was opened (unverified as a stated rule), but *Here*'s object timelines, the collected households and Bryggen's cellars point the same way.
3. **The Tiffany problem.** Jo Walton's name for a true detail that modern audiences read as an error; its twin, parachronism, is an old thing still in use (secondary). A true mix of ages needs cues, such as wear matching each part's age.
4. **The thesis as cause.** A montage curated to an argument implies causes the facts may not hold.

## 5. Games

*Titanfall 2*'s "Effect and Cause" (Respawn, 2016) lets the player switch at will between a facility intact and in ruins. Jake Keating built two stacked maps aligned exactly, because small misalignments dropped players into rooms that did not exist; it was the slowest level to finish and was used once so it would not go stale (Vice). The past is lit and calm, the present burning, overgrown and wet (Kotaku; secondary). *Dishonored 2*'s "A Crack in the Slab" does the same for a mansion three years apart, and acts in the past change the present (secondary). They are the clearest comparisons we found in games, and both cost two aligned builds. *Chrono Trigger* and *Day of the Tentacle* show a cause in the past and its effect at the same place later; Dave Grossman's rule was that doing one thing here causes another thing there (secondary).

Josh Sawyer built *Pentiment* (2022) as tight acts of strictly moving time with jumps of many years between them, and the town remembers whom the player blamed (TheGamer). In act-three playtests a character met in act one who did not matter in act two was forgotten; the team, knowing everything, had not seen it (Game Developer). *Mafia II* (2010) takes the player out of the city through a prison chapter between 1945 and 1951; its creative director wanted the eras to feel very different, winter and muted colour against summer, hot rods and rock and roll (Gaming Nexus). *Final Fantasy VI*, *Fire Emblem: Three Houses* and *Ocarina of Time* bridge their jumps with a sleeping or sealed hero (secondary). Each gives the gap a reason and then a full set of new signals.

*Unpacking* (2021) names each level by its year. Its developers said there was no way to show the time between moves, so players sit in a zone of ambiguity, and one stage made statements nobody intended (GDC 2022 talk, via Game Developer; secondary). A Kotaku reviewer read the boyfriend as the worst, then saw another player read the same room the opposite way (secondary). *Majora's Mask* cut its loop from a week to three days, partly because a week would be hard to remember (secondary). *Deathloop* advances time only when the player leaves a district, so a morning's havoc shows in the evening (secondary). *Outer Wilds* lets slow processes be watched as they happen within each 22-minute loop (secondary).

*The Exit 8* (2023) is a looping corridor with one anomaly per pass or none. After release many players reported resets "with no anomaly", and the developer could not tell bugs from missed changes (4Gamer; secondary). Familiarity breeds blindness, and players blame the game for what they missed. Lucas Pope added chapters to *Return of the Obra Dinn* after testers were confused by deaths shown out of order (secondary). City builders mostly lack time-lapses; players add them as mods (FactorioMaps, RimWorld's Progress Renderer). Worch and Smith's GDC 2010 notes say what matters to play must be unambiguous while optional depth may be missed, and quote Arkane's Raphael Colantonio: players value seeing their acts persist.

## 6. Perception: what the eye catches and what it fills in

### Change blindness

Rensink, O'Regan and Clark (1997) alternated two versions of a photo with a grey blank of 80 ms between them. Changes to things describers had mentioned took 7.3 alternations, about 4.7 seconds; changes to things nobody mentioned took 17.1, about 11 seconds and sometimes over 50, though they were larger. Without the blank both were found in 1.4 alternations, under a second. A one-word cue naming the place made detection fast and erased the gap. Their account: a change is seen only where attention is, and the blank swamps the motion signal that would draw it there. With pictures changed during eye blinks, more than 40 per cent of changes were missed even with the eye on the spot (O'Regan and colleagues 2000). Simons and Levin (1998) swapped the stranger asking for directions behind a passing door: 7 of 15 noticed, though all 50 students asked beforehand said they would. Simons and Rensink (2005) group flicker, blinks, occlusion and gradual change as one family that hides the change signal; many observers missed a building vanishing over twelve seconds, then knew it was gone when shown the first photo again. Rensink (2002) puts the number of things watched for change at once at four or five, and experts in a field spot meaningful changes faster.

### Comparison

Side by side is slow: two fields of 30 objects with one difference took about 11 seconds and 40 fixations, and 13 per cent of differences were missed even after both were looked at (Pomplun and colleagues 2001). Toggling aligned images makes subtle differences pop out; misalignment brings blindness back (Drew and colleagues 2016; secondary). With a prior mammogram beside the current one, false marks fell by 44 per cent (Roelofs 2007; secondary). Subtracting an earlier chest X-ray from a later one raised detection of new findings (ROC area 0.89 to 0.98) and cut reading time by a fifth (Difazio 1997; secondary), while misregistered pairs made false differences (Kano 1994; secondary). Tombaugh found Pluto in 1930 with a blink comparator: two plates of one sky switched fast, so only what moved jumped (secondary).

Animated transitions beat static jumps for tracking objects, staged at about a second a step, but can assert a false identity when one thing turns into an unrelated one, and animated motion invites a perception of cause (Heer and Robertson 2007). Small multiples were more accurate than animation for analysing change, and viewers called animation exciting and confusing (Robertson and colleagues 2008). Overlay beat side-by-side multiples (Ondov and colleagues 2019; secondary).

### Memory, dating and false cause

People recognise a seen object against the same object in another state 87 per cent of the time when both are shown (Brady and colleagues 2008). Noticing a change unprompted is weak, though, and memory widens the frame: 95 per cent of drawings made from memory showed more than the picture had held (Intraub and Richardson 1989; secondary). In VR, 1 of 77 walkers noticed doors moved behind them (Suma 2011; secondary), and people told to look found at most three or four changes in a 360-degree scene, missing about 40 per cent in plain view (Martin and colleagues 2023). Untrained people dated colour photos to the decade 26 per cent of the time against 20 by chance (Palermo, Hays and Efros 2012); renovated houses read younger (Zeppelzauer and colleagues 2018). Whether sepia reads older was not confirmed. Brockhoff and colleagues (2016) cut a clip so a moment of contact was skipped: viewers reported seeing it as often as when it was shown, and referees did no better.

## 7. What this means for us

### Rules for showing time

Each rule follows from the evidence above; together they extend the rules in `history-in-things.md` (section 7) from marks to change.

1. **One record, one date, one drawing.** This is decided (`docs/town-system.md`), and the evidence gives it a second job. A comparison means something only if every pixel difference between two dates comes from the record. Radiology's subtraction images show what misregistration does: it manufactures difference. Seeded texture, tiers and cameras must therefore be identical between two dates for anything the record did not change.
2. **Hold everything but the year.** Same camera, tier, day of year, hour, weather and light, as the rephotographers do. The fabric's year and the light's moment are separate inputs. A comparison across seasons must say so, because a bare tree or a snowed yard otherwise reads as decline (Müller's rotating seasons).
3. **No jump is read through a gap alone.** A cut, a dissolve, a slow fade and a time-lapse all hide the change signal. Whatever carries the passage, an aligned comparison follows it: the earlier state at the same framing, toggled in place with nothing between.
4. **Date every jump.** Viewers treat a jump with continuing action as continuous, and cannot gauge a jump's size unless told. The date shown carries the record's precision: a year for a fact, a decade for a derived work, a season where the record holds a month.
5. **Budget the changes.** People find three or four changes at a time and the meaningful ones first. Across a jump each view points at no more than its history budget (one at the city camera, two at 12 m, three at the street stop), chosen by weight and placed where the eye goes.
6. **Point only at what the record changed.** A valid cue removes blindness; a highlight is a claim. Highlights, outlines and remarks come from the change query, never from a picture difference, and a derived change points at a decade.
7. **No cause by juxtaposition.** A cut from an event to a later state claims the event caused it, and viewers fill in the link. Juxtapose an event and a consequence only where the record links them; never morph one thing into an unrelated one.
8. **Every year is mostly older than itself.** The newest parts date the frame; the rest keep their own ages. The share of visible parts installed in the frame's own decade is a measure, checked against the record.
9. **Light of the date, never a grade of the era.** The sun, the weather and the air of that day (coal smoke in 1935 belongs to the deposit field `history-in-things.md` proposes). A period photographic look belongs on photographs in the world.
10. **Something recurs across every jump.** A person, an object or a landmark the viewer knew before (Pentiment's lesson, Unpacking's objects, *Cinema Paradiso*'s fountain), so the viewer knows it is the same place and can measure the change against it.
11. **Absence must be true too.** An empty street reads as deserted, a quiet history as neglect. People, cars and small acts are drawn from the record at that hour and year, or the view is marked as a study.

Criterion 1 is the building's history read at a glance and right; 3 is a consequence the page does not make obvious; 4 is preferring the scene to the page without being misled.

| Return in play, from memory alone | weak: noticing from memory fails | strong if noticed | weak: a re-framed view reads as change | nothing at the spot shows the earlier state |

The table is our reading of sections 1 to 6. Three transitions serve all three criteria: the aligned toggle, the years strip and the held-up photograph. Three serve feeling and rhythm but not reading: the cut with a card, the dissolve and the time-lapse. They are fine as a passage when followed by one of the first three.

### What each span shows

The decision says every system answers at every resolution. Brand's layers give the clocks: stuff by the week, use by the year, skin by the decade, structure by the generation (`world-that-remembers.md`, principle 4).

| | A week | A year | Generations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What changes on screen | scene state: people, cars, chairs out, lit windows, bins; weather and light; posters pasted; a shop window redressed; a hoarding up | seasons (leaves, snow, the light's height); upkeep: a coat of paint, a patch; a few works; a shop opened or closed; trees grown a little | plans and their scars; demolition, fallow lots, rebuilding; constructions renewed; trees as clocks; people aged, born, gone |
| What does not, and is still true | wear, lichen and growth (true, unseen) | plot lines, structures; most coats | the street lines; the oldest parts that resisted time |
| The moment of passage | none: day and night, time of day | a dated card or chapter, the month as well as the year | a chapter break with a dated card and a reason for the gap |
| How change is shown | the player passes the same places at other hours (Deathloop's districts); a person remarks on what matters | the same views in the same season a year apart; works shown as sites that stand for months | the same views at chapter start, with an aligned comparison to the last chapter's state at the spot; the years strip in an archive |
| Budget across a jump | none needed: changes are few and transient | one or two works per view, the season named | the history budget per view; heavy changes split across views, or read at the coarser view first |
| Serves | 3, through people and records (a poster is a dated record) | 1 weakly, 3 for a shop's fate | 1, 3 and 4 when compared; 3 most of all |
| The risk | posters and windows missed (fine unless play needs them) | the season read as history | everything new; forgotten people; cause by cut |

A generations jump at the street stop may hold fifteen changes. Showing them all at once collapses the read into "everything is different". That is close to how *Mafia II* signals its jump, and a real town does not change that way (rephotography's "less than expected"). Two answers keep it readable. Read the jump first at the coarser view, where one change per unit tells (a block gone, a new unit's seam), then at the street stop with its three. Or step through dated stills, as Third View's third point does.

### The history budget across a jump

The budget in `docs/living-fabric.md` counts the histories a view tells at one date. Across a jump a second count applies: the changes a transition points at. The numbers can be the same (one, two, three, all at eye level), because the evidence for both is the same limit of attention. Three rules follow.

- **Every change is drawn; the budget decides emphasis.** The screen follows the record, so a sign replaced is replaced whether or not it is pointed at. The budget picks which changes get the pointer, the centre of the frame and the light, by the weight the research already uses (caused by a named person, inside the game's window, unusual, linked to the player).
- **Drift is summarised, not pointed at.** Gradual change goes unseen and has no single moment. Lichen grown, paint faded and polish dulled read together as "older", and the change query reports them as magnitudes per layer, not as events.
- **A view over budget is split or coarsened.** If a jump leaves more heavy changes in a view than it can carry, the transition visits more views, or reads at the coarser one first.

### The player's own traces

Play changes the record, so two playthroughs reach different towns, and whether living through years differs from jumping is the engine's choice. On screen this means four things.

- **The player's acts look like anyone's.** A poster the band put up, the path worn to the loft's side door, a shop the player's character opened: each is an event with an actor and is drawn by the same rules. Nothing in the world marks it as the player's. Colantonio's point that players value seeing their acts persist is met by persistence, not by a spotlight.
- **They weigh more in the budget.** "Linked to the player" is already a weight, so a return after a jump puts one of the player's traces in the views it visits, if one exists. Recognition (`docs/town-plan.md`) is this moment.
- **The why-probe can filter by actor.** "What did I change here?" is a query on the change list, answered in the journal or the probe, never in the paint.
- **Two records, one date** is the same machinery as one record, two dates. An aligned toggle between two playthroughs at one date is the counterfactual observatory in `docs/ideas/game-layer-ideas.md`, at no extra cost.

One risk needs a guard. If the engine records more small events where the player has been, those places will look busier. That is true to the record, but a reader must not take a quieter place for a neglected one. Patchwork, coats and growth are derived from care and use at the same rates everywhere, so the fabric's condition never depends on whether anyone watched; only named small acts differ.

### The corner through the years

The plan's step 4 rebuilds the corner live in 1935, 1960, 1989 and 2020, and cold readers date it. From the evidence, it should be shown and judged like this.

**Shown.** Named cameras stored as data: the four judging views and eye level at the gully's landing, the same in every year. One light moment for all four years: 20 October in the same weather, grey drizzle first, then the low sun after showers, then the blue hour (`docs/foundations.md`). The hour is held as the sun's position, not the clock's, because summer time came and went: 20 October 1989 fell in winter time, and 20 October 2020 still in summer time. The day of year held, so the birches are in the same leaf. Two arms of scene state: the year's own people, cars and bins at that hour from the record, and a fabric-only arm without them, since cars and clothes date strongly and would carry the dating that criterion 1 asks of the buildings. Every frame exported with its key: the change list for each pair of years in that view, with cause, year or decade, and weight.

**Judged.** The readers in `docs/foundations.md`, with these tasks added to those in `history-in-things.md`, section 10:

1. *Order.* The four frames of one view, shuffled and undated: put them in order. Then date each.
2. *What changed?* Registered pairs, unlabelled: mark what changed and say why. Scored against the change list.
3. *What happened here?* The strip in order with its dates: free text, coded as fact, fair interpretation, a claim on texture, or a contradiction.

Three controls join the shuffled facts and layers-off controls already set:

- *Season:* the same year rendered in another month. Readers must not report a change of era, care or use.
- *Camera:* the same year from a camera half a metre off. Readers must not report changes. This tests the drawing's stability and memory's widening of the frame.
- *False cause:* a pair where a recorded event fell between the dates but did not cause the change in view. Readers must not tie them together with confidence.

**Passed, provisionally,** when:

- at least four readers in five order the four frames right, in every view;
- frames are dated within a decade wherever a dated part is in view (LT3's bar);
- at least two of the three budgeted changes are found per pair at the street stop;
- the season and camera controls draw under one false change per frame, none held with high confidence;
- the direction is right between every pair (already in `docs/foundations.md`).

Model readers see still frames, so the toggle itself, a moving transition, is tested only by people, in the engine's human test. Criterion 4 keeps its split readers, half shown the page first. 1960 matters most as the third point, where reversals show (setts to asphalt and back, a front stripped and restored). 2020 needs its handful of post-1989 items (`docs/town-system.md`, For Øystein, 3). Every change in the key must trace to an engine fact or a fixture fact labelled `authored` or `derived`, checked against the engine's front door (`rH28`).

### What the building blocks must carry

The architecture is designed next. For time on screen it needs these, each serving every span:

1. **The drawing is a function of (record, fabric date, light moment, weather, view, tier),** hashed. The watched-or-jumped test takes one record, steps the renderer through the gully and the corner month by month to a date, jumps there once in a fresh run, and compares the hashes of the drawn frames.
2. **The date is an input, not a bake.** Today the shaders read years since reset, and the living fabric bakes the life into city-tier vertex data. Bake the reset year and the per-point rate instead, and pass the fabric date as a global. Then any tier answers any date without a rebake until a discrete change, a jump costs a uniform, and two dates render from one set of instances.
3. **Each part's life is stored as dated intervals.** Between works and events a part keeps its construction, material, origin, reset years and rates; the state at any date is a lookup plus the layer law. The yearly runner writes the intervals offline, as it writes `life` now.
4. **Parts carry lifespans.** A part exists from its making to its removal, and the scene draws what exists at the date. Both states of a comparison are then the shared instances plus the few parts alive at only one date. Chunks rebuilt offline for a big change keep their earlier version resident while a comparison in view needs it; comparisons are local, so the memory is bounded. The town system's plots, units and ways are read at the date the same way, and large works keep their dated phases (`docs/living-fabric.md`, Big change), so a jump that lands inside a work shows the hoarding, the pit or the scaffold, and a week-long game set in 1985 sees the site.
5. **A change query.** `changes(scope, t0, t1)` returns the discrete changes (made, removed, replaced, construction moved, coat, sign, use, keeper), each with its cause, actor, provenance, precision (year or decade), weight, the parts it touched and its region in each view, plus drift magnitudes per layer. It serves the transition's budget, the highlight, the cause view across years, the why-probe and journal across years, and the cold readers' key.
6. **Separate clocks.** Fabric year, day of year (leaf, snow, the vegetation's phase), hour (light) and weather are four inputs. A tree's growth reads years since planting; its leaf reads the day of year.
7. **Seeds stable for a part's whole life.** Non-semantic texture is seeded by part id, never by a build or a date; a replaced part gets a new id. The diff check renders two dates, subtracts, and fails any changed pixel outside the change list's regions and the drift layers. It is the mesh-side trace check (`docs/experiments.md`, Findings) run across dates.
8. **Scene state by hour and year,** from habits and the record, with a fabric-only switch for judging.
9. **An actor and a precision on every event,** so the budget can weigh the player's traces and no label can show a year the record does not hold.

None of this asks the renderer to accumulate anything frame by frame. History storage needs the record plus the intervals; both states at once need only the lifespans and a date per render, not two builds.

### Where the evidence points one way within a span

- **A week.** Do not fake fabric drift: in a week it is invisible, and true. Change is scene state and records. Where play needs a change noticed (a new poster, a window boarded), a person remarks on it or the task leads there; otherwise it may be missed.
- **A year.** Compare like season with like. Show works as sites that stand for months, and give each card a month and a year.
- **Generations.** Chapters with dated jumps and a reason for each gap, as *Pentiment*, *Mafia II* and the sleeping-hero games do. Each chapter returns to the same views first, with the earlier state available at the spot (a photograph held up, toggled in place). People the player should know again appear or are mentioned between jumps. A time-lapse may carry the passage but never alone.

## For Øystein to decide

1. **The comparison in play (play).** Recommended: the world's own media first. A photograph from the town archive, or one the player took earlier, held up at its spot, toggles in place to the present, as *Dear Photograph* and Klett's insets do. A free interface toggle only in games whose play is reading the past (the archivist, the restoration architect in `docs/ideas/roles.md`). It follows `docs/research/interface.md` (the world's own media first) and keeps the world free of overlays.
2. **The time-lapse as spectacle (taste).** Recommended: allowed as an opening or a chapter's passage, where it is beautiful for slow things (trees, a plot's cycle, a quay pushed out), always bracketed by dated stills and followed by a comparison. Never the only way a change is shown, because gradual change hides what changed.
3. **Whether the player's own acts are ever marked as theirs (play).** Recommended: never in the world; in the journal and the why-probe only. Persistence is the reward; a spotlight would turn evidence into a verdict on the player.
