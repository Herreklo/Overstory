# City types

What kinds of towns and cities exist, what makes each read as itself, and how each grows and changes. Then how a setting pack would hold a type so the same systems can build it. This is the content side of the town system: the harbour town first, then other towns and metropolises on the scale of London or New York. Each is a setting pack on the same engine and systems (`docs/town-plan.md`, The first of many; `docs/foundations.md`, Packs and facts).

Neighbouring pages cover the rest. The form of cities in general (urban morphology, space syntax, planning systems, the underground) is in `docs/research/urban-design.md`. The forces that shape them (land economics, transport eras, growth, gentrification) are in `docs/research/city-forces.md`, and Stavanger's change since 1945 in `docs/research/town-change.md`. Here the general tools are used only to sort types.

Claims from encyclopaedia, guide or news summaries are marked (secondary); claims we could not confirm against any source are marked (unverified). Numbers set starting values; they are not for copying.

## The idea in one paragraph

A city type is a way of laying out land that a society repeats because it fits how it moves, governs, builds and defends itself. A coloniser who sells lots draws a grid; households that guard their privacy end their lanes in cul-de-sacs; a state that hands land to one developer gets superblocks. Each type has a plan (streets, blocks, plots, open space), a building tradition, a density and grain, and a way of changing. The plan outlasts the buildings, so a real town is several types laid down in sequence, each fitted into the frame the older ones left and joined at seams. A setting pack should hold its types as data: a catalogue of plan types with their numbers, rules and ways of changing. A town should hold its history as dated areas, each of one type. The systems stay the same; the pack brings the types.

## The types

Each in the same order: logic, plan, buildings and grain, change, and what a viewer recognises at a glance.

### Classical grids and their afterlife

A grid divides land fairly and fast, for colonists or soldiers. Miletus was rebuilt on one after its destruction in 493 BC; Hippodamus is credited with the idea, perhaps too generously (secondary).

- **Plan.** Priene had about 80 blocks of about 35 by 47 m, with six streets along the slope and fifteen across it (secondary). The cross streets turned into stairs where the hill was steep, and main streets were six to seven metres wide (secondary). A Roman camp: a walled rectangle (walls about 660 m long) with two main streets crossing at the forum (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Courtyard houses, a few to a block; the better blocks at Priene held four houses, most were divided further (secondary). Fine and even.
- **Change.** The streets last; the blocks do not stay whole. In Syria and Palestine colonnaded streets narrowed and public space passed to private use and trade by the sixth century (Kennedy 1985; secondary). Turin's Via Garibaldi follows its decumanus (secondary). Lucca's amphitheatre filled with houses on its ruins, keeping the oval; in the 1830s the inside was cleared into a square (secondary).
- **At a glance.** A straight cross of streets inside an old, irregular town; an oval of houses where an arena stood.

### Medieval organic and market towns

Growth by many small decisions along routes: a crossing, a castle gate, a church, a harbour.

- **Plan.** Streets follow paths, contours and field edges; the market is a widened street or a triangle where roads meet; a church on high ground; a wall with gates and suburbs outside. Long, narrow burgage plots run back from the street: in Alnwick Conzen found frontages of about 28 feet or simple fractions and multiples of it (about 8.5 m; secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** One house per plot, two to four storeys; the backs filled with workshops and cottages reached by alleys. In Lübeck the brick merchant house turned its gable to the street over a ground-floor hall up to about 5 m high, with alleys of about 1.2 m to courts of small houses behind (secondary). Fine at the front, packed behind.
- **Change.** The burgage cycle (`docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`, principle 5). The wall becomes a ring road or boulevards once it is not needed (Vienna's Ringstrasse; unverified as a rule).
- **At a glance.** Streets that bend and change width; narrow gables; one tower over everything.

### Planted towns

A lord or king founds a town to settle land, raise rents and hold a border; the plan is drawn before anyone arrives.

- **Plan.** Bastides: Monpazier (1284) is a grid on level ground with a market square entered at its corners under arcades (cornières). Gaps of a few dozen centimetres between houses slowed fire (andrones), and rear lanes were one to two metres wide (carreyrous; secondary). Zähringer towns, Freiburg im Breisgau (1120) and Bern (1191), are rectangular blocks along a broad market street, though historians deny a single Zähringer model (secondary). Kraków's charter of 1257 laid a grid round a square of a little over 200 m a side (secondary). The grid bends to keep older churches and an old trade route: a planted plan always meets what was there.
- **Buildings and grain.** Regular plots, two or three storeys, arcades on the square.
- **Change.** Small, economical grids survive almost whole; the squares keep their markets.
- **At a glance.** One square or one wide street as the town's room; a regular plan in open country.

### Islamic medinas

A city built by many owners under shared rules rather than one plan. Besim Hakim (1986) drew those rules from Maliki jurisprudence: no harm to a neighbour, privacy, the rights of whoever built first (secondary). Janet Abu-Lughod (1987) warned that the "Islamic city" was generalised from a few North African cities (unverified), so the type is a loose family of cases.

- **Plan.** A hierarchy: market streets (souqs) from the gates to the great mosque; quarters with their own mosque, oven, bath and fountain; dead-end residential lanes (derb in Morocco; secondary). Fez el-Bali, founded in the ninth century, covers about a square mile (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Courtyard houses with blank walls to the lane, a door and a few grilles (secondary); flat roofs as terraces; rooms bridging lanes. Very fine and dense.
- **Change.** Houses divide among heirs, rooms bridge lanes, lanes close; the hierarchy holds. In colonial Morocco a European town was laid out beside the medina rather than through it, so the city became two (unverified).
- **At a glance.** From above, flat roofs pierced by courtyards, and minarets; below, blank walls and sudden markets.

### Chinese planned capitals

The city as a diagram of cosmos and state. The *Kaogong ji* describes a square capital with three gates a side and nine main streets (secondary), the palace facing south.

- **Plan.** Sui and Tang Chang'an (582 to 904) enclosed about 84 km², with a central avenue about 150 m wide and 108 to 110 walled wards locked from dusk to dawn (secondary). Yuan Dadu (Beijing) set widths by rank: avenues 24 paces (about 37 m), streets 12, lanes (hutong) 6 (about 9 m) running east to west (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Courtyard houses on north-south axes, facing south for sun and shelter (secondary). One storey of grey roofs; only palaces and temples rise.
- **Change.** Walled wards gave way to shop streets (Song Kaifeng; unverified); courtyard houses were divided among many households after 1949 (unverified), then many hutong were cleared. The 1950s work-unit compound became today's gated estate, on blocks of 300 to 500 m between wide roads, handed to one developer (secondary).
- **At a glance.** An axis and a rectangle; a fine grid of courts; later, walled superblocks of identical towers.

### Japanese castle towns and machiya streets

Early capitals copied Chang'an (Heian-kyō, 794; secondary). Castle towns (jōkamachi) sorted people by rank round a lord's castle.

- **Plan.** The castle at the centre; the highest retainers between the moats; lower samurai further out; merchants on shop streets; temples grouped at the edge; roads that turn for defence (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** The machiya is a timber townhouse on a plot about 5.4 to 6 m wide and 20 m deep, the "eel's bed" (secondary). The shop is in front, an earthen passage runs beside the rooms, and small courtyard gardens sit between them (secondary). Narrow frontage is often put down to tax on street width (secondary). Very fine; two storeys of continuous lattice.
- **Change.** The plots endure while houses are replaced one by one; castle grounds became parks and offices (unverified). Tokyo reshapes land by readjustment, owners giving up part of their plots for streets and a reserve sold to pay for the works (secondary), so even new districts keep a fine grain.
- **At a glance.** A castle on a rise; long, narrow houses shoulder to shoulder; streets that kink at regular places.

### Nordic and Baltic wooden towns, and fishing villages

Timber was cheap and close, and fire was the main event in a town's life. These towns grew organically, burned, and were rebuilt by regulation, wider and straighter, sometimes in masonry. This is our town's family.

- **Before fire.** Narrow lanes and plots running to the water. Bergen's Bryggen kept its rows of two- and three-storey buildings (gårder), gables to the harbour and narrow passages between, rebuilt after every fire on the same plots; what stands is from after 1702 (secondary).
- **After fire.** Trondheim (1681): Cicignon laid wide streets over the burned town as firebreaks; the largest blocks were later cut by lanes (veiter), some on pre-fire routes (secondary). Kristiansand (1641): 54 blocks of about 60 by 120 m, streets about 15 m (secondary). After Turku burned in 1827, Engel set streets at least 18 m wide, and this became Finland's standard; the poor moved to cheaper plots at the edge (secondary). Umeå, after 1888, lined its widened streets with birch (secondary).
- **Rules.** Christiania required masonry from 1624 but did not enforce it at first, and wooden suburbs grew just outside the boundary (secondary). Norway's 1904 law, after Ålesund burned, forbade timber building in towns (secondary); Ålesund, with 10,000 of its 12,000 people homeless, was rebuilt in stone and brick in Jugendstil by 1907 (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Painted timber houses of one to three storeys; warehouses gable to the quay with hoists; masonry for church, bank and the rebuilt core.
- **Fishing villages.** Organised round the harbour: in Nusfjord fishermen's cabins (rorbuer) stand on poles at the water beside racks that once held over 600,000 fish (secondary). Lunenburg in Nova Scotia (1753) shows the opposite case: a British "model town" grid laid on a steep slope without regard to it (secondary).
- **Change.** Fire and rule reset the centre; the organic pattern survives at the edges, outside the rule or where the fire stopped (Turku's Luostarinmäki; secondary). Conservation later keeps the least wanted wooden areas (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`).
- **At a glance.** Timber walls and roofs of one colour family on the contours; a wide straight street that says "after the fire" beside lanes that say "before"; gables to the water.

### Colonial and frontier grids

A grid can be surveyed before settlers arrive and sold by the lot: the city as machine.

- **Plan.** The Laws of the Indies (1573) set a plaza one and a half times as long as wide, from 200 by 300 to 800 by 532 feet, best 400 by 600 (secondary). Four main streets leave from the middle of its sides and two from each corner, and arcades run round it (secondary). Philadelphia (1682) had five squares, and alleys were later cut into its big blocks (secondary). New York (1811) laid blocks 200 feet (61 m) deep and 250 to over 900 feet long; most streets are 60 feet wide, most avenues 100 (secondary). Its lots are 25 by 100 feet (unverified). Melbourne (1837): main streets 99 feet; "little streets" of 33 feet, added for rear access, soon became frontages (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Whatever each owner built, to the line; uniformity comes from the plan. Siksna found that a grid's first block size predicts how it develops, and that some sizes adapt better than others (secondary).
- **Change.** Lot by lot: houses become shops, shops become blocks, lots merge for towers; lanes are cut into big blocks and service lanes become streets.
- **At a glance.** One orientation to the horizon; a plaza with a church; the same block repeated, with every era on the same lot line.

### The grand manner: baroque, Haussmann, Cerdà

Power and order shown in straight axes ending on monuments; later the same means served traffic, hygiene and the market.

- **Plan.** Karlsruhe (1715) fans 32 streets out from the palace tower, after Versailles (secondary). Haussmann's Paris (1853 to 1870) cut 60 to 80 km of new avenues; estimates of the buildings demolished run from 20,000 to 40,000, and he put the displaced at 350,000 (secondary). Cerdà's Barcelona (1859): 113 m blocks with chamfered corners, 20 m streets, 50 m main roads (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Paris tied height to street width, about 20 m to the cornice on the widest streets (secondary). The result is six storeys, continuous balconies on the second and fifth floors and a zinc mansard at about 45 degrees (secondary). Cerdà meant each block to be built on two sides with gardens between (secondary).
- **Change.** Speculation closed and raised Cerdà's blocks; groups of nine are now being made into superblocks closed to through traffic (secondary). A cut through an old town leaves wedge plots and bare party walls, with old fabric behind the new fronts (unverified as a measured rule).
- **At a glance.** Diagonals and star junctions; even cornice lines; a monument at the end of every long view.

### Industrial towns, terraces and tenements

Housing for workers near work, as cheap as the law allowed; the law, written after epidemics and fires, designed the type.

- **Plan.** Unregulated courts reached by passages as narrow as 2 ft 9 in (secondary). Britain's Public Health Act 1875 required through houses with a yard and a rear alley; by-law streets were about 36 feet wide (secondary). Back-to-backs were banned nationally in 1909, yet Leeds built them until 1937, by accounts that differ on why (secondary). Berlin's 1853 code and the 1862 Hobrecht plan gave deep blocks of rear courtyards sized to a fire engine's turning, about 5.34 m square (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Two-up two-down brick terraces with slate roofs (secondary); Glasgow's sandstone tenements of three or four storeys round a common stair and back court, three flats in four of one or two rooms (secondary). Company towns graded houses by rank: Saltaire (from 1851) had over 800, an institute instead of a pub, the church facing the mill (secondary); Norsk Hydro controlled 80% of Rjukan's houses in 1925 (secondary).
- **Change.** British clearance from 1930 removed almost all unregulated housing (secondary); by-law terraces survived and were improved; mills became lofts; company towns ended when the company sold the houses.
- **At a glance.** Parallel rows of identical roofs and chimneys, back alleys, the works and its stack at the bottom.

### Garden cities and suburbs

Escape from the industrial town: houses in gardens, the street as a lane, then the car as the organising force.

- **Plan.** Parker and Unwin's Letchworth (from 1903; unverified) brought in the cul-de-sac for privacy, and Unwin's *Nothing Gained by Overcrowding* (1912) argued that fewer houses an acre need less road (secondary). Perry's neighbourhood unit (1929): an elementary school at the centre, a quarter-mile radius, about 160 acres for 5,000 to 9,000 people, arterial roads at the edge only (secondary). Radburn (1929) used cul-de-sacs to keep cars from people (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Detached or paired houses set back behind gardens; low coverage. Levittown, New York (1947 to 1951): 17,447 houses of 750 sq ft on curving streets, up to 30 a day (secondary).
- **Change.** The plan barely moves; the houses change most of any type, one diary at a time: extensions, garages, cladding, paved front gardens (the family's house in `docs/living-fabric.md`). Trees grow until they are the read.
- **At a glance.** Curves and cul-de-sacs; a house in a garden on every plot; after fifty years, more trees than roofs.

### Modernist new towns and estates

CIAM's city of separated functions: slabs and towers in open green, light and air for all, traffic apart.

- **Plan.** Brasília (Costa's winning plan, 1957): superblocks about 280 m a side holding around eleven six-storey blocks on pilotis (secondary). Britain designated 32 new towns from 1946, Stevenage first, built round neighbourhoods with a school and shops (secondary); Milton Keynes laid roads a kilometre apart (secondary). A Soviet microdistrict covered 10 to 60 ha inside main roads, with services within 400 to 500 m of every home (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** Sweden's Million Programme built a million homes from 1965 to 1974; one Swedish home in five dates from it (secondary). The GDR built about 1.52 million panel flats by 1990, up to 42% in one series, WBS 70 (secondary). Oslo's Lambertseter (1951 to 1958, 3,311 homes), often called Norway's first satellite town, set its shops, cinema, school and church on an axis beside the metro (secondary). Very coarse: few, large plots and a high open-space ratio.
- **Change.** Few plots, so the plan resists change. Buildings are reclad and insulated, balconies glazed; open ground becomes car parks, then gardens; some estates are demolished (unverified as a pattern).
- **At a glance.** Repeated slabs at one angle in green; a precinct at the centre; roads that skirt rather than enter.

### Informal settlements

People build on land they do not hold, under rules they set themselves, because no market or state provides a home they can afford. About 1.1 billion people live in slums or informal settlements (UN-Habitat; secondary).

- **Plan.** Footpaths first, then plots claimed along them; lanes follow the land and the easiest walk; stairs up steep hills, lanes along drains.
- **Buildings and grain.** Self-built, then rebuilt. John Turner found that self-builders in Peru took two to twenty years to finish a house and a district about fifteen years to reach a fairly complete character (secondary). Brazil's favela houses grow upward on their roof slab to three to six storeys, and roof rights are sold (secondary). Very fine, very dense, almost no open space.
- **Change.** Consolidation: sheet to brick and concrete, paths to paved lanes. Jakarta's Kampung Improvement Programme (from 1969) paved paths and built drains and water for about three million people without giving title (secondary). Turkey's gecekondu ("built overnight") were legalised and rebuilt as five- to ten-storey blocks by small developers, later by the state (secondary).
- **At a glance.** A hillside of unfinished concrete and brick, every house a different height; water tanks; one paved spine and a maze of stairs.

### The contemporary metropolis

Land value and the rules that cap or reward it make the form. The plan is mostly inherited; the type is in the height.

- **Plan.** Lots merge; the grid stays. China builds gated superblocks (above). Edge cities grow at motorway junctions: Garreau (1991) set the bar at five million square feet of offices and 600,000 of shops, more jobs than bedrooms, and nothing like a city thirty years before (secondary).
- **Buildings and grain.** New York's 1916 zoning stepped buildings back as they rose and let towers cover a quarter of the lot, giving the "wedding cake" (secondary). The 1961 code set floor area ratios and traded bonus floors for plazas: the tower in the plaza (secondary). Hong Kong builds malls over stations and towers on podiums over the malls, and land near the line pays for the railway (secondary).
- **Change.** Each boom raises the ceiling; towers are replaced while the street grid stays.
- **At a glance.** Height variance first: a cluster of towers over a field of older, lower blocks; plazas and podiums at their feet; parking at the edge.

## The dimensions that matter

What differs across the types, roughly in order of how much it decides the read:

| Dimension | Range across the types | How to measure or state it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The network's family and order | accreted, grid, radial, tree-like, footpath | orientation order, four-way junctions, dead ends, circuity; read from the city camera before anything else |
| 2. Block size and what fills the inside | 35 m (Priene) to a kilometre (Milton Keynes); yard, court, backland, gardens, open green | block dimensions; it tracks how people moved when the block was laid (`docs/research/city-forces.md`) |
| 3. The plot | the 5.5 m machiya, the 8.5 m burgage, the 7.6 m New York lot, the estate that is one plot | frontage, depth, module; plots outlast buildings and decide where change can happen |
| 4. Height, coverage and floor space | one storey to a hundred | GSI, L, FSI, OSR, and the rule that caps height: none, the street's width (Paris, Berlin), a setback (New York 1916), a floor area ratio (1961) |
| 5. The street edge | built to the line, set back; shops, stoops, blank walls, gardens | doors per hundred metres; the street's height-to-width ratio |
| 6. The open-space pattern | inward (courtyards), outward (gardens), shared (squares, parks, slab-green), none | kind, size and placement |
| 7. Slope and water | follow the contours, climb by stairs, ignore the slope (Lunenburg), terrace on plinths; gables to the quay, a quay road, fill | a rule per type |
| 8. Who decides | one founder, shared rules among many owners, a regulator, a company, no one | sets the change regime: lot by lot, fire and replan, clearance, consolidation |
| 9. Material and construction | timber in Norway, brick in Lübeck for the same plan | the pack's shelf, not the type's |

The first four are the type's identity and can be measured. The fifth and sixth carry its life at the street stop. The seventh is where a type meets a particular land, and the eighth gives it a history.

## One town, many types

A real town is its types laid down in order, each fitted into what the last left. Four patterns recur.

- **Plan units and seams.** Each area of one type and one date is a plan unit (Conzen). Where two meet, the seam shows the date: the street changes width or angle, the building line steps, plots change size. Seams are where the town reads best (`docs/visual-direction.md`, Areas).
- **The frame.** A new type is laid inside the old one's frame: Trondheim's lanes on pre-fire routes, Lucca's houses on the arena, Melbourne's service lanes turned streets, Kraków's grid bent round its churches.
- **Fringe belts.** Where growth stalled at a wall or a barrier, large open uses gather, and the next ring of growth passes beyond (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`).
- **Transitions.** Types turn into other types for causes, and each leaves named traces:

| From | To | Cause (a fact) | What it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| classical grid | souq and lanes | centuries of encroachment | straight spines through a crooked plan |
| arena or monument | ring of houses, then a square | reuse, then clearance | an oval street line |
| organic wooden town | regulated grid | fire and a rule | old routes as lanes; a surviving wooden fringe |
| town wall | ring road, boulevards | the wall no longer needed | a curved belt of wide street and public buildings |
| open block | closed block | speculation | taller, deeper buildings than the plan meant |
| a row of lots | one merged plot | a boom; zoning | a break in the plot rhythm and the height |
| service lane | street | trade wants frontage | shops on what was a back |
| walled ward or compound | shop street; gated estate | the state's rules change | the old block outline |
| informal settlement | consolidated, then legal blocks | time, savings, legalisation | the footpath plan under new buildings |
| industrial quarter | lofts, studios, then flats | the works close; vacancy | chimneys, hoists, rails in the paving |
| terraces | estate | clearance | an old street line in a park; a pub left standing |
| estate | reclad, infilled, partly demolished | renewal | new skins on old slabs |
| farmland | suburb | the town reaches it | a farm lane's bend in a street; the farmhouse among villas |
| castle and moats | park and offices | the lord's power ends | moats as water, walls as embankments |

The oldest types sit at the origin (the harbour, the crossing, the castle). Younger ones lie outward and at the reset places, where fire or clearance made room. From the city camera a viewer reads the town's history as a sequence of plan units. The history budget allows one history per thing at that view (`docs/living-fabric.md`), and it can spend it on the type itself.

The harbour town is already built this way (`docs/town-plan.md`). It has an organic core at the Basin's head, sea houses gable to the quays and the old town on contour lanes. Then come the Grid after the fires, Tin Town's rows over the cannery, villas on the heights and a garden suburb. The newest layers are New Town's renewal, stopped by conservation, the seventies estate, the oil campus and a half-converted waterfront. What it lacks is the data form that would let the same machinery lay out New York.

## What a setting pack should take

The pack holds types, and the town holds dated plan units, each of one type. The engine decides when and where a type is laid (its fabric pass, `docs/research/generating-the-past.md`). The layout is generated once at that event and stored as facts, as the town plan already requires.

**A plan type** is one entry in the pack's catalogue:

| Field | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `network` | family (accreted, grid, radial, loop and cul-de-sac, footpath); orientation order; share of four-way junctions and dead ends; street ranks and widths |
| `blocks` | size and shape ranges; what fills the inside |
| `plots` | frontage module and range, depth, orientation to street or water; frontage rule (to the line, or set back by a range) |
| `buildings` | the building types allowed (the pack's diaries and era shelves) with storeys, roof forms and coverage |
| `density` | Spacematrix targets: GSI, FSI, L, OSR |
| `open_space` | squares, courtyards, gardens, parks, slab-green: kind, size, placement |
| `slope`, `water` | how it meets the land: contours, stairs, ignore, terrace; gables to the quay, quay road, fill |
| `edges` | wall, fence, arterial, fringe belt |
| `rules` | the dated regulations that bind it (a fire rule, a by-law, a zoning code), each a fact with a year |
| `change` | the verbs it uses and their rates (infill, divide, merge, add a storey, clear, consolidate) and the transitions it may make, with their traces |
| `read` | what tells the type at each view (city camera, 12 m, street stop), for the history budget |
| `measured_from` | two or three real exemplars and the numbers taken from them, as `docs/research/stavanger-centre.md` did |

The pack also holds its land, its dated rules and its transitions table. Its script of eras says which types each era lays, where, and on which trigger: a founding, a fire, a boom, a plan. A pack never changes a rule of the system; if a type needs one, the rule was wrong (`docs/living-fabric.md`, section 6).

## Gaps

- Whole regions are missing: West and East African towns (compounds, Timbuktu, Kano), South Asian cities (Jaipur's planned grid, Ahmedabad's pols) and Ottoman neighbourhoods. So are the North American small town with its main street and courthouse square, Latin American cities beyond the Laws of the Indies, and hill and port towns as families of their own.
- The numbers are mostly secondary. Block, plot and street figures should be measured from OpenStreetMap for two or three exemplars per type, as `docs/research/stavanger-centre.md` did. Spacematrix values per type are not measured at all.
- Transition rates (how fast a grid fills, how long consolidation takes, how often lots merge in a boom) are unknown beyond Turner's figures for Peru.
- The metropolis needs more: how towers, transit and streaming meet at the city camera; how the history budget works when every block is a tower; the skyline as a layer.
- Nothing here is judged in Unity yet. A first test: three plan types side by side in clay from the far stop (a grid, an organic core, an estate), judged blind. Can a viewer name them?
- Which type a new district gets is a fact for the engine's fabric pass, and the request for it is not yet written.
