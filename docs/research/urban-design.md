# Urban design: the form of towns and how it changes

Research for the town system: how the form of any town is laid down and how it changes, as rules a generator can follow and checks a reviewer can run. It is the theory behind the dated plan (`docs/living-fabric.md`, Big change) and the town plan (`docs/town-plan.md`). Neighbouring pages: the forces that shape towns in `docs/research/city-forces.md`; city types and the pack's plan-type catalogue in `docs/research/city-types.md`; Stavanger since 1945 in `docs/research/town-change.md`. The short versions of the plot cycle and of plans carried out in part are in `docs/research/world-that-remembers.md` and `docs/research/generating-the-past.md`; this page goes deeper and turns them into data.

The classic books' central ideas are stated as they are widely taught. Claims from a summary or abstract are marked (secondary); numbers, terms or dates we could not confirm are marked (unverified). Conzen's and Caniggia's own texts could not be read in this pass, so their terms come through secondary accounts.

## The idea in one paragraph

A town's form is three things changing at three speeds: streets, plots and buildings, with uses faster still. It is laid down in plan units, each the work of one founding act with its own rule, and the seams between units are where a town reads as grown rather than drawn. Each plot runs a cycle on lines that hardly move: built, filled in behind, emptied, cleared, rebuilt. Growth comes in pulses, and at each halt a belt of institutions and open land forms against a barrier. The shape of the street network decides where people walk, and so where shops and wear gather; this can be computed. Plans are dated documents with mechanisms, carried out only as plots change, and their scars are some of the most legible history a town has. Under the street lies a dated stack of pipes, cellars, tunnels and shelters, seen above only by their traces.

## 1. Urban morphology as rules

### Three complexes, three paces

M.R.G. Conzen's *Alnwick, Northumberland* (1960, revised 1969) splits a town's form into three complexes, each changing at its own pace, the slow constraining the fast.

| Complex | Elements | Pace | Our data |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Town plan | streets; plots grouped in blocks; the block plans of buildings | streets over centuries, plots over generations | street graph, plot graph |
| Building fabric | buildings in three dimensions | decades | building diaries |
| Land and building use | what goes on in them | years | the engine's places |

The plan is the most conservative layer (secondary, via Whitehand 2001). A plot boundary outlives the owners on both sides. When plots merge, the old line often survives in the new building: a kept party wall, a break in the roof, a change of window rhythm. When a plot splits, the new line follows the old building's walls. So the generator never redraws plots between years; it merges and splits them as dated events and keeps the old lines as traces.

**The morphological frame.** Each layer is laid inside the lines the older ones left. In English midland towns the reverse-S curve of medieval plough strips passed into later field boundaries and roads, and then into housing estates built on those fields (Godmanchester; secondary). Rule: a new plan unit reads the old lines on its land (walls, lanes, streams, boundaries, a quay's line) and keeps those its founder had no reason to break.

### Plan units and seams

A plan unit is an area homogeneous in streets, plots and buildings because one act laid it out at one time (Conzen 1960; secondary). A plan seam is the line between units of different origin, "dividing genetically different parts of a town plan" (Conzen 1969; secondary). Plan units combine with fabric and use into morphological regions, nesting down to the smallest, the morphotope (secondary). `docs/research/city-types.md` defines the plan type; a plan unit is one dated instance of it. The type says "a fire grid of 60 by 100 m blocks"; the unit says "the Grid on the west shore, laid out by the town after the fires, its straight streets bent where they meet the older lanes".

Seams carry history at the city camera and at 12 m, where the history budget (`docs/living-fabric.md`) needs it. The generator places them where units meet, never as noise:

| Seam | Cause | Read from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A street kinks, narrows or drops in rank entering an older unit | the new straight street had to meet the old lane | city camera |
| Plot grain changes: frontages double or halve, plots turn to face another street | a new founder, a new module | city camera, 12 m |
| Blocks meet at an angle and leave a wedge: a garden, a kiosk, a small square | two orientations meeting | city camera |
| A grid stops in a T against an old lane, or dead-ends at a property line | the founder's land ended | city camera |
| One unit's back lane is the next unit's front street | the later unit faced the earlier one's backs | 12 m, street stop |
| The building line and eaves height step | a later regulation or type | 12 m |

The land makes seams too: a unit's rule stops where the slope beats it and the next rule (stairs, terraces, a quay) takes over, as Herring Hill's contour lanes meet the Basin's quay road by stepped lanes down the gullies.

### The plot cycle

Conzen found that Alnwick's long medieval plots ran a cycle of building coverage (secondary). The phase names are his; the timing is ours to set by type and era.

| Phase | On the plot | Coverage | On screen |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Institutive | the first house at the front; yard and garden behind | low | fronts with open backs |
| Repletive | the back fills: workshops, stores, back houses, lodgers; an alley cut through | rising | a crowded deep plot; a gateway |
| Climax | as full as it gets; the yard a court | highest | packed roofs; light wells; a passage |
| Recessive | back buildings fall or empty | falling | gaps, unroofed sheds, weeds |
| Urban fallow | cleared and waiting | lowest | a gravel car park, a bare gable beside it |
| Redevelopment | a new cycle, often on merged plots | low, then rising | a building of its era, bigger than its neighbours |

Rules:

1. The cycle runs on fixed plot lines; plots merge or split only at a dated event with a cause.
2. Booms merge plots, especially in a centre (secondary; Whitehand on city-centre redevelopment); hard times divide houses and let them in parts (`docs/research/city-forces.md`, filtering).
3. Plots come in a module and simple fractions: Alnwick's frontages near 28 feet and its multiples and fractions (secondary, via `docs/research/city-types.md`); Scottish burgh widths differing by quarters of a unit (Tait; secondary); two-perch (about 10 m) plots in Cricklade (secondary). Each plan unit has a module; every later split halves or quarters it.
4. Coverage is a curve the resolver reads at a year: back houses and sheds are the repletive phase made visible, their loss the recessive phase. Norwegian timber towns filled their back yards the same way (unverified as a measured pattern).
5. The cycle's length is the type's clock: centuries for a medieval burgage, decades for a plot in a booming centre (`docs/research/city-forces.md`, the rent gap).

### Fringe belts and fixation lines

Herbert Louis named fringe belts in a study of Berlin (1936); Conzen developed them and Whitehand explained them (secondary). A fringe belt is a band of large plots, low coverage and institutional or open uses that forms at a town's edge when growth halts, and that the next pulse passes beyond.

- **Why.** Housing outbids other uses for edge land in booms; in slumps it cannot, land is cheap, and uses that need big sites and care less about access take it (Whitehand 1972, via Kristjánsdóttir and Elíasson 2022; secondary). `docs/research/city-forces.md` marks the mechanism unverified; we take it as the working rule.
- **What.** Hospitals, cemeteries, schools, sports grounds, parks and allotments, barracks, gasworks and waterworks, railway yards, villas in big grounds; much of a city's green space (secondary).
- **Where.** Against a fixation line, where the edge stood still: a wall, a river, a railway, a ridge, a shore, or an owner who would not sell. The inner belt lies outside the oldest line; middle and outer belts mark later halts (secondary).
- **After.** Absorbed belts persist because their plots are big and their owners slow. They lose plots piecemeal, gain institutions and are recycled as land values rise (Whitehand and Morton 2003; secondary).

Rule: every halt in the growth script leaves a belt against the fixation line of that moment, and institutions founded during the halt take sites in it. From the city camera the belts read as rings of trees, big plots and low roofs between rings of housing: the growth pulses made visible. The starter town's railway by the Mere, cemetery, hospital, the Mudbowl and Tower Wood's edge are placed in this spirit (`docs/town-plan.md`) and now need dated halts and fixation lines.

### Typology and process: Muratori and Caniggia

The Italian school reads a town through its building types and how they change. Saverio Muratori began it with Venice (1959); Gianfranco Caniggia and Gian Luigi Maffei made it a method (1979; in English *Interpreting Basic Building*, 2001). Conzenians work down from the plan, Caniggians up from the type (secondary).

- **Base and specialised building.** Base building is the ordinary fabric, made from what builders of a time and place took for granted; specialised buildings (churches, halls, markets) are designed on purpose (secondary).
- **The typological process.** A type changes in steps, each built from the last. Within one period it varies by position: a corner house, a house on the main route, a house on a lane (secondary).
- **The row house** (steps and terms unverified). One room on a narrow plot fronting a building route; a second room behind, on the strip that belongs to it (the pertinent strip); storeys added; the yard filled; neighbours fused into one house of flats round a shared stair. This is the burgage cycle seen from inside, and what our diaries already do: extend, add a storey, divide, merge.
- **Routes in a hierarchy** (secondary; terms unverified). A matrix route first, joining two poles; building routes branch off it to give plots; connecting routes join them; restructuring routes are cut later through the fabric, as Haussmann's boulevards were. The strongest crossings become the town's poles.
- **Territory first** (secondary). Routes run first on ridges, dry and with a view; settlement moves down to springs and promontories, then to valley floors once drained (universityheritage.eu). For a harbour town the sea is the first route and the quay the matrix.

Kropf's synthesis nests form in eight levels, from materials and structures through rooms, buildings, plots and blocks to streets and tissue (secondary); our data nests the same way. What we take: the pack defines base types and their process (for the starter town, the one-and-a-half-storey timber house on a slope plot, extended back and up, then divided), variants follow position on the route hierarchy, and specialised buildings stand at nodes and on high ground.

### Kostof and Moudon

Spiro Kostof (*The City Shaped*, 1991; *The City Assembled*, 1992) shows that "organic" and "planned" are not opposites: an organic town is many small planned acts fitted to land and ownership, and a planned grid is soon bent by life; towns change by additions and by subtractions (fire, war, clearance). The generator needs both. Anne Vernez Moudon studies form at several resolutions (plot, block, city, region) through time (1997); her *Built for Change* (1986) followed a San Francisco neighbourhood of houses on standard lots absorbing a century of change without losing its streets (secondary). A good plot module and a flexible type let a district change house by house, as the starter town's old town does.

## 2. Space syntax: the network decides where life gathers

Bill Hillier and Julienne Hanson set out space syntax in *The Social Logic of Space* (1984). "Natural movement" (Hillier et al. 1993) argued that the street grid's configuration is the main generator of pedestrian movement, with integration its strongest correlate; shops then locate to catch the passing trade and multiply it (secondary), which Hillier called the movement economy (1996). Centres form by the same chain, configuration to movement to land use, into a network of centres and sub-centres against a background of housing ("Centrality as a process", 1999; secondary). Most cities show a "deformed wheel" of hub, spokes and rim, and split into a foreground network linking centres and a local background (secondary). Porta and colleagues found retail density in Bologna correlated highly with the network's global betweenness and slightly less with closeness (2009; secondary).

| Measure | What it counts | What it predicts | Radius |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Integration (closeness) | how little turning it takes to reach every other street | destinations; pedestrian density | 400 to 800 m for walking; n for the whole town |
| Choice (betweenness) | how many shortest routes pass through a street | through-movement; retail strings; vehicles at large radius | 800 m, 2,000 m, n |
| Angular segment analysis | depth as the sum of turn angles | pedestrian R² from 0.532 to 0.574 (Turner 2001); better vehicle fits than axial or metric measures (Turner 2007) (secondary) | as above |
| NAIN | (node count)^1.2 ÷ (total depth + 2) | integration comparable across towns and decades (secondary) | as above |
| NACH | log(choice + 1) ÷ log(total depth + 3) (Hillier, Yang and Turner 2012; formula unverified here) | the foreground network at any size | as above |

**Computing it.** Street centre lines split into segments between junctions, with lanes, stairs, quay edges, passages, bridges and ferries as links, each with its dates. Angular shortest paths by Dijkstra, cut at each radius, cost about n² log n: seconds for the heart's few thousand segments, minutes for the town's twenty thousand. Recompute per decade and when a plan changes the network; store NAIN 800 m and NACH 800 m, 2,000 m and n per segment, with their percentiles. Classic syntax ignores slope; a town of stairs needs a climbing cost, or Herring Hill's stepped lanes will read as well connected as the quay road. That departs from the method and must be tested.

**Using it.**

- **A prior, never a fact.** Syntax ranks vacant spaces for the engine's ventures (`docs/living-fabric.md`, ventures take a building) and seeds the feet field, labelled `derived`, until the engine supplies trips (request 16).
- **Frontage follows choice.** High local choice and integration carry active ground floors; the background carries homes and gardens.
- **Corners and nodes.** Where a high-choice segment meets a locally integrated one, corner shops, pubs and kiosks gather; specialised buildings take the strongest nodes.
- **Change.** A bypass or a new bridge moves choice, and trade follows over a decade or two; the street that lost its movement keeps its shopfronts as ghosts.
- **Check.** The town's shops, placed by the engine or the generated past, should correlate with NACH at the pack's radius.

Syntax predicts potential, not use: strong attractors (a school, a ferry, a works gate) and slope bend real flows, and Ratti criticised axial maps for ambiguity and for favouring long straight streets (secondary, via Hillier's 2004 rejoinder). For placing and checking, that is enough.

## 3. What makes a town legible, varied, alive and whole

`docs/research/between-buildings.md` takes Gehl, Whyte, Cullen, Sitte and Lynch for the street layer. At the scale of the town, the theorists give these rules and checks.

| Lynch, *What Time Is This Place?* (1972) | time should be legible; the past kept in present use rather than frozen (secondary) | layers of eras side by side, each still used | the years sheets read as one place changing |

| Gehl, *Life Between Buildings* (1971) and *Close Encounters with Buildings* (2006) | about seven times as many stops before active façades as passive ones (secondary) | frontage grade follows syntax and the unit's plot module; residential streets get soft edges | grade A: over 15 premises and 25 doors and windows per 100 m; B 10 to 15; C 6 to 10; D 3 to 5 (*Urban Design Compendium*; secondary) |
| Alexander, *A City is Not a Tree* (1965) | grown towns are overlapping sets; planned ones are trees. | Alexander, *A Pattern Language* (1977) | 253 patterns, many with numbers | small squares 45 to 60 feet (14 to 18 m) across and never over 70 feet (21 m) the short way; a green within about three minutes' walk, 750 feet (230 m) (secondary) | generated squares and greens measured, with the pack's own numbers where its tradition differs |
| Alexander et al., *A New Theory of Urban Design* (1987) | seven rules (piecemeal growth, larger wholes, visions, positive urban space, large buildings, construction, centres) under one: each increment should help heal the whole | each new unit continues the lines round it unless a fact breaks them | seams have causes |
| Alexander, *The Nature of Order* (2002 to 2005) | fifteen properties of living structure; change by structure-preserving transformations (secondary) | our rule that additions fit or visibly do not (`docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`, principle 8), at town scale: a careful era continues the old lines, a careless era breaks them, and the break is a dated fact | the break reads at 12 m |

Gathered: **legible** means districts that are plan units with one theme; **varied** means variety from founding acts, cycles and keepers, never from random choice inside a unit; **alive** means frontage that follows syntax and belts that are crossed; **whole** means each change keeps the lines round it and the units share places at their seams.

## 4. Planning as a process with actors

### Who acts

A town's form sums many actors' decisions, each on its own horizon (`docs/research/city-forces.md`, Who owns land). In Birmingham Whitehand found local government, families, religious bodies, societies and firms at work; in housing areas landowners, developers and builders dominated, and between the wars one actor often controlled streets, plots and buildings at once (*The Making of the Urban Landscape*, 1992; secondary). One actor holding all three makes a uniform unit; many make a varied one.

| Actor | Decides | Leaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The state | railways, highways, harbours, defences, housing finance, law | the line through town; standard house types; bunkers; the rules every unit is built under |
| The council | street plans, building lines, permits, paving, lighting, clearance, conservation | widened ends, stepped fronts, paving campaigns, cleared blocks, protected quarters |
| Landowners | when and how to sell, covenants | unit edges; fixation lines where they would not sell |
| Developers and builders | layouts inside a unit; a type repeated | uniform rows; a seam at their land's edge |
| Institutions | sites for church, school, hospital, cemetery | fringe-belt plots; landmarks |
| Utilities | where and when to dig | trenches, covers, poles, substations, each on its own schedule |
| Preservation bodies | what is listed, protected, restored | kept fabric; modernisations reversed |
| Owners and citizens | thousands of small acts; protest | the plot cycle; plans stopped |

### Plans as dated documents

A plan is a document with a date, an author and claims on geometry: a building line, a widened street, a cleared block, a new road, a protected area. Its states are drafted, adopted, in force, carried out in part, lapsed, replaced. What reaches the ground depends on its mechanism, and each mechanism leaves its own scar.

| Mechanism | How it reaches the ground | Scar | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| On rebuild | a building may only be rebuilt behind the new line | stepped fronts; a street widening house by house | Paris's alignment plans: in 1819 the prefect Chabrol reckoned they would take centuries (secondary). On Japanese roads under 4 m wide a rebuilt house stands 2 m back from the centre line (secondary) |
| By purchase or expropriation | the council buys or takes land within a window | a widened end where money ran out; a lone block | Stavanger's clearances, two of four done (`docs/research/town-change.md`) |
| By catastrophe | a fire clears the ground and the replan is built at once | a regulated grid beside old lanes | `docs/research/generating-the-past.md` |
| By one actor | an estate or company builds the unit, often only in part | streets laid out with no houses | `docs/research/city-forces.md`, busts |
| By stopping | a protest, a bust or a new law halts it | old houses on the line of a road never built | Stavanger's harbour ring, dropped 1984 (`docs/research/town-change.md`) |

Scars to add to the catalogue in `docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`: a single house set back in a straight front, the one rebuild under an alignment; a blind gable facing a road reserve; a green strip that is a reserve kept for decades; a ramp ending in the air; underpass stair heads paved over; a "temporary" car park on a cleared block, thirty years old; block corners cut back for a junction never widened.

### Rules shaped the fabric

A building regulation is a rule about form, so every building carries the rules of its year.

| Year and place | Rule | Form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1709 and 1774, London | window frames set back in reveals (1709); building rates with set wall thickness and party walls (1774) (secondary) | recessed sashes; terraces in a few ranks |
| 1853, Berlin | courtyards at least 5.34 m square so a fire engine could turn (5.30 m in one source), raised to 60 m² in 1887; the 1862 Hobrecht plan fixed only streets and blocks (secondary) | deep blocks round chains of tiny courts |
| 1896 and 1904, Norway | brick fire gables in towns; after Ålesund burned, masonry in every town (murtvang) | brick gables between timber houses; masonry centres, timber edges |
| Today, Germany (BauGB §34) | with no binding plan, a building must fit its surroundings in use, density, construction and plot coverage | continuity of whatever pattern is there |
| Today, Japan | roads at least 4 m; set back on rebuild (secondary) | lanes that widen one house at a time |

New York's tenement acts and 1916 and 1961 zoning are in `docs/research/city-types.md`. The rule for us: each pack holds a dated sequence of regimes, each with a few form rules (height to width, coverage, courtyard, fire distance, building line, setback, parking), and a building or unit takes the regime of its year. A rule's exemptions and edges are where its scars are: the poor built just outside the masonry rule's boundary.

## 5. The vertical and underground town

The underground is a dated stack: each layer has an owner, a route, a depth, a year and a surface trace. Stavanger's mains, shelters, rock car parks and tunnels are in `docs/research/town-change.md`; here is the general form.

| Layer | Depth | When | Surface trace |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cellars and pavement vaults | 2 to 3 m | any era; London's coal vaults mostly Georgian and Victorian (secondary) | light wells, cellar steps; foundry-named coal-hole covers; prism pavement lights (Hayward Brothers; secondary) |
| Telecoms | about 0.25 to 0.35 m under a footway (UK; secondary) | 1880s on | small covers, cabinets |
| Electricity, low voltage | about 0.45 m (secondary) | 1890s on; on poles first | link boxes, substations; poles until buried |
| Gas | about 0.6 m (secondary) | 1840s on | valve covers; the gasholder |
| Water | 0.75 to 0.9 m in Britain; 1.25 to 2 m in Norway for frost (Haugesund, Steinkjer; secondary) | 1860s on | hydrants, valve covers |
| Sewers and drains | deepest: they fall by gravity | 1850s to 1900s on | manholes, gullies, outfalls |
| Utility tunnels | walkable | Paris's sewers carried water pipes, about 600 km by 1878; Japan's Common Utility Tunnel Act, 1963; Helsinki's bedrock tunnels from the 1960s (secondary) | hatches, vents |
| Metro and rail | cut and cover, or bored deep | London's first underground line, 1863 | stair heads, portals, vent shafts |
| Road tunnels | through hills, under water | the car era | dated portals, vents |
| Car parks | one to six levels, under ground or in rock | lifts from 1901, ramps from 1918 to the 1920s, a post-war boom (secondary) | ramps, barriers, lift heads, vents |
| Underpasses | just under the road | the 1960s and 70s | stair heads, ramps, murals; new paving where filled |
| Shelters | basements; rock halls in centres | Norway's duty from 1948; Helsinki requires them in buildings of 600 m² and over (secondary) | steel doors, vent stacks, signs |

**The order is a rule.** Shallow services lie under the footway, deep ones under the carriageway, and the sewer is deepest because it must fall to the outfall. A cold pack lays its water deeper, in harder, often blasted trenches. Each utility digs on its own schedule, so the paving records which body dug when.

**Levels and their reversal.** Buchanan's *Traffic in Towns* (1963) proposed separating people from traffic, in environmental areas or with pedestrians on decks over the cars (secondary). The full programme was never built, but its influence gave British centres precincts, decks, underpasses and big car parks (secondary). Montreal's underground city began with Place Ville-Marie in 1962 and grew with the metro from 1966 (secondary). The reversal came from the 1990s: Birmingham removed a first ring-road underpass in 1993, demolished the raised Masshouse junction in 2002 and brought St Chad's crossing back to street level in 2008 (secondary); Stavanger filled Torget's underpasses after 1989. Grade separation is a dated era with a dated removal, and a filled underpass leaves newer paving where its stairs were, a stub of wall, a kerb that changes.

**Where it meets the street.** The generator places each interface by its layer's route and era.

| Interface | Belongs to | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stair head and canopy | metro, underpass | high-choice segments near nodes |
| Portal with a date stone | rail and road tunnels | where a route meets a hill |
| Ramp | car parks | secondary streets, behind the main frontage |
| Vent grille, tower or box | tunnels, car parks, shelters | along the line below, at intervals |
| Covers by maker and era | every utility | along mains and at each connection |
| Steel door or hatch | shelters, emergency exits | quiet places, a rock face |
| Light wells, coal holes, pavement lights | cellars, vaults | along old frontages |

## 6. Growing a town for a game

`docs/research/generating-the-past.md` gives the pipeline and `docs/research/city-forces.md` the economic steps. The form side of the same pass: the land and what was on it (the frame); the first routes (the sea and the quay, ridge and coast paths, the country road at a horse's grade); a seed unit where land meets sea; then pulses that found plan units of the kinds the era allows, each reading the old lines on its land, taking its module and regime and leaving seams; halts that form fringe belts; plot cycles run yearly; syntax recomputed at each network change; plans carried out by mechanism; utilities laid along the streets of their years; checks, then accept or rerun.

**What reads as real:** irregularity with reasons (terrain, an older line, a boundary, a plan stopped), never noise; seams at the scale the camera sees; repetition at the right scale (a row of identical workers' houses is true, because one builder built it, and variety comes later from keepers); a few strong streets over a quiet background; layers of eras side by side, with a few anomalies that each have a cause.

**What goes wrong.** `docs/research/city-forces.md` covers city builders' economies. The failures of form:

| Tool or game | What it does | What goes wrong | What we take |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Parish and Müller (2001); CityEngine | roads grown from water and density maps under global goals and local constraints; lots split; buildings by grammar (secondary) | a city of one moment, founded at once; one rule over an area; no persistent plots | road growth inside one unit; split grammars |
| SimCity; Cities: Skylines | zoning on 8 m cells up to four deep (32 m); buildings level up by land value and education and are replaced (Paradox wiki) | no plot memory; square cells leave bare spots on curves (players; secondary); the game added "historical" buildings that keep their look, a sign players wanted persistence | agents and fields, not the cell grid |
| Townscaper | an irregular quad grid; wave function collapse picks modules by adjacency; decoration after structure (Game Developer) | texture with no function, owner or date; it may "fail silently" | the decorate-after pass; irregular outlines inside a unit |
| Wave function collapse | fills a grid so neighbours agree | no global structure without added constraints (Boris the Brave; secondary) | detail inside a decided structure, never layout |
| Watabou's city generator | Voronoi wards, walls, straight-skeleton lots; its author calls the method rather arbitrary, aimed at good-looking maps | towns radial round a plaza; wards without dates | straight-skeleton lot splitting |
| Manor Lords | burgage plots along roads, houses scaled to the plot, extensions behind (secondary) | identical rows (players; secondary); no cycle | plots with back extensions as the repletive phase; add the cycle and a diary per plot |

Each generates a moment, not a history. The cure is the same each time: founding acts with dates, cycles on persistent plots, and plans and halts that leave their marks.

## What the town system should take

City types give the pack's plan types; city forces give plots, owners and the economy. These records complete the form.

### Data

| Record | Fields |
| --- | --- |
| Plan unit | id; plan type; founded (year); founder and cause; the land taken and the old lines kept; module (frontage and its fractions, depth, orientation); regime at founding; seams (with which unit, of which kind); fate by year (kept, renewed in part, cleared) |
| Plot (adds to city forces' plot) | plan unit; frontage segment; coverage by year; cycle phase by year; merges and splits with year and cause, the old lines kept as traces |
| Fringe belt | the halt that formed it (years); fixation line (wall, water, rail, ridge, an owner's boundary); its plots and institutions; absorbed (year); plots lost or gained, with years |
| Street segment | geometry in integers; laid, widened, closed, pedestrianised (years); rank; route role (matrix, building, connecting, restructuring); syntax by decade (NAIN 800 m; NACH 800 m, 2,000 m, n; percentiles) |
| Plan document | author; adopted (year); kind (alignment, regulation plan, renewal, road plan, conservation); claims as geometry; mechanism (on rebuild, purchase or expropriation within a window, at once, by one actor); lapse or replacement year; for each claim, the year carried out or never |
| Regime | years in force; where; form rules (height to width, coverage, courtyard, fire distance, building line, setback, parking, materials); exemptions |
| Underground layer | kind; owner; route (segments, offset from the kerb, depth band); laid, renewed, abandoned, filled (years); interfaces (covers by era, hydrants, stair heads, portals, vents, ramps, doors) |

Each is a fact with a date and a cause, written with provenance `generated-past` (or `derived` where a rule chose it) until the engine's fabric pass owns it. Values computed at read time, such as syntax and coverage curves, are never facts.

### Rules

1. **Plan units with dates and seams.** Every part of the town belongs to one unit, laid by one founding act with its own module and rules. Irregularity lives at seams and in the land, never as noise inside a unit.
2. **Three paces on persistent lines.** Streets change slowest, then plots, buildings and uses. Plots run their cycle on lines that move only at a dated merge or split, and the old lines stay visible.
3. **Fringe belts at halts.** Each halt leaves a belt of big plots and institutions against the fixation line of the moment; growth passes beyond, and the belt persists, crossed by paths.
4. **The network decides where life gathers.** Angular integration and choice at walking and town radii, per decade and per network change, rank active frontage and vacant space and check that shops sit where the streets say. A prior and a check, never a fact.
5. **Plans are documents with mechanisms.** Claims reach the ground only as their mechanism allows and stop when a fact stops them; each unfinished claim leaves its scar. Buildings take the regime of their year.
6. **The underground is a dated stack** in a fixed order of depth, seen above by its interfaces and by each owner's trenches.

### Checks

- Every plot has frontage and every door a way (`reach_check.py`); every unit touches a shared node; no unit hangs by one street unless its type is a cul-de-sac estate.
- Every seam has a kind and a cause; seams per kilometre of unit boundary are measured.
- A fringe belt for every halt in the script.
- Frontage grade against NACH percentile within the pack's ranges; shops correlate with choice.
- A cold reader names Lynch's five from the city camera.
- The mix of building ages per street within the type's range.
- Every unfinished plan claim has its scar; every filled underpass and closed line its trace.
- A foreign pack's town grown by the same steps (`docs/foundations.md`).

### For Øystein to decide

1. **Plan units as the top layer of the town's data,** and when to rebuild the heart's clay (`blender/blockouts/heart_layout.py`) as dated units with seams: before more clay, or after the corner.
2. **Syntax as Overstory's `derived` prior** for the feet field and vacancy ranking until the engine supplies trips (request 16), with a climbing cost for stairs that departs from standard space syntax. The alternative is to ask the engine to own it as part of the network's facts.
3. **Who owns plans and regimes:** the pack's script before the engine's start year; the engine's council and developers after it (requests 14 and 21).
4. **How deep the underground goes first:** traces only (covers, trench strips, stair heads, portals, vents), or one walkable place below (a shelter in Watch Knoll, a cellar, the tunnel under the Bluff).

## Gaps

- **Originals unread.** Conzen, Caniggia and Maffei, Whitehand and Hillier's 1993 and 2012 papers came through abstracts and summaries. Caniggia's route terms and the row house's steps are least certain.
- **No rates.** How long a plot cycle runs by type and era, how often plots merge in a boom, how wide a fringe belt grows: no Nordic numbers found.
- **Syntax on steep ground.** No study of towns of stairs and terraces, and no pedestrian counts for a small Nordic town. A first test: the heart's syntax with and without a climbing cost against where Stavanger's shops stand on OpenStreetMap.
- **Norwegian plan mechanics.** Whether a regulation plan's right to expropriate lapsed after a set time under the 1965, 1985 and 2008 acts, and how building lines applied on rebuild. This decides how plans freeze half done.
- **Smaller doubts.** Gehl's own A to E frontage scale; the lateral order of services under a footway; game critiques that come mostly from players' forums; East Asian systems only sketched.
