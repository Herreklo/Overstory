# Park and garden trees: Stavanger around 1989

Research for the tree system (`docs/trees.md`) and the town plan (`docs/town-plan.md`), October 2026: the trees of the old gardens, the parks and the town's planted woods, after Øystein pointed at Eiganes and Ledaal's old beeches and ashes, Vålandskogen and Sørmarka. Claims not checked against a primary source are marked (unverified); counts marked as ours were made from the map labels in the municipality's 2014 plan for the historic gardens.

## The bottom line

- **The old gardens are beech gardens.** In 2013 the municipality mapped every plant at Ledaal, Breidablikk and Munkehagen ([plan, 2014](https://www.stavanger.kommune.no/siteassets/samfunnsutvikling/planer/temaplaner/natur-og-fritid/de-historiske-hagene-plan.pdf)). Beech is about a third of the labels; beech, lime, ash, elm and holly together about two thirds. Sycamore, though among the commonest town trees ([NIBIO 2019](https://hdl.handle.net/11250/2619926)), is only about 2% of the old gardens.
- **They came in waves, so they age together.** Ledaal's garden was laid out in 1773, a beech grove followed early in the 1800s, a landscape conversion in 1863 and a restoration about 1930; Breidablikk dates from 1881, Munkehagen from 1897; many trees "were planted at the same time" (plan, pp. 11, 22, 34). In 1989 they are about 90, 108, 126 and up to 186 years old.
- **Vålandskogen is a planted beech wood, about 90 years old in 1989:** even-aged beech planted about 1900, some sycamore and groups of big conifers, and a lower wood of birch, pine, rowan and goat willow on the stony south (NIBIO 2019, p. 16). The water tower of 1895 stood on an open, treeless top.
- **Sørmarka is a different kind of forest:** started by Stavanger Aftenblad in 1908, school classes planting from 1910, about 20,000 seedlings a year from 1930 until the mid-1950s; mostly spruce and pine, with beech, larch and Sitka ([no.wikipedia](https://no.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8rmarka_(Stavanger))). In 1989 a conifer plantation 35 to 80 years old, with pasture and boulders between its stands.
- **The town's trees were healthy in 1989:** elm disease reached western Norway only in 2023, ash dieback Norway about 2006 to 2008, the horse chestnut leaf miner 2013.

## The places

**Ledaal (the manor on Eiganes).** Consul Jacob Kielland bought the plot in 1773 and laid out a park with box hedges, beds, a kitchen garden and trees; his son rebuilt the house by 1803. The baroque garden had parterres, a beech grove and stone walls; in 1863 the gardener Poul Holst Poulsson turned it into the English landscape style; the family gave it to the town in 1917; Arno Berg restored it about 1930, partly to the baroque axis, keeping the big trees (plan, p. 11). The lower garden is "mainly big trees, with beech and lime in the majority"; the upper garden is edged with elm, beech, lime, hazel, wild cherry and field maple; inside the clipped privet by the main axis stand a large copper beech and a large yew; an elm avenue runs diagonally from the courtyard to the road; a single ash with a thin crown in the courtyard (pp. 12 to 17). Our count of its labels (196): beech 54, copper beech 3, wych elm 39, field elm 4, lime 24, ash 16, holly 13, goat willow 7, horse chestnut 6, yew 4, sycamore 3, oak 4, hazel 3, birch 3, others. A photograph of the 1950s shows a massive, low-limbed old beech before the house. The big trees close over the paths ("the trees form a roof"); lawns mown, hedges clipped.

**The Eiganes villa gardens.** Eiganes was the town's cow pasture until 1771, then split into walled plots on stony heath and sold to the town's upper class (plan, p. 9). *Breidablikk* (1881): Poulsson's landscape garden with exotic species, big trees and rhododendrons to the north, a monkey puzzle, a fern-leaved oak and magnolia to the south; paths almost grown over with moss, too dark under the crowns to plant, an unmown meadow under big trees cut once or twice a year; taken over by Stavanger Museum in 1989 (pp. 22 to 29). Our count (277): beech about 89, ash 30, lime about 22, holly 18, birch 18, horse chestnut 11, sycamore 7. *Munkehagen* (1897): rows of big trees along the road and the edges with dense moss under them, a beech avenue, a half circle of clipped limes round an old fountain; the empty villa burned in 1979, so in 1989 a lawn lies where the house stood (pp. 34 to 40). Our count (131): beech 47, lime 21, ash 13, birch 11, holly 6, horse chestnut 5, with a monkey puzzle, an Atlas cedar, cypresses and a walnut. Poulsson ran his own nursery and drew about 80 gardens in Norway.

**Byparken, the lake and the cathedral.** The park was part of the Kongsgård property until the 1960s, its part nearest the cathedral a cemetery; the music pavilion dates from 1921, the fountain from 1924. No tree inventory or planting dates were found. A photograph of 1900 to 1930 shows young standard trees along the shore path behind an iron railing; one of 1950s shows newly planted, staked standards. The story that Byparken's beeches came from a shipwreck has no source (dropped from `docs/research/vegetation.md`'s use). On 24 October 2015 the lake's trees were still in full leaf, yellow, orange and green.

**Vålandskogen.** A west-facing slope on thin moraine over phyllite, site quality high; even-aged beech planted about 1900 (by whom, and from what seed, unknown; probably continental or English nursery stock); no field layer under the beech, abrupt edges; aerial photographs show trees on parts of the hill by 1937 and canopy over most of it from 1960; managed as a park forest with a high canopy and good sightlines (NIBIO 2019, pp. 15 to 17). The tower (1895) gave the lower town water pressure from Mosvatnet and served as a fire lookout until 1931.

**Sørmarka.** Heath and farmland at the end of the 1800s; planted from 1910 with Rogaland Skogselskap; the planting company founded 1919; the name proposed in 1933; planting stopped in the mid-1950s (resumed 2000); about 2,300 daa, 1,500 of them forest. Species: spruce and pine, beech, larch, Sitka, shore pine, with oak, birch, holly, maple, hazel, rowan and aspen.

**Others.** *Mosvatnet*: in the country among bog and heath 150 years ago, the town's water 1860 to 1930; motorway fill of 1968 to 1973 took an islet and a wetland; its islet overgrown from the late 1980s; now a park with beech and broadleaf wood and shore woodland. *Bjergsted*: a festival ground from 1890, its present form from the 1925 exhibition, the conservatory 1979 and the concert hall 1980; proud old trees, a venerable cedar, the remains of an old tree called the troll. *Lagård gravlund* (1834, extended 1868 and 1882): a tight frame cemetery of back-to-back graves, iron railings and gravel; its trees not found. *Eiganes gravlund* (1910). *Byhaugen*: a hill with a walled house of the 1840s and woods its owner planted (dates unverified).

## Growth data for open-grown broadleaves

Trunk diameter by age after White's two phases ([Forestry Commission, 1998](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/1998/01/fcin012.pdf)): a core phase of constant ring width, then a constant area of wood a year, so the rings thin as the trunk grows; about 0.5 mm when the crown declines. Our reading of his tables for an average garden or parkland site, centimetres at breast height:

| | 20 y | 50 y | 80 y | 100 y | 150 y | 200 y |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Beech (core 70 y, 4 mm) | 16 | 40 | 63 | 76 | 101 | 121 |
| Ash (70, 4) | 16 | 40 | 63 | 76 | 101 | 121 |
| Sycamore (60, 6) | 24 | 60 | 93 | 110 | 144 | 171 |
| Lime (70, 5) | 20 | 50 | 79 | 95 | 127 | 152 |
| Horse chestnut (40, 5) | 20 | 49 | 69 | 80 | 101 | 119 |
| Oak (100, 3.5) | 14 | 35 | 56 | 70 | 99 | 121 |

On poor or exposed ground a beech is about 73 cm at 100 years; in a wood about 60. A Norwegian check: the 200-year-old Ringve beech in Trondheim, about 138 cm. Crown diameter of open-grown trees grows roughly with the trunk's ([Hemery](https://gabrielhemery.com/how-big-will-this-tree-grow)): at 0.5 m of trunk, beech about 8.8 m, ash 10.2, sycamore 10.0, oak 9.6; so a park beech is about 13 m across at 100 years. Heights, realistic not record: British beech about 15 m at 40 years where exposure is moderate, mature 23 to 24 m exposed against 30 to 32 m sheltered ([FC 1953](https://cdn.forestresearch.gov.uk/1953/04/fcbu020.pdf)); Copenhagen limes about 7 m at 20 years and 20 m at 100. Working ranges for Stavanger gardens at 100 to 150 years (estimate): beech 22 to 28 m, lime, ash and sycamore 20 to 25, horse chestnut 15 to 20, oak 15 to 22; about a quarter less on exposed knolls. Life spans (Woodland Trust): beech about 225 years (ancient from 200), ash about 200, lime 300 to 400, oak about 600, horse chestnut up to 300, sycamore about 400. Signs of age: hollow trunks, dead wood in the crown, bark loss, fungi; trees grown in woods, avenues or as pollards stay thinner than open-grown ones.

## What it suggests

These are inputs, to be tested before they become design:

- **The Heights (the villas):** a manor park with a beech grove of about 1800, a landscape conversion of 1863 and a restoration of about 1930, so the trees stand in even-aged cohorts; an elm avenue; one copper beech and one yew as its centrepieces; stone walls on the plot lines of 1771 with rows of trees along them; villa gardens of 1881 to 1900 by one gardener and nurseryman (a person for the engine), each with an exotic specimen; dated facts in 1989 such as a villa burned in 1979 leaving a lawn, a garden passing to the museum, moss on paths nobody walks.
- **Tower Wood:** Stavanger is evidence for a beech park wood planted about 1900 beside a water tower of 1895 on a bare hill: high crowns, nothing growing under the beech, groups of conifers, sycamore seeding in, natural birch, pine, rowan and willow on the stony part. The town plan has a mixed wood of birch, spruce and pine on an old pasture there; worth revising with Øystein.
- **A second, planted town forest at the edge:** started by a newspaper and the schoolchildren, planted 1910 to 1955, conifer-dominated, with pasture and boulders between its stands.
- **For the engine:** planting events with who planted (a family, a gardener, the municipality, school classes); tending (clipped lime arbours, crown lifting, mowing under trees, moss); cohorts, a garden whose trees age together being itself a dated consequence; diseases as dated spreading fronts in the pack, none present in 1989.
- **For the tree system:** White's two phases fit the pipe model and can calibrate trunks by species and site; crown width from trunk diameter; heights capped by exposure.
