# What owners did to their houses, 1900 to 1989

What owners in Norway's coastal towns did to their houses in the twentieth century. It is evidence for the diary's event vocabulary and the era shelves (`blender/resolver/eras.py`); see `docs/building-system.md` for the diary itself. The sources are best for Stavanger, where the city conservator (byantikvaren) has written down what was lost and why. Bergen adds roofs; Ålesund, Haugesund and Kristiansand are thin. Claims taken from search summaries rather than the source itself are marked (secondary). Claims no source confirmed are marked (unverified).

## The story in short

Houses were built rich between about 1865 and 1925: carved trim, spires on the gables, many-paned windows, slate and tile. After the war many owners found them old-fashioned, impractical and cold, and modernised them for decades (Heng 2015). In Stavanger, funkis windows came first, from the 1930s to the 50s. Eternit sheets followed in the 50s and 60s, easy-to-wash *husmorvinduer* and concrete roof tiles in the 60s and 70s, and plastic cladding in the 80s (Heng & Windsholt 2020; SNL). Oil, found in 1969, paid for much of it. Each change took away what told one style, and one house, from the next. Reversal began with Gamle Stavanger, saved in 1956 or 1957 (sources differ). It reached the rest of the wooden town from the 1980s, and was often done wrong.

## Decade by decade

**1900 to 1929: build and paint.** Swiss-chalet houses (to about 1910) had double-rabbet beaded boards (*dobbeltfalset staffpanel*), cross-mullion windows, slate or flat tile, two or more chimneys and glazed verandas. Swiss-Nouveau houses (about 1905 to 1920) added small winter gardens, small panes or an arch at the top of the windows, and a broken gable (Brochure 2021). The carved boards under the windows went out of use as jugend came in (Heng & Windsholt 2020). Paint was linseed oil. From the late 1800s to about 1950, painters primed bare wood with oil and ochre (*okre huset*), which later made coats crack, flake and blister (Brænne 2017). Chalet houses were first painted in ochres that imitated new wood, with structure and windows picked out in brown. All-white houses came only with neoclassicism in the 1900s, with ochre, brown, grey, blue or green trim (Westman). After Ålesund burned in 1904, the national masonry rule put brick houses inside Stavanger's wooden town; they keep its plots and styles (Brochure 2021). Around 1900 up to half of Stavanger owned its house and the other half rented from them, in the horizontally divided two-family house (Heng & Windsholt 2020).

**1930 to 1945: funkis windows.** Funkis windows (two or three lights, no glazing bars) went into older houses from the 1930s to the 1950s (Heng & Windsholt 2020). New funkis houses had flat tiles or roofing felt, and mitred corners with no corner boards. Their colours were stronger: English red and ochre around 1930, then grey-greens, pale yellows and light blues in the 40s and 50s (Westman). In the old harbour streets of the 1930s and 40s, houses were draughty, with privies in the yards, sickness and poverty (Stavangeren 1/2016).

**1946 to 1959: clearance, Eternit, concrete tiles.** Stavanger's 1946 centre plan (adopted 1948) was nicknamed "tear down and build". It would have kept only the cathedral and the Valberg tower. Clearance was carried out at Klubbgata (1948 to 1960) and Olavskleiva (1952 to 1962); two other areas never were (Stavangeren 1/2016). City architect Einar Hedén's plan to keep part of the old cottages passed by one vote. Sources give 1956 or 1957, and 35 or 54 houses (Lokalhistoriewiki; Stavangeren 1/2016). It kept the least wanted part, the run-down cottages west of Vågen (Wikipedia). It had no legal force: the houses were zoned as open space until a legal basis came in 1965 (Stavangeren 1/2016). Building materials were short and the tile works could not keep up, so concrete roof tiles came in (Bergen, secondary). Many houses were clad in Norsk Eternit's flat facade sheets, in several colours, in the 1950s and 60s (SNL). A Swedish coastal museum says the sheets needed less upkeep than wood and carried some status (secondary). Mineral wool became usual only in the late 1950s (Fortidsminneforeningen). Sealed insulating glass came in the mid-1950s (secondary).

**1960 to 1969: husmorvinduer.** This is the decade of husmorvinduer and concrete tiles (Heng & Windsholt 2020). A husmorvindu was a plain, easy-to-wash window (Heng 2015). One often replaced two old windows, and openings were usually made smaller (Brochure 2021). The surrounds went with them: replacing windows and their trim was among the commonest changes, so most 1860 to 1915 surrounds are gone (Heng 2015). Spires went too. Stavanger once had hundreds; today mostly stumps remain under the ridges (Heng & Windsholt 2020; the decade they went is unverified). From about 1965 houses were painted only in alkyd (Brænne 2017). Plastic gutters came into common use (Bergen roofs). Insulating-glass windows were made in Norway from the mid-1960s (secondary). Television opened in 1960 and 40% had it by 1964 (SSB, secondary); roof aerials followed (unverified). Colours turned to nature: moss greens and similar (Westman).

**1970 to 1979: oil money.** Oil was found at Ekofisk in November 1969, and in the 1970s Stavanger became the industry's centre (Stavangeren 1/2016). The oil brought the money to "upgrade" old houses. So many were rebuilt that the old town now shows only in glimpses (Heng 2015). Brown, tar-like colours (Westman). Household oil use fell from the early 1970s as electric heat rose (secondary). Gamle Stavanger grew to 79 houses in 1965 and 154 in 1975 (Lokalhistoriewiki). Hedén became Stavanger's first byantikvar in 1974 (Stavangeren 1/2016). In 1975, European Architectural Heritage Year, Gamle Stavanger was one of Norway's three pilot projects (SNL, secondary). In 1974 Bergen dropped plans to raze Marken and much of Nordnes (secondary). Norsk Eternit closed in 1978 (SNL).

**1980 to 1989: plastic and the turn.** This is the decade of plastic cladding (Heng & Windsholt 2020). Husmorvinduer and concrete tiles still went on: Tidegeilen 9, a classicist house of 1865, got both in the 1980s (Brochure 2021). Asbestos was banned in 1985 (SNL). Colours swung to off-whites and pastels (Westman). Windows got stuck-on glazing bars (unverified, one forum post). From the 1980s Stavanger worked against the trend (Heng & Windsholt 2020).

## How restoration reverses it

- **It needs evidence.** Old photos and drawings help, and so do paint traces that show an old window's size and trim. An original attic window is often left high in the gable, and corner boards sometimes survive (Heng 2015; Brochure 2021). At Tidegeilen 9 the original colour came from scraps of the original cladding.
- **What it does.** Openings go back to their first size. Doors are copied. Red single-curved tiles are relaid, sometimes old ones, with zinc gutters (Brochure 2021).
- **It is often wrong.** Owners buy "old style" parts: mass-produced brackets and Swiss-style surrounds go on every kind of window. The errors spread as neighbours copy each other (Heng & Windsholt 2020). One neoclassical house got Swiss-style windows before the right ones went in (Brochure 2021).
- **Two owners, two windows.** In a two-family house each floor's owner may restore on their own, so a house can show two window types for years (Brochure 2021).
- **Gamle Stavanger's white may be a convention.** Its paint layers show earlier earth tones, greens and ochre. White spread in the 1900s, and the 1950s protection fixed an idealised white (NTNU 2025, secondary). In the classical era, only the main front was often painted light; backs and outhouses were cheaper earth reds and ochres (Westman).

## Masonry

From 1904 the masonry rule gave Stavanger brick houses that follow the wooden town (Brochure 2021). Ålesund was rebuilt in brick and stone from 1904 to 1907 (secondary). Little was found on what owners did to them. In Oslo, ornamented render fronts were often re-rendered plainer while rules were weak (secondary). One Oslo front was repaired in 1987 with cement and plastic paint over lime render (secondary). Patched render, painted brick and sandblasting are all unconfirmed for Stavanger (unverified).

## What shows

- From the street, a few metres off: window type and glazing bars; a window smaller than its surround, or the ghost of a bigger one; trim rich or stripped; the door. Also board width and profile, sheet joints (Eternit) or a plastic sheen; paint colour and how it fails; zinc or plastic gutters; picket fence, hedge or parking.
- From above (the city camera): roof material and colour (curved red tile, slate, flat concrete tile); chimneys present or gone; spires at the gable tips; dormers; eave depth. Also verandas and extensions in plan, aerials and a paved garden. Back walls show, so a cheaper back colour reads. Window changes show only in the gables, where the attic window often survived.
- Invisible: loft insulation, inner storm sashes, the paint type itself (only how it fails), rent and ownership (except through split windows), asbestos.

For the era shelves:
- Add a neoclassical shelf (about 1920 to 1940). It has symmetric fronts, small-paned or empire-style windows (later three-light), flat or glazed tiles, and hipped or steep roofs. Walls are white or grey-white with coloured trim (Brochure 2021; Westman).
- Make the husmorvindu the replacement window of 1955 to 1989 on old houses. The large living-room window belongs to new houses of 1945 to 1975 (Brochure 2021). The Swedish *perspektivfönster* is not confirmed for Norwegian replacements (secondary).
- Add concrete tile to the roofs of the post-war, seventies and eighties shelves. Add Eternit sheet (1950 to 1978) and plastic (1975 to 1989) as recladding.
- Original chalet coats lean to ochre with brown trim, and classical coats to grey, ochre and earth colours. White comes as a later repaint, especially after 1956 in protected streets.
- The seventies' browns and the eighties' pastels match what `eras.py` already holds.

## Gaps

- No dated source for when spires, eaves, chimneys and doors went, or for shopfronts. Shops did sit in old houses: 13 of 29 addresses in Øvre Holmegate are businesses (Lokalhistoriewiki).
- Nothing on dormers and lofts added, garages, or glazing verandas in.
- Ålesund, Haugesund and Kristiansand (Posebyen) have almost nothing. The Sognnæs 2015 essay on Gamle Stavanger, Posebyen and Lærdalsøyri is the next read; its PDF was too large to fetch.
- No source gave a repaint cycle. Brænne advises waiting until the paint is clearly worn; ten to fifteen years for oil paint is a working guess (unverified).
- The NTNU thesis on Gamle Stavanger's colours (Sola 2025) is in Nasjonalt vitenarkiv and worth reading in full.
