# City forces

Research for the town system: the forces that put towns where they are, and make them grow, sort, decline and change. It is theory for every setting pack, not one town. Its neighbours cover the form of cities (`docs/research/urban-design.md`), city types across the world (`docs/research/city-types.md`) and Stavanger's change since 1945 (`docs/research/town-change.md`). This page covers what lies under the form. It feeds the living fabric (`docs/living-fabric.md`), the town plan (`docs/town-plan.md`) and the generated past (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`). Claims taken from summaries are marked (secondary); claims we could not confirm are marked (unverified).

## The idea in one paragraph

A town is the sediment of many decisions by many owners. Each was made with the money, knowledge and horizon its owner had, under that year's transport and rules. Five forces do most of the work. Access: everyone wants to be near what they need, and the era's transport decides what "near" means. Clustering and repulsion: like uses gain from each other and push away what harms them, and that makes quarters. Durable capital: buildings and streets outlast the reasons they were built, so each era's layer stays until someone pays to remove it. Keepers: who owns a thing, with what means and what horizon, decides whether it is kept or let go. Cycles and succession: money comes in waves, and buildings pass down to poorer users as they age. They keep passing down until what a place could earn is far enough above what it earns that someone reinvests. Get these five right and the town reads as lived in. Optimise any of them from above and it reads as a player's plan.

## 1. Land and location

### Site and situation

Towns sit where something has to stop. Cooley (1894) put cities at breaks in transport, where goods are unloaded, stored and sent on by another mode (secondary). The classic break is where sea meets land route. The harbour town is the purest case: the quay is the break, so merchants, stores, agents and banks crowd it. Weber (1909) placed industry (secondary). A works that loses weight in processing sits near its raw material: the cannery at the fish quay, the sawmill at the river mouth. One whose product is bulky or perishable sits near its market: the bakery, the brewery.

Site is the ground: a sheltered basin, a ford, a knoll to defend, flat land for a railway. Situation is the place in the network: on the coast route, at the head of a valley road. Site decides what can be built; situation decides why anyone would.

### Rings: von Thünen and bid rent

Von Thünen (1826) asked what farmers would grow at each distance from one market town on a featureless plain (secondary). Each use can pay a rent that falls with distance, because transport eats its margin. The use that bids most wins each ring: market gardens and dairy close in, then forest for fuel and timber, then grain, then grazing. Alonso (1964), Muth and Mills moved the logic into the city (secondary). Firms, shops and households bid for land by what access to the centre is worth to them. Shops and offices bid steeply and win the middle. Households wanting space bid flatly and win the edge. So land price and density fall with distance, and plots and houses grow. Clark (1951) found density falling roughly exponentially from the centre in 36 cities, and the slope has flattened as incomes rose and transport got cheaper (secondary).

The model is wrong in detail and right in direction. It gives a generator a price field over the land. It gives the order in which uses replace each other as a town grows: field, villa, flats, offices. And every change in transport reshapes the field.

Where the rich live is not fixed by the model. If the centre has amenities the edge lacks (history, the harbour, the view, culture), the rich stay central, as in Paris. If it does not, they leave for the edge, as in Detroit (Brueckner, Thisse and Zenou 1999). A fast new mode that is dear takes the rich out first. When it gets cheap the rest follow, and the centre can become wanted again (LeRoy and Sonstelie 1983; secondary). For a setting pack this is a dial. A Norwegian harbour town, London and a Midwestern city sort differently because their centres carry different amenities.

### Rings, wedges and nuclei

The three classic models read best as three forces acting together.

| Model | Its force | What it gives a generator |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burgess, concentric zones (1925) | Access cost. The ring next to the centre is a "zone in transition", held by owners waiting for the centre to grow into it, so it is let cheaply and decays (secondary) | rings by distance; a decaying collar round the core |
| Hoyt, sectors (1939) | Direction. The better-off move outward along the fastest routes, toward high ground, water and open country, away from industry, and keep moving the same way for decades (secondary) | wedges that persist; the better-off hill grows one way |
| Harris and Ullman, multiple nuclei (1945) | Specialisation. Some uses need each other, some repel each other, some need special sites, and each grows its own nucleus (secondary) | a harbour core, a market core, a works, a station quarter |

Hoyt's monograph, written for the US Federal Housing Administration, is also said to name the homes of the community's leaders as a pull (unverified). The practical point holds either way: prestige is path-dependent. Once a direction is the good side, it stays the good side.

### Central places: how towns sit in a region

Christaller (1933) worked from southern Germany. Each good or service has a threshold, the smallest market that keeps it open, and a range, how far people will travel for it. Bread has a low threshold and a short range, so every village has a baker. A cathedral, a hospital or a department store needs a big market, so only the bigger places have one. The result is a hierarchy: many small places, fewer big ones, each bigger one offering all the smaller ones do and more. On an ideal plain the market areas are hexagons, nested by a ratio k (secondary). k is 3 when the fewest centres serve everyone, 4 when centres line up on transport routes, and 7 when each higher centre governs its own district. Lösch (1940) let each good form its own lattice (secondary). Real regions bend the hexagons along coasts, valleys and roads, but the hierarchy and the spacing survive.

It gives us the region's settlements as a graph with ranks, each rank with a list of what it offers. A fishing village has a chapel, a shop and a quay. A market town adds a church, a doctor, a bank and a weekly market. The regional town adds the hospital, the cathedral, the station, the newspaper and the top of the venue ladder. It also predicts what faster roads do. The range of goods grows, small places lose their shops to bigger ones, and villages become dormitories.

### Agglomeration: why quarters specialise

Marshall (1890) gave three reasons firms of a kind gather: a shared pool of skilled workers, shared suppliers, and ideas "in the air". Duranton and Puga (2004) restate them as sharing, matching and learning (secondary). Hotelling (1929) adds that competing sellers cluster (secondary). Nuisances push the other way. In British industrial cities the prevailing westerlies blew coal smoke east, so the east sides became poor and stayed poor. Historical pollution still accounts for up to a fifth of neighbourhood sorting in 2011 (Heblich, Trew and Zylberberg 2021).

| Quarter | What pulls it together | What it pushes away | What it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The harbour | the break of bulk; shared cranes, stores, chandlers, agents | homes, by smell and noise | warehouses gable to the quay, hoists, offices facing the water |
| The market | buyers and sellers finding each other on set days | little | the square, the hall, the bank and the hotel round it |
| The works | processing near the boats or the line; a shared labour pool | homes, by smoke and smell downwind | the works on the edge or downwind, workers' housing within a walk |
| The better-off hill | view, sun, height, upwind, the prestige of neighbours | all of the above | villas in big gardens, iron fences, old trees, a church |
| The trades' streets | like trades together: coopers, furniture makers, later car dealers | the core's rents | lanes of workshops with yards behind |

## 2. Transport eras and the shape they leave

### The travel budget

People everywhere spend about an hour a day travelling, half an hour each way, whatever the mode (Zahavi, Marchetti 1994; secondary). Faster transport does not shorten the trip. It spreads the town. Walking at 4 to 5 km/h gives a radius of about 2.5 km. No large European city before 1800 was much more than about 5 km across (secondary). So an era's door-to-door speed, times about half an hour, is the reach of the built town along the routes that mode serves.

Two consequences matter. New roads fill: in American metropolitan areas, driving on interstates rose one for one with the lane kilometres built (Duranton and Turner 2011). And travel per person in eight rich countries stopped growing in the early 2000s while incomes kept rising (Millard-Ball and Schipper 2011). That is part of today's turn back toward the centre.

### The eras

| Era | Mode and speed | Reach | Shape it gives | What stays |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Walking city, to about 1850 to 1880 | feet, cart, boat; 4 to 5 km/h | 2 to 3 km | dense and mixed; rich and poor close, the rich at the centre | lanes, steps, small plots, the oldest core |
| Omnibus and horse tram, about 1850 to 1890 | 8 to 10 km/h | Boston: from 2 miles on foot to 4 miles (secondary) | first suburbs for the better-off along a few lines | a middle-class street along the old line |
| Electric tram, about 1890 to 1930 | 12 to 20 km/h | Boston: 6 miles (secondary) | fingers of growth along the lines; shops at the stops | the tram street with corner shops, its width, rails under asphalt |
| Commuter railway, from about 1870 | 30 to 60 km/h | beads 10 to 30 km out | villages at stations grow; the line as a barrier | the station quarter, the goods yard, a town that stops at the line |
| Bus and early car, 1920s to 1950s | 20 to 40 km/h | infill between the fingers | the garden suburb; garages added at the back | houses in gardens; a garage on the lane |
| The automobile city, 1950s to 1980s | 40 to 80 km/h | 20 km and more | low density; ring roads; centres cut for roads and car parks | the street that widens and stops; a car park where houses stood; the paved front garden; the dropped kerb |
| The reversal, from the 1960s and 70s | walking and transit back in the centre | the centre | pedestrian streets (Copenhagen's Strøget from 1962), conservation, flats back in the centre (secondary) | the pedestrian street, the quay as promenade, bike racks where parking was |

Dates differ by country; the order does not. Adams (1970) set out the same sequence for Midwestern cities (unverified). Warner's *Streetcar Suburbs* (1962) showed what the tram did to Boston: it split an inner city of work and slums from outer suburbs of commuters (secondary). Newman, Kosonen and Kenworthy (2016) describe every city as an overlay of three fabrics (secondary). The walking fabric lies within about 1 to 2 km of each centre. Transit fabrics run along the lines. The automobile fabric covers the rest.

### Density and the car

Newman and Kenworthy (1989) compared 32 cities. Petrol use per person climbed steeply as density fell. Driving rises sharply below about 20 people per hectare, and bus service turns poor below about 30 (secondary). Their method has been criticised, and a reanalysis found a weaker link (secondary). The direction is not disputed. A fabric built for one mode makes the others hard, so each era's fabric keeps its mode alive after the era ends.

### How the layers stay

A transport era is cheap to start and dear to remove. The street laid for the tram keeps its width when the tram goes. The suburb laid out for walking to the stop keeps its corner shops while they pay. A road cut through for cars stays after its plan is dropped. Streets outlast buildings (Conzen, in `docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`). So a town reads era by era along its streets: the old road curving at a horse's grade, the straight tram street, the 1970s cul-de-sac.

## 3. Growth, scale and change over time

### Scaling laws

Bettencourt, Lobo, Helbing, Kühnert and West (2007) measured how city quantities change with population. Homes, jobs and water use grow in proportion. Social and economic outputs grow faster, with an exponent of about 1.15: wages, GDP and patents, and also crime and some diseases. Infrastructure grows slower: road surface 0.83, power cable 0.87, petrol stations 0.77 (secondary). Bettencourt (2013) derived the numbers from the city as a network of social contacts laid on a built network (secondary).

For us they are checks, not laws to simulate. A city of 8 million is 84 times a town of 95,000. Per head it has about 1.9 times the output and about half the road surface. Bigger means more shops, venues and crime per head, and fewer metres of street. A pack of another size can have its counts of pubs, shops, venues and road length checked against these ratios. The exponents vary between countries and with where a city's edge is drawn (secondary), so they bound the counts rather than fix them.

### Complexity

Batty (2005, 2013) treats cities as built from the bottom up by many local decisions, never in equilibrium, with thresholds and surprises (secondary). Two lessons follow. The form comes from what local rules do over time, not from a planner. And growth is uneven: long quiet spells, short bursts.

### Models of growth

| Model | How it works | Good for | Not for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cellular automata (White and Engelen 1993; SLEUTH) | each cell changes use by its own suitability and the uses round it; growth totals set from outside | fractal edges; the spread of a use; cheap | streets, plots and causes (`generating-the-past.md`: masses without street logic) |
| Agent-based (Schelling 1971 and kin) | agents move by simple local preferences | sorting and tipping | the town's economy |
| Microsimulation (UrbanSim; Waddell 2002) | every household, job, building and parcel, stepped yearly: totals, movers, location choice by price, access and neighbours, developers, a price update (secondary) | the structure that matches our engine | real time; it wants calibration data we will not have |
| System dynamics (Forrester's *Urban Dynamics*, 1969) | stocks and flows of housing, jobs and people | it is where SimCity's model came from (secondary) | anything spatial; its policy conclusions are disputed |

We want UrbanSim's structure, made coarse. It steps yearly, plot by plot. People choose among real places by price, access and neighbours, developers build where it pays, and the totals come from the setting timeline. It must run 120 years over about 10,000 plots in minutes.

### Booms, busts and halts

Building comes in waves. Chicago's land prices peaked in 1836, 1854, 1872, 1892, 1907 and 1925 (Hoyt 1933; secondary). That is roughly every 18 years. Long swings in building of 15 to 25 years run through Western cities (Gottlieb 1976; secondary). Oslo's boom of the 1890s ended in the crash of 1899 (`docs/upkeep.md`).

| Phase | What happens | What it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boom | speculative building in the day's style; plots merged in the core; land bought ahead; streets laid out faster than they fill | a quarter in one style and one date |
| Bust | building stops mid-street; firms close; land returns to the banks | half-built streets, lone blocks, fallow lots, empty shops |
| Halt | the edge stops moving | a fringe belt of big, low-density institutions on cheap edge land: hospital, cemetery, sports ground, school (Conzen) |
| Decline | demand falls; the buildings remain | prices fall first; people leave only as buildings wear out; cheap space draws the poor and the new |

Whitehand (1972) found that what gets built at Glasgow's edge differs between booms and slumps. The usual reading is that institutions win the edge in slumps, when housing cannot bid (unverified).

Decline is not growth in reverse (Glaeser and Gyourko 2005). Houses last, so a town that loses its trade keeps its houses. Prices fall below the cost of building, and nobody builds. The town empties slowly as the houses wear out. In 1990 nearly 60 percent of owner-occupied single-family homes in Midwestern central cities were worth less than their construction cost (secondary). For us, a bust shows first as low prices and poorer occupants, then as missing upkeep, and only decades later as gaps.

## 4. Sorting and succession

### Filtering

As a building ages it passes down the income scale. Rosenthal (2014) followed American homes from 1985 to 2011. The real income of each new occupant fell about 2.5 percent a year in rented homes and 0.5 percent in owned ones. Filtering is slower where prices rise fast, and renovation reverses it. This is a quarter's clock. Built for the better-off, the houses are let to clerks, then divided for workers and lodgers. Then they are cleared, left, or bought back and restored.

### Succession

The Chicago school of the 1920s took "invasion and succession" from ecology: a new group enters a quarter, the old one leaves, and the quarter's character follows (secondary). Hoover and Vernon (1959) gave a neighbourhood's life in stages, usually summarised as development, transition, downgrading, thinning out and renewal (the names unverified). Schelling (1971) showed that mild preferences about neighbours add up to strong separation. Nobody has to want a sorted town for the town to sort. Our packs sort people by income, household stage, trade and taste, never by ethnicity (Rule 8).

### Decline and neglect

Decline feeds itself. An owner who expects the street to go down does not repair, so it goes down, and the next owner expects it too. Davis and Whinston (1961) are cited for framing this as a prisoner's dilemma: each owner gains most by letting the others improve (unverified). Institutions can tip a street. In the 1930s the US Home Owners' Loan Corporation graded neighbourhoods for lending. The lowest-graded areas, starved of credit, had lower home ownership, values and rents decades later (Aaronson, Hartley and Mazumder 2021). Those grades were drawn largely on race, which our packs never use. Credit withdrawn by map is the general mechanism. In 1970s New York the city closed fire companies in poor districts on a model's advice. The Wallaces argue it set off fires and abandonment that spread block by block (secondary; disputed).

### Renewal and clearance

Jacobs (1961, chapter 16) set "gradual money" against "cataclysmic money" (secondary). Small sums over many years keep a district alive. Large sums at once, for clearance, big schemes or highways, wipe it out and rebuild it in one date. Post-war renewal in Europe and America cleared "slums", mostly cheap old housing, for roads, blocks and car parks. Most schemes were carried out in part (`docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`). Conservation from the 1960s and 70s saved what was cheap to keep and not yet cleared.

### Gentrification

Ruth Glass named it in London in 1964 (secondary). Middle-class households bought and restored working-class houses in Islington, and once it began, it ran on until most of the old occupiers were gone. There are three accounts of why, and all three are useful.

1. The rent gap (Smith 1979; secondary). Disinvestment widens the gap between what a plot earns now and what it could earn. When the gap is big enough, reinvestment pays and capital returns. Hamnett and Bourassa contest it.
2. Cheap space and culture (Jacobs 1961; Zukin 1982; secondary). New ideas and marginal trades cannot carry the cost of new buildings, so they use old ones. Artists take cheap lofts, which makes the place known; capital follows and prices them out, as in SoHo.
3. Stages (Clay 1979; secondary). First come risk-oblivious pioneers, often artists. Then early middle-class buyers and estate agents. Then the press, rapid change and displacement. Last, the well-off compete for the best houses.

In our world each step of the cycle is a fact. A works closes and its space is cheap. Rehearsal rooms, studios and a bar move in, and the quarter gets a name. Flats are converted next door, the new neighbours complain about noise, and the venue closes. London lost 35 percent of its music venues from 2007 to 2015 (`docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`). Rent regulation slows the cycle and changes what shows (section 5).

### How a quarter's state shows

| State | Facts behind it | What shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New and sought | built within 20 years; owners living there; prices high | one style and date; full gardens; fresh coats in one era's palette |
| Steady | long-held; care steady | coats on many cycles; gardens kept; small repairs in the right material |
| Filtering down | owners leaving; letting rises; houses divided | more doorbells; two window types; gardens paved or bare; cheap repairs; more cars |
| Neglected | absentee owners; low rents; clearance expected | failed paint, boarded windows, weeds, fallow lots, posters; repairs stop |
| Pioneered | cheap space taken by artists, bands, odd trades | one lit loft in a dark row; posters; a café in an old shop; bikes |
| Gentrifying | buyers restoring; prices rising | restored fronts beside failed ones; old windows put back; skips and scaffolding |
| Renewed | cleared and rebuilt | a block of one later date in an older grain; a set-back building line; a car park waiting |

The history budget applies (`docs/living-fabric.md`). At the city camera a quarter's state is one shape (a darker house, a run of restored fronts), never an even speckle.

## 5. Institutions and money

### Who owns land

| Owner | Horizon | How they behave | Their signature |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Great estate (London's Grosvenor, Bedford, Portman) | centuries | lets on building leases, usually 99 years; re-lets or rebuilds when a lease ends; controls design; leasehold reform up to 1993 weakened the cycle (secondary) | squares in one style; rebuilt in sweeps as leases fall in |
| Owner-occupier | a household's life | keeps what they use, by money and life stage | variety from house to house; care that tracks the family |
| Resident landlord | years | keeps the parts they share | between owner and absentee |
| Absentee landlord | the rent | spends what keeps the rent coming | in Rochester in 2017, absentee-owned homes had the most code violations and owner-occupied the fewest, from the tenure's incentives rather than the owners' tastes (Rose and Harris 2022) |
| Heirs, an estate in probate | uncertain | nobody decides | empty and boarded for years (the corner's house D) |
| The town | decades, by department | builds streets, schools, quays; buys land ahead; clears; conserves | public buildings of each era; works done in part |
| The state's bank | decades | lends by rule | Norway's Husbanken, from 1946, financed about half of all post-war homes (secondary): standard plans and sizes everywhere |
| Cooperative | generations | members' savings and state loans; a shared schedule | OBOS, from 1929 (secondary): blocks repainted together |
| Municipal landlord | generations | builds and keeps on a schedule | Red Vienna's Gemeindebau: about 60,000 to 65,000 flats by 1934, 40 percent paid by a steeply progressive housing tax of 1923 (secondary) |
| Land bank with leasehold | a century | buys land ahead and lets sites | Stockholm bought Enskede in 1904 and let its sites on 60-year leases from 1908 (secondary) |
| Church | centuries | land-rich, cash-poor | open ground in the centre; the oldest trees |
| Company | the works' life | houses by rank near the works; keeps them while it needs workers | Norsk Hydro's company towns of Rjukan and Notodden, from the 1900s, now World Heritage (secondary): one plan, houses that age together and are let go together |
| Harbour board, utilities | decades | keep their own assets on their own schedules | quays; cuts in the street; covers by era |

Who owns matters more than how much money there is. The same money buys different upkeep under a long horizon and a short one.

### Why one street is kept and the next let go

An owner repairs when the next years of use or rent are worth more with the repair than without. Whatever changes that sum shows on the fabric.

1. Means. No money, no repair.
2. Tenure. Owners who live there keep more than landlords, and absentee landlords keep least.
3. Horizon. A short horizon kills upkeep. A road scheme drawn over a street blights it, because the houses cannot be sold at their value. In Britain owners on a safeguarded route can force the promoter to buy (secondary). That owners then stop spending is expected but unverified. A lease near its end, a building due for clearance and an heir waiting to sell act the same way.
4. Regulation. Rents held below the market cut the return on repair. Oslo and Trondheim regulated rents in flats built before 8 April 1940 until 2010, after a ten-year phase-out (secondary). About 12,000 Oslo flats were covered in 1992. When San Francisco extended rent control in 1994, the landlords it covered cut their rental supply by 15 percent (Diamond, McQuade and Qian 2019). They sold to owner-occupiers, converted and rebuilt. So regulation shows twice: worn buildings with long tenants, then a burst of conversions when it ends.
5. Neighbours. Repair pays only if the street holds its value, so care clusters. Fresh paint spreads along a street, and so does neglect.
6. Credit. Where lenders will not lend, buyers cannot buy and owners cannot borrow for repairs.
7. Taxes. A tax on a feature removes the feature. England taxed windows from 1696 to 1851, and bricked-up windows still show on houses of the period (secondary). A tax on buildings discourages improving them; a tax on land value alone does not.

### Infrastructure networks

Water, sewers, gas, electricity, telephone and trams each came in their own decades. Each was laid mostly along existing streets, first where people could pay and later everywhere by law. Networks fix the street pattern, because a street with mains under it is dear to move. They order growth: land with water and a tram is worth building on, and land without waits. They leave dated traces: covers, cuts and patches, poles and wires, then wires buried. Separate bodies run them on separate schedules, which is why a street is dug up a year after it was paved (`docs/living-fabric.md`).

## 6. What this means for a game

### What the engine simulates, and what Overstory derives

The engine has people with money and jobs, workplaces, ventures that open places, a council with plans, and events with causes. It has no land market, homes, buildings or transport. The standing rule splits the work: the engine decides that and why, and Overstory decides how it looks.

| Force | The engine decides (facts with reasons) | Overstory derives (written rules) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access | transport works: a line, a road, a bridge (request 14) | the access field: travel time from each plot to work, shops and the centre by the era's modes |
| Clustering and repulsion | where ventures and works locate (request 21) | nuisance and amenity fields: smoke and smell by the wind rose, noise, view, sun, height |
| Land and rent | ownership, sales, inheritance, prices, rents, vacancy (requests 10, 13, 15; prices and tenure are new) | a price field for the generated past, labelled `derived`, until the fabric pass owns it |
| Keepers | care from means, tenure, horizon and neighbours (request 13) | upkeep rates and how they look (`docs/upkeep.md`) |
| Cycles | the setting timeline: booms, busts, sectors (request 3) | each wave's style from the pack's shelves |
| Sorting | households choosing homes by price, access, neighbours and life stage (request 15) | filtering as an interpretation, shown through care, division and stuff |
| Institutions | budgets, plans, regulation, clearance, conservation, lenders' rules | each plan's scar |

### Growing a believable town and keeping it changing

1. The region first. Land, water, resources and the routes between them. Settlements are placed by site and situation (breaks of bulk, crossings, valley mouths) and ranked as central places. The town grows over land that already has farms and villages (`docs/town-plan.md`, Expansion).
2. The era script, from the pack. Each era has its transport, its building shelf, its economy and its rules on fire, rent, zoning and tax. Booms, busts and plans come with causes.
3. Yearly steps over plots. The economy sets the year's totals. Movers choose among plots by price, access, amenity, nuisance and neighbours, by a seeded logit. Developers build where the expected value beats the cost. Prices update and feed the next year.
4. Inertia. A building takes the next use its form allows (Brand's low road) before it is rebuilt. It is rebuilt only when the rent gap exceeds the cost of clearing and building.
5. Waves. Booms grow the edge with housing and merge plots in the core. Halts give the cheap edge to institutions. Busts leave streets half-built and lots fallow.
6. Ageing and succession. Occupants filter down at the pack's rates unless someone reinvests. Reinvestment comes from neighbours, from pioneers in cheap space near amenities, or from a plan.
7. Upkeep from keepers, so care and neglect cluster.
8. Plans carried out plot by plot as plots change hands or burn; the rest is a scar.
9. Check and reject. Every plot must be reachable. Counts of shops, pubs, venues and road length must sit within the scaling ranges for the town's size, and eras and quarter states within the pack's ranges. Otherwise reject and rerun (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`).

Each step writes dated facts with causes into the event log. During play the same steps run on at the engine's pace.

### What goes wrong in city builders

- The player is the only owner. In SimCity and Cities: Skylines the player zones land and buildings appear from demand. No one owns a plot, so no one keeps it or lets it go for a reason. Cities: Skylines II launched with rents so high that homes were abandoned. The 2024 fix removed the "virtual landlord" and set rent from household income (secondary). If you simulate rent, simulate who collects it.
- Agents without homes. In SimCity (2013) a sim kept its money and happiness but owned no house and held no fixed job. At first agents took the shortest route, not the quickest (Maxis, via secondary accounts). Players noticed, and trust in the simulation fell.
- No inertia. Buildings level up and down whole, and abandonment is a switch. Nothing is half-kept, reused or patched, so the town is always this year's town.
- Optimisation from above. Grids, road hierarchies and efficient zones win. Bereitschaft (2016) finds city builders biased toward the car, single-use zones and simple social dynamics (secondary). A town grown by one rational mind reads as planned.
- No region and no before. The map is empty until the player builds.

What makes a result feel lived in:

- Many owners with different means and horizons, so streets differ for reasons. In Caesar III each house rose or fell with the services and desirability round it (secondary). Manor Lords and Foundation let plots and houses follow the roads, which gives organic streets (secondary).
- History before the map. Dwarf Fortress generates its world's history first and places sites from it, with founding dates in the legends (secondary).
- Partial things: plans done in part, the old use showing through the new, repairs in the wrong material, the works that closed, the street that stops.
- Slowness. Most of the town changes slowly and a few things change fast. Seeing slow change needs years between views, which our fixed views across years give.

| Fields at a year | access by mode; nuisance (smoke by the wind rose, smell, noise); amenity (view, sun, height, water); price. Cached by decade, like the living fabric's fields |

### Rules, with starting values to tune

1. The town's reach at an era is the door-to-door speed times 0.5 h, along the era's lines. Walking gives 2.5 km.
2. Price falls with access time, and density follows. Each faster mode flattens the slope.
3. The better-off choose by amenity: upwind, high, a view, away from works, near their own. A direction once chosen keeps winning.
4. Like uses cluster (harbour trades, market, works). Nuisances push homes away along the wind.
5. Filtering: the new occupant's real income falls 2.5 percent a year in rented buildings and 0.5 percent in owned ones (Rosenthal). It falls less where prices rise fast, and renovation resets it.
6. Reinvest when the rent gap exceeds the cost of the works. The gap grows with disinvestment and with nearby amenity and access.
7. Care is a function of means, tenure, horizon, neighbours' care and regulation. A horizon under about ten years cuts care sharply (a starting guess, unverified).
8. Building comes in waves of about 15 to 20 years. Edges grow by housing in booms and by institutions in halts.
9. In decline, prices fall before population, and buildings empty only as they wear out.
10. Plans are carried out plot by plot as plots change hands.
11. Scaling is a check: socioeconomic counts grow as N^1.15, infrastructure as N^0.85, homes and jobs as N.
12. Sort by income, household stage, trade and taste, never by ethnicity (Rule 8).

### For the engine

Beside requests 13 to 21, four additions. Prices, rents and tenure as facts (owner type, whether they live there, a lease's end), so care has a horizon. Households choosing homes with a reason, which extends request 15. Transport eras in the setting timeline, which extends request 3. Lenders' rules as facts of a bank or the council. Until these exist, the generated past runs the rules above with provenance `derived`.

## Gaps

- The rates are American: filtering, upkeep by tenure, decline. We found no Nordic numbers for them or for building cycles.
- Unchecked against the originals: Hoyt's list of pulls, Hoover and Vernon's stage names, Whitehand's slump mechanism, Davis and Whinston's framing, Adams's eras.
- We found no model of a land market in a town of 95,000 with strong state housing finance and long rent regulation, as in Norway after 1946.
- UrbanSim wants calibration data we will not have. We need a coarse version and a test that its towns look like real ones, on real maps and with blind readers.
- These forces are slow, and play spans months to a few years. Whether the game skips years decides how much of this a player sees.
- The metropolis is not covered: land assembly for towers, growth round transit, global capital.
