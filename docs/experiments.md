## Findings

Results that surprised us or might be new, newest first. Each says what the evidence is, what it does not show, and what would disprove it.

- **A camera that holds its height over an old town never sees its lanes (20 October).** In clay at Stavanger's grain (houses about 10 by 7.5 m, lanes 3 to 4 m), a city camera 13 to 19 m over the ground saw a field of roofs; the lanes, steps and squares, where the town's life is, vanished, and even 9 m up a narrow uphill lane gave only a canyon of walls (`reviews/lt1/corner/blockout/`, `reviews/lt1/heart/clay/`). It changed the city view: it now follows the space under it, low along lanes and over open ground, high over roofs, down to a street stop. It does not show how the camera should move between heights, or whether players find that natural; a playable camera test would. It would be disproved if a fixed-height camera with a steeper tilt read the lanes as well in a playtest.

- **Every part a plan shows names its cause, checked by machine (20 October).** `blender/tools/trace_check.py` resolved every fixture building in every year it stands, plus 500 of the stress test's random diaries: 73,121 plans and 1.6 million visible elements. Every element names the diary event that put it there, and every named event is in the diary by that year. We know of no game that checks this; it turns "every visible difference is a true fact" from a promise into a test. *It does not show* that the mesh adds nothing: lean and sag are seeded texture applied at build time, kept out of history by rule rather than by check. Nor does it show that the event is the right reading of the part, or that viewers read it (exit criterion 1). *Gap found:* `condition`, which wear is drawn from, is a number with no trail: the plan knows a house is worn but not which neglect caused it. Next, the plan keeps the events behind its condition, so the diary inspector can point from a worn wall to the years it stood empty. *Disproved if* a built mesh shows something no plan element accounts for; a mesh-side trace in Unity would test that.

## LT1g: the playable corner, a clay town, a walk and a first case (22 October)

**Status:** Phase 0 (design) done, 22 October. The record is in `reviews/lt1/playable/`:
- `brief.md`: agreed with Øystein;
- `pitches.md`: 21 case pitches and 18 testing pitches, by Opus in roles;
- `pick.md`: Fable's pick;
- `outline.md`: Phase 1's build, and Phase 2 as a ladder of cheap tests;
- `premortem.md`: a fresh reviewer's pre-mortem, its fixes applied.

Phase 1, the walk and the light, starts in its own session once Unity is free. Phase 2 is the ladder: its Unity-free tests run beside the walk. This is step 1g of `docs/plan.md`, the greybox.

**The question.** Can we build something whole, with the world first: a town to walk and a case to play? Two things decide it:
- whether reading the corner beats the page at carrying Agnes Fielding's death to the two people who were with her, before the Anchor says it aloud;
- whether choosing whom to tell first costs enough to be a decision.

It moves exit criteria 3 and 4, and tests the game rule in `CLAUDE.md` in play.

**The case: Before Friday** (pitch C3.1, with Fable's changes).
- **The death.** Agnes Fielding, 103, of house E, dies after a long illness (e-6557, public). The record gives her no kin.
- **The two who came.** In November, Hannah Tanner climbed to her door every week (e-6531, private) and Olive Shaw kept turning up with something (e-6532, private). Nobody but the steps shows it.
- **The day.** The player is at A, at the foot of the steps. The town's one café in 1989 is Peter Lane's, so the player's trade is open (Øystein's choice, from test 5). On Friday 15 December, between 10:00 and 18:00, they find and tell people.
- **The spread.** News spreads from whoever they tell, by one stated rule over ties from the export only. It shows on screen, is labelled local scene state, and is never written back.
- **Before the Friday.** Three November evenings come first, each with one vantage chosen in advance and no replay.
- **Score.** Who heard it from the player, minus those who heard early through them, minus each private act said aloud. The close shows the exposure ledger. A fail is Hannah hearing it at the bar.
- **Labelled `authored`:** the player, the key, the day, the homes, the hours.
- **Cut:** no Anchor interior. The death is never shown.

**How it is judged.**
- **The walk (Phase 1).** Øystein walks the build at eye height, through the judging views in `docs/foundations.md`.
  - The light's lasting fix (1c) and the colour grade are judged in drizzle, low sun and blue hour, on clay and stone alike, under SEASON's rule: shadow and ambient colour come from the light, and the grade acts on the whole light, never per material.
  - The harness's whole-scene run names the three biggest costs.
- **The play (Phase 2): the ladder.** Øystein ruled on 22 October that Phase 2's build, about 16 Opus-days, is too much to commit to before we know what to test. So five cheap tests come first (`outline.md`, section 3).
  - Together they cost about one Opus-day (8% of the weekly limit) and an hour of Øystein's.
  - None needs the command port, solver, bots, agent players or replays.
  - The spread rule, the score and the hours are frozen and hashed before the first test.
- **The views:** eye level at A's window, on the steps, on the Row, and at the Anchor's door.
- **The seeds:**
  - A is Agnes, December 1989.
  - B is Andy Shaw, October 1998 (e-8899). He has no kin. His last September's private visitors were Trevor Lane, Ben Reed and Oliver Brook of house B (e-8881 to e-8883).
- **The ladder's pass lines,** in points of 80:
  1. **The tie script,** over the export's ties at the seed's month, on both seeds:
     - the branches at the first telling (Hannah first, Olive first) differ by at least 16;
     - the best line beats the page's (the Anchor's lead first) and Peter-first by at least 16;
     - telling the two back to back is not best;
     - the page's line reaches at least six people by 18:00;
     - the ranking holds with every spread parameter at half and one and a half times.
  2. **Sight, then stills:** one vantage sees both doors and most of Hannah's climb; on stills at the real hours, a cold reader says which door each figure left and entered.
  3. **Two cold readers** on Sonnet: the one with the stills and the page names Hannah from the steps and Olive, each sourced, and invents nothing. The one with the page only does not name Hannah, or marks her as a guess.
  4. **Øystein's paper run,** under an hour, refereed by the tie script: he names the tell-order trade-off unprompted and wants seed B.
  5. **The facts check:** every contradiction between the case, the export and the houses' diaries has a fix in our authored layer.
- **After the ladder.** If it passes, the testing design and the case's build are sized afresh, as their own decision with Øystein: the command port, the solver and the agents of `pick.md`, and Øystein's three plays (seed A out loud, seed B in silence, one try without reading).

**What it does not test:** fun and return; human misreadings (humans come only through the engine's human test); a building's history; the Anchor's inside; city scale; the decade payoff; whether the spread rule is true to life.

**What would change our minds,** each a kill line in the ladder:
- the first-telling branches score within 8 of each other, still flat after one frozen change (there is no decision; move to I Saw It's attention grammar);
- the ranking flips inside the sweep (the rule is tuned, not designed);
- no vantage shows the climb, even in daylight (the steps tell nothing);
- the page-only reader names both visitors with confidence (the screen adds nothing);
- the reader with the stills reads the climb as something false, or invents causes;
- Øystein calls the evenings watching, or sees the Friday's answer without a trade-off;
- a contradiction only the engine could settle.

## LT1, the building blocks' architecture (21 October)

**Status:** decided; the strict review's fixes applied (ready after fixes: 4 must, 15 should, 6 could; `docs/research/building-blocks-review.md`). Step 1a. The design is `docs/building-blocks.md`, its reasons `docs/research/building-blocks-decisions.md`.

**The question:** what must the foundations be so every kind is built once, right, and holds for a bigger city and the three example games (a manager, an exploration game, a family across generations)?

**How it was made:**
- an Opus register of 123 decisions from fourteen studies and five digests (`docs/research/building-blocks-inputs.md`);
- a Fable pre-mortem of the architecture (`docs/research/pre-mortem-architecture.md`);
- Opus packets of the code and of the surrounding docs;
- Fable's decisions, each firm or provisional.

**The outcome, kept:**
- Parts are made offline; the town is assembled in the player, C# becoming the one source stage by stage after parity.
- Contracts are JSON Schema files, with golden fixtures and migrations.
- History is a function of the record and the date: maps hold rates, reset years and age offsets, and a jump costs a uniform.
- Ids are stable down to slot and fill. One dated slot-and-fill record serves windows, coats and repairs.
- Five levels switched by error in pixels; chunks of 4, 32, 64 and 128 m; shadows from proxies.
- Budgets in milliseconds, megabytes and triangles, per pass and per kilometre, on the 16 GB M4.

**Where it overruled the studies' leans:**
- kind files at the repo root, `kinds/`, not `blender/kinds/`, since both languages read them;
- a rate change rebakes a map region on the GPU, not the chunk's mesh;
- the far shell may cache an output bake, the one exception to inputs-only maps;
- the painted far town replaces conventional far levels per kind, whether or not the atlas is painted;
- Python's hash is the spec, C# matching it;
- a strict review accepts baselines while building, Øystein the milestone sheets;
- the word `engine` retires as a provenance value, leaving four.

After the strict review, repetition follows `NOW.md`'s decided wording: libraries only for what was mass-produced, everything hand-made for one place its own; only hand-split wall stones may go to Øystein, and only if the scale test's memory forces it.

**What we drop:** the statements listed in `docs/building-blocks.md` (What this changes), among them offline-only life, vertex-baked city life, "years since" per coat, the tiers' switch distances, LFS for generated files and `random.Random`.

**Still provisional:** every budget (the harness run and the scale test); byte widths and the history carrier (the gully's A/B at 3.2); map density and unit sizes (the scale test); repetition (Øystein's reading and a look test); the painted atlas (its look test, when Øystein chooses); picking (the spike).

**The port re-estimated:** 13.5 to 17.5 Opus-days for the whole port, 6.5 to 8 through history as a function, against the plan's three. The cut line is step 3, history as a function: Friday shows steps 0 to 2 reviewed and step 3 as far as it got.

## LT1f: the corner through the years, in clay

**Status:** a first round run and recorded (22 October); the strict review and Øystein's judgement to come. Step 1f of `docs/plan.md`: the game's question tested early, before any final house part is made. It needs none of the building blocks' architecture and does not decide any of it.

**The question:** can cold readers tell, from the corner on screen, which parts of its five houses are old, changed or neglected, and read what changed between 1989, 2000, 2010 and 2020; and do they understand it better than from the same facts on the page? It moves exit criteria 1 and 4, and through the pairs of years, 3.

**What is built:**
- *The facts, first.* What would make the test lie is fixed before anything is drawn (`docs/research/inputs/5-buildings-fronts-time.md`, §5 and §6):
  - House B joins the corner's fixture, with the house that burned before it, so "rebuilt after a fire" is a dated fact.
  - Classical houses no longer roll a mansard by seed: on D or E it would claim a roof raise the record does not hold.
  - Every house gets events from 1989 to 2020, authored by named rules of real practice (`docs/research/house-changes.md`, `town-change.md`) and labelled `authored`, since the engine does not model buildings yet: repaints on a kept house's cycle, a shop opened and one closed, a house let go, one restored, windows restored in two steps by two owners, an extension.
  - A shop no longer deletes the windows it covers, so a closed shop's windows can return. Dated cells are left to the architecture.
- *The corner at four dates.* The five houses built by the test-quality generator from their diaries at 1989, 2000, 2010 and 2020, at the corner's real proportions (`gentle`). They stand in the heart's clay at the corner's places, in place of their grey masses: A and B on Harbour Row at the foot of Gully Steps, C beside the Landing, D on the knuckle, E at the top. Nothing else differs between the years.
- *Ways of showing time.* At least twenty ideas, ranked against the time study's constraints and our style; the best two or three prototyped in Unity, in clay.

**How it is judged:**
- *The frames.* Four views, stored as data and the same in every year (`LT1Time.Views`): eye level (1.65 m) on Harbour Row, across from the lane's foot, at A's and B's fronts; the street stop (2.8 m) on Gully Steps, up to the Landing, C, D and E; 12 m over the foot of the steps, up the lane; the city camera, pulled out and steep, all five houses in frame. Each house is in at least two views. One sun for all four years: 20 October at 15:00 UTC, 8° up at 234°. That is 16:00 on the clock in 1989, when Norway was on winter time, and 17:00 in 2000, 2010 and 2020, when summer time still ran on 20 October. Cloud shadows are off, so nothing but the year moves. Clay (every surface one matte grey) in the game's style, at the player's size: 2560 by 1440, one sample a pixel with the game's MSAA (`PlayerFrame`), never rendered larger and averaged down.
- *The readers* (`docs/foundations.md`, Cold readers): fresh agents on Sonnet, a model other than the maker's (Opus), told only that these are views of a town, each seeing the frames as tiles at the player's pixel size. Three roles: someone who has walked such a street all their life, a building historian, a player of exploration games. Nine readers in three arms, one of each role in each arm:
  - *scene first:* the frames, then the page;
  - *page first:* the page, then the frames. The page is the corner's record as text, every dated event with its cause, as the cause view would print it;
  - *shuffled:* frames rendered with each house's later history (everything after the standing house was built) moved to another house, scored against the true record.
- *The tasks* (`docs/research/time-on-screen.md`, The corner through the years; `docs/research/history-in-things.md`, §10):
  1. Order the four frames of each view, shuffled and undated, and date each to a decade.
  2. With the dates: mark each house's roof, upper windows, ground floor, additions and overall care as original, changed later or neglected, each with a confidence.
  3. Say what changed between each pair of years, and why.
  4. Say what happened to each house.
  5. Say whether the corner grew busier or quieter, and better or worse kept, between each pair.

  The scene-first and page-first readers answer 2 to 5 after each medium, then say which told them more, and whether either misled them.
- *The order.* The pack, the briefs and the key are committed before any reader starts. The key is the record: the changes per pair of years and each part's state per year, exported from the resolved plans. A coder agent codes the free text against the key, blind to the arm, and a script scores everything.
- *The bar.* It is provisional, from `docs/foundations.md` and the time study. This round reports against it; it is not yet a gate.
  - Parts marked right: at least four in five of the parts a reader marks, counted over the parts clay can draw. Coats and paint are in the record but not in clay, and are scored apart.
  - Order: every reader in an arm puts every view in order (four in five at a gate).
  - Dating: within a decade where a dated part is in view.
  - Changes found: at least two of three of the budgeted changes a pair.
  - False history claims: at most one a frame on average, none held with high confidence.
  - Direction: right between every pair of years.
  - Shuffled: parts marked right, against the true record, fall by at least thirty points.
  - Criterion 4: scene readers at least as right about what happened as page readers, not misled (no contradiction of the record held with confidence), and preferring the scene.
- *The ways of showing time* are judged for beauty and against the constraints by a strict review (Fable if available, else Opus, noted). For readability, one fresh reader per way sees only that way's frames and says what changed, when and why.

**What would change our minds:**
- If the clay is read well and the shuffled arm falls by thirty points, form alone (windows, shopfronts, boarding, extensions) carries a house's history, and the house parts can be made in the order the plan has them. If the shuffled arm scores as well, readers read conventions (small means old, boarded means poor), not our facts, and the histories need stronger, more particular marks before any part is made.
- If clay carries the changes of form but misses care and paint, the coats and wear layers carry criterion 1, and they come with the first house parts, not after them.
- If a single frame reads restored copies as original but the pairs read them right, showing time is needed for criterion 1 as well as 3, and the history budget counts changes across a jump as well as at one date.
- If page readers do as well on every task, the scene adds nothing in clay, and criterion 4 waits for materials. If scene readers are misled where page readers are not, the drawing claims more than the record holds, and the rule that drew it is tightened.
- If readers find more than three changes a pair at the street stop without loss, the budget of three is too low for clay at this grain; if they find fewer than two, it is too high.

**What it does not test:** final materials and parts (the windows, doors and shopfronts are the test generator's); colour, coats and wear (clay has none); people, cars and lit windows (this is the fabric-only arm); trees and the ground's growth (the heart's clay has none); the toggle and other transitions in motion (model readers see stills; people test them in the engine's human test); other lights and seasons; the time study's season, camera and false-cause controls (a later round); frame rate.

**Outcome (22 October, one round while building; the sheet for Øystein is `reviews/lt1/time/sheet.md`):**
- *Built.* The facts are fixed: B and its fire are in the record, there is no seeded mansard, and the diaries run to 2020. A shop keeps the windows it covers, and the stress test passes to 2020 (`docs/building-system.md`). The five houses stand in the heart's clay at four dates, the frames rendered at the player's size (`LT1Time`, `reviews/lt1/time/frames/`). The pack, briefs and key were committed before any reader started. Nine Sonnet readers took part, one of each role in each of three arms, with one more reader for each of two ways of showing time. A Sonnet coder coded all 287 claims blind to the arm, and `time_score.py` scores them (`reviews/lt1/time/readers/scores.md`).
- *Against the bar* (scene arm unless named):
  - Order: 11 of 12 views right. One reader swapped two 12 m frames that differ only at D and E. Pass.
  - Dating: 0 of 48 frames within a decade. Every reader put the frames between 1885 and 1965, a century early, from the houses' styles. Clay holds no part made after 1989 that looks it. Fail.
  - Parts marked right, of those marked: 80, 75 and 88 per cent against what clay draws (mean 81); 86, 77 and 84 against the record. By part: care 95 per cent, roof 89, upper windows 82, additions 72, ground floor 67. Borderline: one reader is under four in five.
  - Changes found: 6 of the 8 changes clay draws, for each reader (75 per cent). Pass.
  - False history claims: 0.12 to 0.31 a frame, so under one. But two readers each held one with high confidence, so this fails. One of the two is C's 2011 windows called its "front" windows: they are in the wall that faces the lane, which the record calls the back, so it is a naming slip more than a false history.
  - Direction: only the activity is scored, since care goes both ways in every pair. Each reader got 1 of 3 right. Fail. The café's closing in 2009 does not show in clay, so readers saw 2000 to 2010 as busier and 2010 to 2020 as quieter (A's shopfront going).
  - Shuffled: parts marked right against the true record fell from 81 to 49 per cent, 32 points (the bar is 30). Against what the shuffled frames showed, readers scored 80 per cent, as the scene arm did. Pass: readers read the drawing, not conventions.
  - Criterion 4: fail in clay. All six readers who saw both media said the record told them more. On parts they were level (scene 82 per cent, page 83). The page found every change and made no false claim; the scene found 75 per cent and made a few.
- *What reads in clay:* boarding, at every view down to the city camera, and changes of form at eye level, the street stop and 12 m. Those are a shopfront fitted or taken out, a veranda added, windows boarded or unboarded, and a gable window renewed. The D-to-E swap of the empty house was read by every reader. So was A's window rhythm returning when the shop went: "a shopfront cut across two bays is plainly a later insertion".
- *What does not read in clay:*
  - paint and care short of boarding (A's forty years without paint, D's leaking roof);
  - a house empty but not boarded (E from 1993: all three readers had it lived in, in 2000);
  - whether a shop is open (the café's closing; B's open bakery read as "shuttered", because its test-quality shopfront looks blank);
  - a copy from an original (C's 1998 copies, its 2011 factory "old style" windows and D's 2007 copies all read as old or careful work);
  - the date.
- *What misled:*
  - Juxtaposition invited a cause: all three scene readers guessed, with low confidence, that a household moved from E to D, because one was boarded as the other opened. That is the time study's "a cut invites a cause", seen in stills.
  - The undrawn closing put the cause of A's shopfront going in the wrong decade.
  - The page's words disagreed with the part. The page said "a balcony", and the generator draws a glazed veranda with a balcony. Page readers flagged it, and the coder's record now uses the part's words.
- *The ways of showing time* (`docs/ideas/showing-time.md`, 25 ideas ranked; prototypes in `reviews/lt1/time/ways/`):
  - the toggle in place: animated pairs. Its stills are the scene arm's dated strip, with 75 per cent of changes found.
  - repainted, stroke by stroke: each recorded change brushed in over its house in date order, dated and lettered. Its one reader found 7 of 8 changes with no false claim. That reader noted that a tag (E, 1993) promised a change it could not see.
  - a window into another year: a round pane, or a photograph held at the spot, showing the earlier year aligned. Its one reader found 3 of 8: panes over small, distant houses covered most of the house and left little of the later year to compare.

  Pointing from the record within the budget raised what was found and added no false claim. A pointer at a change clay cannot show is a promise the picture breaks. These are one reader each, so they are indications, not results.
- *Protocol faults found:*
  - Two readers (one scene, one shuffled) compared pixels with code in part 1. Part 2 forbade it, but their orderings are tainted.
  - Readers spawned in the project carry its `CLAUDE.md`. One reader said so. None used it to date the frames, which were dated a century early.
  - The direction task needs a defined measure, since our pairs mix decline and repair.
  - The key's drawn flag comes from rules, not the render. It marked E's empty 1993 house as drawn (the curtains go), but at 75 m nothing shows.
- *Measured:* outside the five houses, 0.2 to 0.6 per cent of each frame changes between two dates. That is the changed houses' shadows and the sky map's openness. Inside A, with no event, 0.4 per cent of the eye view changes, from sag that grows with age. Model readers comparing pixels used that drift to order near-identical frames.

**What we keep and what it means:**
- *For the houses (plan step 2):*
  - Form carries the big changes; the care, the date and a shop's life need the coats and wear layers, readable shop glass and lettering, and parts that look their decade. So these come with the first house parts, not after them.
  - The shelf for parts after 1995 (old forms again) gives readers no date. The research on house changes stops at 1989 (`docs/research/house-changes.md`); what owners and the town did from 1989 to 2020 is a research gap, studied before step 4. Datable things are likely candidates: heat pumps, dishes, new glazing and signs (unverified).
  - A copy must be identifiable on close inspection, as the charters ask: a restored part needs its own trace (newer coat, crisper edges), or a single frame reads it as original.
  - An empty house must show it before it is boarded. That is the ground's history (the yard growing over) and dark windows at dusk.
- *For the history budget:* readers found about two changes a pair where two or three were drawn, and pointing at three a pair added none false. The budget of one, two, three stands.
- *For the architecture (step 1a):*
  - Every drawn pixel must name its part. The window had to use the house's changed pixels as a stand-in.
  - The diff check must allow each change's shadow and sky-openness footprint, and count sag that grows with age as drift, as a rate against the date rather than a bake.
  - Cells need dated lives. The covered-cell fix still keeps one `original` a cell.
  - The change query's "drawn" must come from what the view shows, not from rules.
- *For showing time:* repainted stroke by stroke and the toggle go on to the strict review and to people. The window's diegetic form, the photograph held at the spot, is kept for close views only.
- *Not yet judged:* beauty, by strict review and by Øystein; people, in the engine's human test.

## LT1: the harbour street, 1989

**Status:** open.

**The question:** what makes a west-coast harbour town stunning from the city view, and does it hold up in ordinary light?

**The setting:** a fictional harbour town rooted in the Norwegian west coast, in 1989: three years after the oil-price crash, a boom town in a slump. White timber houses, quay warehouses, cobbles, 1980s shopfronts and streetlamps, some shops empty. The town in its landscape: it steps up from a harbour basin with quays on both sides, with the newer town beyond, then low islands and the open fjord.

**What is built:**
- The building system's first version (`docs/building-system.md`): building diaries from fixtures, a resolver, and era shelves of parts for timber houses and a quay warehouse. Stylisation is a set of parameters; the strength is stronger.
- A hero slice, authored in the fixture for one camera: the quay with the Anchor and the oxred warehouse, the town stepping up behind in two or three levels with a stepped lane, and hills, mountains and the fjord mouth as layered landforms.
- The city camera: perspective, not isometric, free to orbit. Its tilt follows its zoom: steep and map-like far out, the horizon in frame at the middle stop, just over the rooftops close in.
- Light as a colour script keyed to facts (date, hour, weather, so sun height and cloud): one mood manager blends sun, sky, shadow colour, the colour of the air by distance, ambient light and grade.
- Two looks on the same geometry: A plain URP Lit (control) and B painted ramp (shadow colour from the light, flat wet colour). C soft toon and D graphic only if B disappoints. Everything else is shared: palette, wetness, glow, atmosphere, grade.

**How it is judged:**
1. *Shape step, in Blender:* done; the strength is stronger.
2. *Clay pass:* the hero slice in grey clay from the city camera at its middle stop, with a few variants of height and angle. Judged on composition, silhouette and layering only. A yes is a frame that has its bones in grey.
3. *Hero frame:* blue hour at the middle stop, look B against A, side by side with a north-star reference Øystein picks. A yes is a frame Øystein finds stunning.
4. *Colour script:* the hero frame across one October day (low morning sun, grey noon, golden late afternoon, blue hour, night) and the same hour through the year (December, March, June, October), each also in greyscale. A yes is a town beautiful because of its light, with ordinary light holding up.
5. *Robustness:* the hero frame in grey drizzle, and at the far and close zoom stops.
6. *Variety check:* the generator on 30 fresh diaries. No two alike; everything that reads as history traces to a diary entry, and seeded texture claims none; the same diary gives the same building after a reload.

**What it does not test:** people, interiors and scenes, history over time, snow cover, the final palette, and surface texture and wear (until a look is chosen).

**Shape step, so far (19 October):**
- Øystein picked strong over gentle.
- Wild is dropped. Its proportions were the attraction; its deformation (a lean of up to 4.5°, up to half a metre of sag, bowing walls) read as caricature and broke construction: walls stood outside roofs.
- Stronger is added: wild's proportions with strong's deformation. Strong and stronger are compared on the same street.
- The first wild sheet went out with a known fault. Since then, building checks and a stress test gate every sheet: 1,564 random buildings at strong and stronger pass, and the street fixtures pass in every year.
- A blind reviewer found what the checks could not see: floating ridge caps, merged double chimneys, window heads hidden by deep eaves, dangling barge ends, and bays that hit porches, cross-gables and the eave. Each became a rule (`docs/building-system.md`, Fitting), and bays became checked parts.

- *A style for the game, after the masters of colour (Øystein, 20 October: stylise more, exaggerate, keep it truly stunning; look to the masters of colour in painting and film):*
  - *The question:* how far can colour, value and air be pushed beyond the light the world gives and stay stunning and true?
