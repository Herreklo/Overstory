## Findings

Results that surprised us or might be new, newest first. Each says what the evidence is, what it does not show, and what would disprove it.

- **A camera that holds its height over an old town never sees its lanes (20 October).** In clay at Stavanger's grain (houses about 10 by 7.5 m, lanes 3 to 4 m), a city camera 13 to 19 m over the ground saw a field of roofs; the lanes, steps and squares, where the town's life is, vanished, and even 9 m up a narrow uphill lane gave only a canyon of walls (`reviews/lt1/corner/blockout/`, `reviews/lt1/heart/clay/`). It changed the city view: it now follows the space under it, low along lanes and over open ground, high over roofs, down to a street stop. It does not show how the camera should move between heights, or whether players find that natural; a playable camera test would. It would be disproved if a fixed-height camera with a steeper tilt read the lanes as well in a playtest.

- **Every part a plan shows names its cause, checked by machine (20 October).** `blender/tools/trace_check.py` resolved every fixture building in every year it stands, plus 500 of the stress test's random diaries: 73,121 plans and 1.6 million visible elements. Every element names the diary event that put it there, and every named event is in the diary by that year. We know of no game that checks this; it turns "every visible difference is a true fact" from a promise into a test. *It does not show* that the mesh adds nothing: lean and sag are seeded texture applied at build time, kept out of history by rule rather than by check. Nor does it show that the event is the right reading of the part, or that viewers read it (exit criterion 1). *Gap found:* `condition`, which wear is drawn from, is a number with no trail: the plan knows a house is worn but not which neglect caused it. Next, the plan keeps the events behind its condition, so the diary inspector can point from a worn wall to the years it stood empty. *Disproved if* a built mesh shows something no plan element accounts for; a mesh-side trace in Unity would test that.

## LT1: the harbour street, 1989

**Status:** open.

**The question:** what makes a west-coast harbour town stunning from the city view, and does it hold up in ordinary light?

**The setting:** a fictional harbour town rooted in the Norwegian west coast, in 1989: three years after the oil-price crash, a boom town in a slump. White timber houses, quay warehouses, cobbles, 1980s shopfronts and streetlamps, some shops empty. The town in its landscape: it steps up from a harbour basin with quays on both sides, with the newer town beyond, then low islands and the open fjord.

**What is built:**
- The building system's first version (`docs/building-system.md`): building diaries from fixtures, a resolver, and era shelves of parts for timber houses and a quay warehouse. Stylisation is a set of parameters; the strength is stronger.
- A hero slice, authored in the fixture for one camera: the quay with the Anchor and the oxred warehouse, the town stepping up behind in two or three levels with a stepped lane, and hills, mountains and the fjord mouth as layered landforms.
- The city camera: perspective, not isometric, free to orbit. Its tilt follows its zoom: steep and map-like far out, the horizon in frame at the middle stop, just over the rooftops close in.
- Light as a colour script keyed to facts (date, hour, weather, so sun height and cloud): one mood manager blends sun, sky, shadow colour, the colour of the air by distance, ambient light and grade.
- Two looks on the same geometry: A plain URP Lit (control) and B painted ramp (shadow colour from the light, flat wet colour). C soft toon and D graphic only if B disappoints. Everything else is shared: palette, wetness, glow, atmosphere, grade.

**How it is judged:**
1. *Shape step, in Blender:* done; the strength is stronger.
2. *Clay pass:* the hero slice in grey clay from the city camera at its middle stop, with a few variants of height and angle. Judged on composition, silhouette and layering only. A yes is a frame that has its bones in grey.
3. *Hero frame:* blue hour at the middle stop, look B against A, side by side with a north-star reference Øystein picks. A yes is a frame Øystein finds stunning.
4. *Colour script:* the hero frame across one October day (low morning sun, grey noon, golden late afternoon, blue hour, night) and the same hour through the year (December, March, June, October), each also in greyscale. A yes is a town beautiful because of its light, with ordinary light holding up.
5. *Robustness:* the hero frame in grey drizzle, and at the far and close zoom stops.
6. *Variety check:* the generator on 30 fresh diaries. No two alike; everything that reads as history traces to a diary entry, and seeded texture claims none; the same diary gives the same building after a reload.

**What it does not test:** people, interiors and scenes, history over time, snow cover, the final palette, and surface texture and wear (until a look is chosen).

**Shape step, so far (19 October):**
- Øystein picked strong over gentle.
- Wild is dropped. Its proportions were the attraction; its deformation (a lean of up to 4.5°, up to half a metre of sag, bowing walls) read as caricature and broke construction: walls stood outside roofs.
- Stronger is added: wild's proportions with strong's deformation. Strong and stronger are compared on the same street.
- The first wild sheet went out with a known fault. Since then, building checks and a stress test gate every sheet: 1,564 random buildings at strong and stronger pass, and the street fixtures pass in every year.
- A blind reviewer found what the checks could not see: floating ridge caps, merged double chimneys, window heads hidden by deep eaves, dangling barge ends, and bays that hit porches, cross-gables and the eave. Each became a rule (`docs/building-system.md`, Fitting), and bays became checked parts.
- Øystein picked stronger over strong. It is the default strength (`blender/resolver/eras.py`) and the shape language for the street. This ends the shape step.

**Unity step, so far (19 October):**
- The Unity 6 URP project is set up from code (`unity/Assets/Code/Editor/LT1Setup.cs`). Blender exports the street at 1989 (`blender/tools/export_street.py`); Unity places it and generates the ground. An axis check guards the hand-over: Unity's x, y, z is the street's x, z, y.
- The fixture has an authored street block (bands, lamps of three eras, bollards, the ground behind) and the lit floors at blue hour.
- At stronger proportions neighbours overlapped and porches stood in the road. The plots are re-spaced with at least 1.2 m clear, and the building line now holds what stands on the ground (steps, porches, bays), as a real building line does.
- Look A is URP's own lighting on a surface every look shares: palette, wetness by porosity, puddles on paving, window and lamp glow, our own height fog. Screen-space reflections are on.
- Checkpoint (`reviews/lt1/checkpoint/`): look A in drizzle from both cameras, exposed for the white walls, with greyscale copies. A fresh reviewer's findings were fixed at the cause: doors differ from their walls in lightness (a resolver rule), puddles lie only on paving, distant setts average to one tone instead of speckle, bollards stand on the coping, and the world carries on past the street's ends.
- Open: every surface is one flat colour per face, so everything reads as plastic. Also warehouse roofs without tile courses, steps the same value as their plinths, and the empty hillside behind the row (the fixture has nothing there).

**Plan revised (19 October):**
- The look A checkpoint showed the street's bones, not a look. Flat colour under physical lighting reads as plastic; one row on an empty plane makes no picture; and grey drizzle is the light to prove a look in, not to find one. The plan narrows: find stunning first in one composed frame in the most favourable light, then test it in ordinary light.
- The street-level camera leaves LT1. Eye level is closer than the city view, and scenes are their own subject, not yet discussed.
- References that came up:
  - *Overland:* take the high camera, flat colour, a strong colour script and the world dimmed outside the area in play; leave the cut-earth board edge.
  - *A lit cutaway of a warehouse home:* take warm interior light against a dark outside. Whether the closest zoom ever opens buildings stays open.
  - *A Coober Pedy overview:* take the density of props and the soft contact shadows; leave realistic ground textures for now.
  - *Firewatch:* take a colour script per hour and the air coloured by distance; leave saturating everything, since our base stays muted.

**Clay pass, so far (19 October):**
- The hero slice is authored in the fixture: two levels above Harbour Row with fifteen houses (older and smaller higher up), dry-stone retaining walls, lanes, a stepped lane from the quay, the hillside, hills on both sides of the bay and mountain ridges. The export checks gaps per level and keeps the stepped lane clear. Unity generates the terrain; a clay switch turns every surface one matte grey.
- First frames (`reviews/lt1/clay/`): the frontal middle stop is dull. Views along the shore, oblique, read in layers and are far stronger.
- Straight parallel terraces with houses in rows read as a stage. A bay with hills close on both sides gives the town a setting.
- Ridges built as extruded strips read as curtains, not rock: landforms need a real terrain generator.

**Look sketch, so far (20 October):**
- The research's shared foundations are in: what each face sees (sky, open ground, openness) is baked into every part by the generator; a sky term built from it; air in a designed colour ramp by distance (near, middle, far, as Firewatch's fog), thicker low down so hills grade from a hazy foot to a clearer crest.
- The ground is a height field generated from a few site facts (the bay, the hills beside it, the hillside behind, seeded roughness); the terraces fill the bay.
- Look D, the most promising: light cut into flat plates in the light's own colour (each plate one exposure step), thin indigo key lines on real edges, fading with distance. Carving and white-line engraving were tried and dropped: they read as pattern laid over the picture.
- The camera moved into the village, over the quay at roof height. From a distance the town read as a model; close, it reads as a place.
- Two lights carry it: blue hour (indigo air, warm windows) and a hot summer evening (low sun on the harbour fronts, warm haze). Frames in `reviews/lt1/looks/`.
- Cloud light: the weather's clouds cast their shadows along the sun from a layer overhead, so the low sun falls on the town and the land in pools (after a SEASON harbour still). Shade in look D takes a richer version of the sky's colour. Land and mountains are crisp facets that catch the light plane by plane.
- Open: the sky is still a plain gradient, where the references put half their drama (clouds with volume, light breaking through); the land is underworked; the shore is a straight line.

**Direction chosen, and where it stands (20 October):**
- Øystein chose painterly realism, with Simon Stålenhag leading (`docs/visual-direction.md`). Look P is its first version: soft wrapped light, shade in the sky's colour, a warm turn at the shadow's edge, muted base colours, world-fixed brushwork, grime where surfaces see the ground. The town was exported at real proportions (gentle) for it; real proportions read as a place where stronger read as a toy village.
- Trees: a first Blender generator (four species, autumn colours, size from planting year). Judged poor: made quickly, without the care the houses had.
- Øystein's verdict: the scene is now the limit, not the shading. The town's structure is weird and boring (rows on flat terraces), it has no logic and no places where light can do something (lanes, squares, gaps, overhangs), and the nature is a placeholder. Next: give the town a believable structure and the trees the same care as the houses, then judge look P again.

**The reset: target frames and a beauty corner (20 October, proposed):**
- *Why:* everything so far is test quality, and the night sketch showed that going wide and fast makes nothing good. Final quality is now defined first (target frames), reached first in one small place (the beauty corner), and built from parts made one at a time and judged (`docs/visual-direction.md`, How the look is built).
- *The question:* can one corner of the town, in one light, reach the quality of its target frames in Unity, with each house's history, and the ground's, readable at a glance? It moves exit criterion 1, and criterion 3 through the ground: an empty house's yard growing over says how long it has stood empty.
- *How it is judged:* each camera's render beside its target frame, in colour, greyscale and at one-eighth size; Øystein judges. For criterion 1, a cold viewer shown the hero frame says which house is oldest, which was altered, which is neglected and which is kept up, and is right.
- *What would change our minds:* if the corner, built from approved parts, still falls clearly short of its targets in Unity, the gap is in shading, light and air, and that work comes before more parts. If the targets need what cannot run in real time (painted detail everywhere), they are repainted closer to what runs.
- *What it does not test:* drizzle, blue hour and night (next, in the same corner), wet reflections, people, history through the years, scale and frame rate.
- *Where:* the stepped lane from the quay between 104 and 105, authored as its own small fixture and scene, on the seam between two areas: the waterfront and the hill, where the town meets the bare rock it is built on. The lane climbs about 20 m over about 60 m in flights of steps. Halfway up, a knuckle of bedrock on its south side, with a rock face of 4 to 5 m towards the lane, pushes it north, which is why it bends: a landing at about 9 m, then a steeper flight. The houses stand at their own heights on stone plinths that take up the slope, not on flat terraces; E stands on a rock step at about 19 m. Above E the hillside goes on, unbuilt, to the ridge. Steep as it is, there is room between the houses: the quay road at the foot is flat; the landing widens into a small level place, about 8 by 10 m, made with the wall and cut into the rock, where the lane, D's door and C's lamp meet; a narrow alley runs between C and E to the back yards; and every house stands clear of its neighbour. These are where light falls and people stop, and where the assembly rules for space are first set: how a lane meets a door, how flat ground is made on a slope.
- *The five houses,* five histories a viewer should tell apart:
  - A. 104, a cannery owner's house (jugend, 1912), last painted 1949, on the corner of the quay road and the lane. A shop was fitted in its ground floor in 1931 and closed in 1974; in 1988 a young couple opened a café in it, the rent low after the crash. A faded house over a new front: old and changed in one building. Foot of the lane, north side, its long wall along the lane, in sun.
  - B. 105, functionalist (1936, after a fire), painted by new owners in 1987: crisp. Foot of the lane, south side, its north wall to the lane, in shade.
  - C. A cooper's chalet house (1879; 203's diary), cheap new windows in 1971: altered. Above the bend, north side, in sun; the lamp is on its corner and its yard, behind the wall, holds the birch.
  - D. A fisherman's house (classical, 1828; 107's diary), empty and boarded since 1986: neglected. Above the bend, south side, on the bedrock knuckle, its plinth built onto the rock; in shade, with sun through the gap below it.
  - E. 304, the oldest and smallest (classical, 1826), new windows in 1965. At the top, its gable closing the view up the lane, in full low sun.
- *The wall:* a dry-stone retaining wall, about 1.5 to 2 m, along the north side from A to C, holding C's yard and turning with the lane at the landing.
- *The trees:* a birch self-seeded in C's yard around 1950, about 14 m, leaning over the wall into the lane, in sun; a spruce planted behind E in the 1930s, about 18 m, dark against the sky, the frame's vertical.
- *The natural ground,* each part with a date, so it changes with the years like the houses (`docs/research/nature.md`):
  - The bedrock knuckle at the bend: bare grey rock in planes, lichen on its sunny faces sized by the years since it was last scraped (the lane was cut past it in the 1870s), moss on its north side.
  - The hillside above E: coastal heath, grazed by sheep until 1958. Since then heather, juniper and young birch have come in, thickest nearest the old birch, the first step from heath to woodland.
  - D's yard, empty since 1986: three summers of long grass, rosebay willowherb and the first birch seedlings; the path to the door grassing over.
  - The steps: worn smooth and hollow in the middle of the busy lower flight; moss in the joints on the shaded side; grass in the joints of the quieter upper flight.
  - The wall: lichen on its sunny face, moss under the coping and in the shade.
  - In other years the same ground tells the time: in 1935 open heath with sheep, a young spruce and no birch; in 1960 the birch a sapling; in 2020 D's yard a thicket, or cleared if someone bought it.
- *The lamp:* a lantern on a post at the landing, put up in 1955 when the council brought electric light to the lane. Off in this light: here it is a shape; at blue hour it will be the light.
- *The light:* 20 October 1989 at 16:00 (first written as 17:00, as if on summer time; Norway went back to winter time on 24 September 1989), after a day of showers, the cloud breaking in the west. The sun is 8° high in the south-west (234°), the air damp with a little haze off the fjord, the ground damp, not wet. Sun lies on the north side's walls and on E's gable; the lane is in cool shadow from the south side's houses, with sun crossing the steps in bands through the gaps; the birch is lit gold from the side. It shows planes, edges and the two value families at their clearest, it is the light of the trees' lead reference (Firewatch), and it is the favourable light to reach final in first.
- *Built to change:* the corner is a place with a diary, not a set, and the ground's diary is part of it. The town will grow from village to city, and people will open shops, cafés and workshops in old ground floors and close them again. So every part made for the corner is made to fit the assembly rules (its era, its sockets, the range of sizes it must fit), and the generator places it; nothing is placed by hand for one frame. Later, the same cameras show the corner in other years.
- *The cameras are the game's camera.* Every frame is one the player can make with the city view, at its stops, lens and tilt; nothing is staged from where the player cannot go. A composed frame is the best case. The player turns the camera any way, so the corner is built in the round (backs of houses, yards, the hillside) as far as the close stop can see, and a strip of headings shows the honest case. The first values for the camera are in `docs/visual-direction.md` (Open).
- *Target frames* (painted over):
  1. Close stop, up the lane (the hero): over the quay road at about 13 m, just over A's ridge, looking east-south-east. Near: A's sunlit roof and wall, B's shaded corner. Middle: the steps, the landing, the lamp, the birch. Beyond: E's lit gable, the spruce, the heath and the sky. Rendered at the strengths gentle and strong, so the push in proportion is chosen by eye.
  2. Close stop, turned round, into the light: about 13 m over the landing, looking west-north-west down the lane over A's and B's roofs, across the basin to the quay row on the far side, the newer town and the islands beyond, the sun low to the left. The birch backlit, the far row in silhouette against the bright sky, the water glittering.
  3. Middle stop: about 35 m over the water, the horizon in frame, the corner among Harbour Row's roofs with the rock and heath above it.
  4. Far stop: about 120 m, steep and map-like. Does the corner still read (the lane, the rock, the yards, the heath) when the player navigates?
- *Checks* (not painted over): the close stop at eight headings round the landing, whatever way the player turns, each in colour, greyscale and at one-eighth size.
- *A reference Øystein showed (20 October), a lit harbour town at dusk under mountains:* take its areas (a working waterfront on flat ground, a hill of houses on rock, landmarks on the high rock, the land beyond), roads winding round the rock, houses on rock edges, lit working ground floors, trees among the houses. Leave its style (everything lit, detail and clutter everywhere, a rendered sheen) and the fishing-village postcard.
- *Two versions of one view Øystein showed as closer to Stavanger (20 October):* from the old town's lane, over white houses with red tile roofs, a stone wall, a fence and a lantern on a post, across the basin to the lit quay row, the newer town and a tower on a rock knoll beyond, then low islands and the sea. One is detailed and rendered at dusk, the other flat and graphic at sunset. Take the setting (the basin, the old town above it, the town's eras beyond, low islands, mountains only far off), red tile roofs on white houses, the lane's furniture, and the view itself: it is the corner's camera 2. From the graphic version, take few values in planes, shade in the sky's colour, lit windows and their reflections as the only strong accents. Leave the rendered version's sheen and its detail everywhere. Which way the target frames lean, between painterly realism and the graphic version, is Øystein's call (open).
- *Restart of the visuals (Øystein, 20 October):* the first base frames, made from the generator's houses, the night sketch's trees and generated ground, could not be judged: the trees and nature were poor and the houses uninspired. Kept, because they are hidden and work: the diaries, the resolver and its checks, the deterministic export, the corner's facts, the light keyed to the date, the camera stops. Dropped: every visible asset (the tree generator, the ground's look, the houses' boxes for parts) and every frame made from them. The order of judging is now: the corner in clay; target paintings over the clay; one part at a time, judged alone; the corner filling with approved parts.
- *First clay pass (20 October, `reviews/lt1/corner/clay/`):* the layout itself fails. Houses stand scattered on a bare slope like a suburb, not shoulder to shoulder along lanes as in an old town; the landing is cut so deep it reads as a pit; the stepped lane barely reads from the cameras. The corner's layout is next blocked in by hand, at the old town's density, and judged in clay before any target is painted.
- *Block-in by hand (20 October, `blender/blockouts/corner_blockout.py`, previews in `reviews/lt1/corner/blockout/`):* the corner and about ninety houses round it as grey masses, laid out as an old town: rows shoulder to shoulder along two contour lanes that follow the hill's bend, the stepped lane in the stream's gully crossing them at small level places, yards and walls behind, the town thinning to heath at the top; across the basin the quay row and the newer town's blocks. At the middle stop it reads as a dense town for the first time. Found: at a close stop of 13 to 19 m over an old town of narrow lanes, the camera sees only roofs; the lanes vanish. Up a narrow uphill lane, even 9 m gives a canyon of walls. Proposed: the close stop follows the space under it, low over open ground (a quay road, a square, a lane seen along its length) and higher over roofs; and the hero frames look along a lane or down over roofs to the basin, not up a narrow lane.
- *The camera comes down into the town (Øystein, 20 October):* the player must be close enough to see the details, feel the place and move through it. The city view runs from a street stop just above head height in a lane up to the map, following the space under it. The corner's target frames gain a street-level frame at the landing (the wall, the lantern, the steps, C's door, D's rock), and every part is judged at the street stop as well as from the roofs.
- *Decided at step a (Øystein, 20 October):* real proportions, exaggerated, the push chosen by eye in the corner; no drawn outlines, as long as value, colour and edges carry the look; every frame from the game's camera; height difference and a natural ground with its own dated history are part of the corner.
- *The heart in clay (20 October, `blender/blockouts/heart_layout.py`, `heart_build.py`, frames in `reviews/lt1/heart/clay/`):* the town's centre as grey masses on its designed land, from seven game cameras, with every door on a way and every way joined (`reach_check.py`). Øystein: good, though it looks a bit dead and flat, which he puts down to the level of detail; and a wall stands inside Gully Steps. The wall was a level yard whose edge ran 0.3 m into the steps; its retaining wall and pad cut through the lower flight. The check now also proves that nothing stands in a way: no building, yard, wall or shed reaches into a street, lane, stair or path, along its whole outline. That found more: houses on curving lanes stood up to 1.7 m into them (only their corners had been tested), School Lane ran through the cathedral's nave, the Fish Hall stood on the end of Barrel Quay, the cooper's house C stood across High Terrace, and the stairs between the sea houses cut through houses. All fixed: houses step back until their whole front is clear, C is now a downhill house fronting High Terrace with its yard below, the cathedral fronts the square, and the road tunnel under the Bluff has its cutting. Watch Knoll's face no longer steps: the land's solve pinned each drawn line to whole 4 m cells, leaving stairs along diagonal ridges, and a light rounding of the land removes them.
- *Light, air and atmosphere on the clay (20 October, proposed and agreed):*
  - *Why now:* part of the flatness is the light, not the detail. The clay is lit as if every point saw the whole sky: a lane at the foot of two walls gets as much skylight as the open quay, so lanes do not read as spaces; shade on walls is a muddy brown while shade on the ground is a strong blue; the far ground goes pink. No drawn outlines means light and air must carry depth, so they come before parts, and the parts' values are chosen under them.
  - *The question:* can light and air alone give the heart depth and air in clay, at every camera stop and in ordinary light, without hiding a fault of shape? It serves criterion 5 (the look holds at city scale) and the value structure criterion 1 needs (age and neglect read only where light reads).
  - *What is built:* (1) the sun from the date, the hour and the place (59° N, 5.7° E), never typed in; (2) a sky map of the town: its height seen from above, drawn in Unity whenever the town changes, from which each point knows how much sky it sees, so lanes, yards and the feet of walls darken and roofs stay open, at every stop and through every change; (3) shade that takes the colour of the sky it faces (warm towards the sun, cool away from it, blue from overhead) and a warm bounce from sunlit ground; (4) a sky with a low broken cloud layer and a high thin one, lit from the sun's side; (5) air in coloured layers by distance, and a low haze lying on the Basin and the Roads that thins up the hill; (6) a colour script of four hours on 20 October: 09:30, a low sun (13°) in the south-east behind Herring Hill (the old town faces west-north-west, so it stands in its own shade); 13:00, grey drizzle; 16:00, the low sun 8° high in the south-west (first written as 17:00, which assumed summer time; on 20 October 1989 the sun is then 1.6° high and nearly down); 17:50, blue hour, the sun 5° below the horizon; (7) one weather event, sea fog rolling in from the Roads.
  - *How it is judged:* the heart's cameras (seven, and an eighth on Watch Knoll) at each hour, in colour, in greyscale and at one-eighth size, each beside its flat clay frame. A yes: in greyscale at one-eighth, near, middle and far separate in every frame; the water reads as water and the lanes as spaces at every hour; the grey noon looks good, not only the low sun; and Øystein finds the 16:00 frames have depth and air enough to paint the target frames over.
  - *What would change our minds:* if the best hour still reads flat, the flatness is in the shapes and the missing detail, and the target paintings and the first parts come before more light work. If the sky map does not make the lanes read at the street stop, occlusion is baked into each part as well.
  - *What it does not test:* materials and the colours of things, lamps and lit windows (night waits for them), wet reflections, trees, people, and frame rate (measured, not judged).
  - *The bar raised (Øystein, 20 October):* the light and shading must be superb, stunningly beautiful, and the system must carry every atmosphere a story needs: sad, hopeless, gritty, hopeful, grand, success; rain, wind, sun and snow; autumn and spring. So the four hours are not four rigs to keep but the first test points of a light model: the world's facts (date, hour and place; the season; the weather) give the light, and mood is how a scene is shot within them. Proposed order, each judged: the four hours on the clay (this step); the light model, any date, hour and weather to a rig; weather (overcast, drizzle, rain, snow, sea fog, wind); the seasons; moods as grades, judged blind.
  - *Five references Øystein showed for the light (20 October):* a diner in a green forest fog at night, its neon and window the only warm light; a lighthouse's red lamp in teal fog over a wet lane, a small figure, its light in the puddles; a city at blue hour, a vast blue wall and one orange doorway with a figure, a lamp, a taxi; a man in a dark room before a window of towers fading into blue haze; a painted courtyard of grey-teal panel blocks, one lit by low sun, a slit of blue sky, a family below. *What they share, and what we take:* one dominant colour of air (green, teal, blue) that carries the mood, and one warm accent against it (orange, red), and almost nothing else; atmosphere thick enough to lay the distance in flat layers of value, so shapes read as silhouettes stepping back; light that means people (a lit window, a doorway, a sign, a lamp), small against large dark masses; that light again in wet ground; big simple masses with detail only where the light is; night, blue hour and fog more than sunshine; grain and soft glow round the lights. *What we leave:* the graphic flatness of the illustrations (our look stays painterly realism), signs and neon of other places and times, the empty cities (our town is lived in), and the surreal light trails of the last. *What it changes:* the light model must be art-directed hard on top of the physics: the physics says where light comes from and what is lit; the art direction chooses the palette. - *A style for the game, after the masters of colour (Øystein, 20 October: stylise more, exaggerate, keep it truly stunning; look to the masters of colour in painting and film):*
  - *The question:* how far can colour, value and air be pushed beyond the light the world gives and stay stunning and true? The candidates: *nocturne* (Whistler, Jansson, Grimshaw: the air is the picture, close values, lights as points of gold), *luminous* (Shinkai, Oga, Mary Blair, Firewatch: saturated air, violet and blue shade against coral and gold, glow round every light), *painted* (Sorolla, Sargent, Sohlberg, Hopper: painterly realism pushed, full-colour shade, value masses, deep saturated darks).

- *The first part, trees: a tree system (20 October, planned):* Øystein: everything is blocky; parts fix it; make a system for trees (trunks, branches, leaves, species, seasons), mindful of time and of other cities, looking at how other games and tools build them, in our style. The rock follows.

  - *The growth timeline's first runs (20 October):* `blender/generators/tree_growth.py` grows a spruce and a birch year by year, read as side-on sketches at 5, 15, 30, 60 and 100 years. What they taught:
    - The paper's shadow pyramid lights a crown only from above, so an open-grown spruce shed every lower limb and stood as a pole with a tuft. Under this coast's overcast skies the lower crown lives on side light: each bud now looks along 26 rays at an overcast sky (the CIE luminance) through a grid of its foliage and of the site's houses and walls, and grows towards where its light comes from. With that, the spruce is a full cone to the ground at 15 and 30, as an open-grown one is.
    - A seedling's shoots are centimetres long: shoots and the light's grid scale with the tree until it is grown, or the young tree cannot fit a branch inside its crown.
    - Droop added along a limb makes its far end hang; real limbs sag at the base and turn up at the tip. Each order steers towards its set-point angle (the gravitropic set-point), different at the crown's top and foot.
    - A branch is shed when its buds have been starved for years running, by the species' shade tolerance (spruce tolerant, birch not), or when it has no buds left; light per internode starved every long conifer limb.
    - Heights are the place's, not a forest record: each species' top height is a good, sheltered site's on this coast (spruce 30 m, downy birch 16 m), scaled by the site's quality (a birch on a knoll in the sea wind: 6.4 m at 60 years; in a garden: 16 m), and the crown keeps to that height curve all its life.     - The timeline stops where the hand-made parts take over (the spruce at its second order, the birch at its fourth): the needle sprays and twigs beyond are parts. A 100-year spruce grows in under two minutes, a 60-year birch in twelve seconds.
    - Every crown fills its placeholder envelope (a cone, an ovate solid) as one even mass: a perfect geometric cone, a lemon, with no medium shapes and no sky between the tiers, the opposite of our shape rules. The crown masses, made by hand in our style, are the envelope that gives the medium shapes; they are the next part. Øystein: every part follows the same stylised look and proportions as the houses and lamps; trees take the houses' push dial (`eras.py`, `stronger`), and what it means for a tree is found in the look test before it is written as a rule.
