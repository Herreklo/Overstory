# The town plan

The first town: its land, its areas, how they meet and what happens in each. It is planned here and laid out in clay before any area is built at final quality, one area at a time (`docs/visual-direction.md`, How the look is built). The town is fictional. Its structure is Stavanger's, taken as evidence: a harbour basin with quays on both sides, an old wooden town on the slope, warehouses turned pubs, a market square at the basin's head with the cathedral and a lake, a tower on a knoll, a newer town in its eras, an oil base at the mouth, villas above, a wooded hill with a water tower, low islands in the fjord. Real maps (OpenStreetMap) are the reference for lane networks, plot widths and density; nothing is copied street for street.

The plan is part of the setting pack. It sets the land and the building history. Who runs a pub, who lives where and what opens or closes are the engine's facts; until the engine supplies them, fixtures author them, and the plan only says what a place offers (cheap old warehouses by the water draw bars, as Jacobs and Zukin found).

## The first of many

This is the starter town. Later come other towns, bigger cities and metropolises on the scale of London or New York, each its own setting pack on the same engine and the same look. The starter town is where the method is proved, so nothing in it may be hard-coded to a timber harbour town.

**What carries over to every later place:**
- the method: the land, a script of eras, areas with their own grain, a heart laid out by hand in clay, districts round it grown by rules and checked in clay;
- the look's rules, light, air and shading (`docs/visual-direction.md`);
- the part system: families of parts that connect by the same rules, with each region bringing its own families (brick terraces, tenements, stone, steel and glass);
- the engine's growth: booms and busts, plot cycles, fringe belts, plans partly carried out;
- the camera and its stops, and the tools (export, checks, sheets).

**What each new place brings:** its land, its script of eras, its part families, its areas, its heart laid out by hand.

**What a metropolis adds, to be designed for now and built later:** towers and deep blocks, so the camera needs stops for height and streets that are canyons; transit in layers (metro, elevated rail, stations as nodes); many districts, each with its own heart laid out by hand and its body grown by rules; crowds by the thousand (`docs/living-world.md`, Crowds); streaming and levels of detail so a whole city holds its frame rate. The starter town is built so these can be added without starting again: the town is data in a setting pack, parts are families, and the city view is built to stream.

**Its place in the world.** The starter town has a latitude, longitude and time zone (Stavanger's: 58.97°N, 5.73°E, Central European Time), and its light is computed from them, so every later city's light is a fact of where it stands. Its routes out (the ferry quay, the station, the airport, the motorway) are the doors to a world map later (`docs/ideas/world-map.md`).

## Size

The town is the size of Stavanger in 1989: about 95,000 people over a built area of about 5 by 4 km, with farms, a beach and the fjord at its edges. Its centre, around the basin and about 1.3 km across, is planned area by area below; the town around it is planned by district (The town around the centre). The centre is laid out by hand in clay. The districts around it are laid out by rules learned from the centre and from the real map, and checked in clay from the far stop. Exit criterion 5 (a street at city scale holds its look and frame rate) is tested on the whole town, not on the corner.

## What the plan must do

- **Give every exit criterion its places.** Buildings whose history reads (the old town, the warehouses turned pubs); consequences the page does not make obvious (the empty shops after the 1986 crash, the closing canning works, the brewery standing empty); scenes (a pub, the square on market day, the forest, the ferry quay); the band's ladder of venues.
- **Read from the camera.** Lynch's five elements must all be there and visible: paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks.
- **Contrast.** Each area has its own grain (what stands there, how close, how high, on what ground), its own sound and its own light. Moving between them is the pleasure of the town.
- **Sightlines.** The best views are planned: from the old town over the roofs across the basin to the pub quay (the corner's camera 2); from the knoll over the whole town; along the pub quay to the water; from the forest's edge over the roofs to the fjord.
- **Grow.** It is built in clay one area at a time, and it extends to a larger town later without being redrawn.

## The land

The harbour basin, Vågen, is the town's heart and its biggest space: about 900 m long, 180 to 260 m wide in its outer part and narrowing to about 90 m at its head, where the square is. It runs north to south with a gentle bend in the middle, so no view shows all of it at once and every walk along it opens new views. Its mouth opens north into the fjord, past a breakwater and a lighthouse; in its outer part stands a small rock islet with a beacon. The outer basin is deep (ferries, express boats, supply ships); the inner basin is shallow (fishing boats, small craft, pontoons, the fish quay at the head). Both shores are stone quays with steps down to the water, slipways and mooring rings; the quays are where both shores' lives meet the water. The old town climbs the east shore's slope to villas and a wooded hill (about 80 m) with a water tower on top. The west shore is flatter and lower, with the knoll (about 25 m of bare rock) rising behind the pub streets. North of the basin, across the mouth, the land runs out to the oil base, then low islands and skerries in the fjord. Mountains only far off to the east.

The old town faces west-north-west across the water: the evening sun lies on its fronts, and from it the sun sets behind the pub quay. The pub quay faces east-south-east: morning sun, and at night its lit windows lie in the water, seen from the old town.

The town's history, which sets which eras stand where: a medieval cathedral town at the basin's head; the herring boom of the early 1800s, when the wooden old town filled the east slope; fires, after which the west shore was rebuilt in straight streets (the fire grid); canning from the 1870s, its works at the mouth; villas for the merchants above; the modernist renewal of the 1950s to 70s, which cleared part of the old centre for the newer town and was stopped by conservation in the 1970s; the oil boom from the 1970s; the crash of 1986.

## Paths, edges, nodes and landmarks

- **Paths:** the quay roads on both shores, meeting at the square; the pub street; the main street from the square into the newer town; the old town's contour lanes and stepped lanes; the road up through the villas to the water tower; the forest paths.
- **Edges:** the basin (the strongest); the forest's edge; the oil base's fence; the lake.
- **Nodes:** the square; the landing in the old town (the beauty corner); the ferry quay; the fish quay; the bus station's square.
- **Landmarks:** the cathedral; the tower on the knoll; the water tower on the forest hill; the oil base's cranes; the lighthouse; the canning works' chimney.

## A city to play in

The town must make many games, not one: the band, a murder mystery, a family across generations, a venture, talent spotting, the town's politics, growing up. What makes a game city exciting, drawn from games (Yakuza's Kamurocho, Disco Elysium's Revachol, Shadows of Doubt, Night in the Woods, Pentiment's Tassing, Kentucky Route Zero) and from real towns (Jacobs, Gehl, Hillier):

- **Dense with purpose.** Small and full beats large and empty. Every block of the centre gives some scenario a reason to go there: a third place, a workplace, a secret. The districts round it are visited for reasons, not crossed for distance.
- **Everyone passes the same few places.** The square, the quays, the bus station and the bridge are where routines cross, so chance meetings happen and are true (who walks where comes from the engine).
- **Places change with the clock.** The square by day and empty at night; the pub quay shabby by day and alive at night; the beach in summer; the lake frozen for skating in a cold winter; the market on Saturdays. One map is many towns.
- **Front and back.** Every place worth a scene has a front and a backstage: the pub's front room and its back yard, the villa's drawing room and its kitchen door, the cannery's office and its loading bay. Drama lives in the back.
- **Edges and margins.** What happens out of sight happens at the edges: the forest, the boatyards, the empty brewery, under the bridge, the oil base at night, the end of the breakwater. Crime, romance and youth need them.
- **Views and climbs.** Stairs, lookouts and the bridge orient the player and reward the climb: the knoll, the water tower, the lighthouse, the corner's landing.
- **Contrast is conflict.** Villas above Storhaug; the conserved old town against the oil offices; the seventies estate against the fishing village. Social tension made spatial gives every scenario its stakes.
- **Secrets with dates.** The history system becomes play: the old name under the new sign, the wall under the trees, the bricked-up door, the moss that has not grown back. Following an anomaly leads to a true story.
- **Recognition.** The same places, years apart, changed by what happened: the strongest moment any scenario has.

## How the town grows and changes

**Before play: a script of eras.** The town is grown from a timed script (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`), each era leaving its layer where it could: the medieval core at the basin's head; the herring boom filling the east slope with timber houses and the quays with warehouses; fires, and the fire grid rebuilt in straight streets; canning at the mouth; the railway along the lake; villas above the old town; between the wars the garden suburb; the modernist renewal of the 1950s and 60s, which cleared part of the centre for the newer town and was stopped when the old town was saved; oil from the 1970s, with the base, the offices, the bridge and the motorway; the boom of the early 80s, when the pub quay's warehouses became bars; the crash of 1986. The town in 1989 is all of these at once.

**How growth finds its place.** Growth follows roads and stalls at barriers: the basin, the lake, the railway, the forest's edge. Where it stalls, belts of big, low-density uses gather (Conzen's fringe belts): the hospital, the school, the cemetery, the football ground, the university. Plots run through their cycle (built, filled in behind, decayed, cleared, rebuilt). Booms build in their own style; busts leave empty shops and lots. Plans are only partly carried out, and their scars stay.

**During play: change on many clocks.** Each layer changes at its own speed (Brand): crowds, posters and cars by the hour and the week; businesses by the year; paint and cladding by the decade; buildings and streets by the generation; the land by the century. What drives it is the engine's facts: the economy (a boom builds, a bust empties), people (families grow and move out, the young move in), ventures (cheap old space draws artists, then bars, then offices), plans (a bridge opens an island, a road cuts a district), accidents (a fire, a storm) and nature (trees grow, an abandoned yard grows over, the forest takes back pasture).

**Expansion.** The town grows outward into a region that exists before it: farms with their walls and lanes, a fishing village, forest, heath, coast. The land is never spawned when the town reaches it; it is already there, with its own history, and the town grows over it. What it grows over survives as traces: a farm lane becomes a street and keeps its bend, a field wall becomes a garden boundary, the farmhouse stands among the villas, the fishing village's harbour and chapel become a district's heart. The ways a town expands, each a dated event:
- *along roads:* houses strung out along the road out of town, then filled in behind;
- *as one planned estate:* a whole district laid out at once by a plan, often only partly built;
- *by infrastructure:* a railway's station seeds a suburb, a bridge opens an island, a motorway draws offices to the edge;
- *by absorbing villages:* the town reaches a village and takes it in, and the village keeps its centre;
- *by new land:* quays pushed out, land filled into the water, forest cleared;
- *inward:* infill, taller buildings near the centre, plots merged for big blocks in booms.

Expansion stalls at barriers and leaves fringe belts, and the edge is always the newest and rawest place: half-built streets, fields with surveyors' stakes, a farm waiting to be sold. Who grows and how fast comes from the engine (people needing homes, jobs moving, booms and busts); the layout of a new district is generated once, at the event, from rules learned from the hand-made heart and from real places, and stored as facts, so it is the same on every load. Research behind it: growing in eras (Emilien et al. 2012), road networks that grow (Beneš et al. 2014), cities in four dimensions (Weber et al. 2009), parcels (Vanegas et al. 2012), Conzen's fringe belts (`docs/research/generating-the-past.md`).

**From town to metropolis.** A city is the same process run longer and on more seeds: a region of villages and farms that a town grows over, until the villages are districts and each keeps its heart. So later cities are planned as regions first, their hearts are the old villages laid out by hand, and their bodies are grown.

**What the player sees.** The same views across the years: a scenario set in 1960 and one in 2010 meet the same quay, the same corner, the same view from the knoll, each changed by what happened in between.

**Who decides.** These are facts, so the engine's fabric pass owns them (`docs/engine-requests.md`); the plan supplies the setting pack's land and its script of eras. The same machinery grows other towns and, later, cities, each with its own land and script.

## Changing the town in the machine

Change is cheap as long as one rule holds: everything that can change is a dated fact, and the geometry is rebuilt from the facts. A house is never deleted; its diary says `demolished`, and the next build leaves it out.

| Change | How | Status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repainted, extended, new windows, a business opens or closes | An event in the building's diary; parts from that decade | Works (`blender/resolver`) |
| Demolished, an empty lot, a new building | `demolished` then `built` on the same plot; the lot's state (gravel car park, garden) | Works in the resolver; lot states to add |
| Plots merged or split | A diary for the plot | Designed (`docs/research/world-that-remembers.md`), not built |
| A street laid out, widened or cut through | A diary for the street; the plots along it change; the ground re-cut locally | Designed, not built |
| New land: a quay pushed out, land filled, forest cleared, farmland built on | A diary for the land; the ground recomputed from land and cuts | Not built; the same machinery as streets |
| Any of these during play | Unity assembles buildings from parts at runtime and rebuilds only the chunk that changed | Not built: buildings are still meshed in Blender per year |

What keeps change local: layout is data in the setting pack, buildings are assembled from parts, the ground is computed from the land and its cuts, and the world is cut into chunks by plot. What would make it hard, and is avoided: geometry placed by hand in the shipped game (hand block-ins are references, then turned into facts), baked lighting, and meshes that span many plots. Until the engine's fabric pass models buildings and land, the dated facts come from fixtures.

**Early tests, before the town grows:** the corner across four dates, rebuilt live in Unity from its facts, including a house demolished and replaced, two plots merged, and the quay pushed out into the basin; it proves runtime assembly and local rebuilds. Then the town's edge from the far stop in 1935, 1960, 1989 and 2020, in clay: farms becoming a garden suburb, the estate laid out, the bridge opening the island; it proves expansion and the traces it leaves.

## The order in clay

1. **The corner**, refined (the old town around the landing).
2. **The heart:** the basin, both shores and the square: the old town's lower slope, Harbour Row, the pub quay and pub street, the fire grid's first blocks, the knoll. The corner's camera 2 then looks at a real pub quay.
3. **Above:** the villas and the forest hill, with the heath and the water tower.
4. **The rest of the centre:** the canning works, Storhaug, the newer town, the oil base, the ferry quay, the islands.
5. **The districts around it,** laid out by rules learned from the centre and checked in clay from the far stop: first the lake and the railway, the garden suburb and the bridge, then the others.

Each step is judged in clay at the middle and far stops before the next. Parts are made at final quality in parallel and fill the corner first.
