# Lives and families across time

Ideas, not decisions: no game is chosen (`NOW.md`). Every idea is catalogued in `README.md`; this page holds the full detail of this family's ideas.

## In short

- **A family across generations** is one idea in several forms: a life and its heirs (option 4 of `first-game-options.md`, the role-playing game), the acorn, a child growing up, Up the Hill with Class of '89 and the living board, and the anthology and its relay.
- **The house itself** watches families come and go, and can only notice and nudge.
- These games share a long clock: acts years apart, a town that goes on between them, and a return that shows what changed and why.
- An idea marked *(old game)* starts from an older game that reached for something its tech or its time could not give. The details of the old games are from memory and are checked before any idea is built on them.

Costs are rough weeks of the weekly usage limit, the plan's own unit, before the architecture. The criteria are the five in `CLAUDE.md` (What it is for). Claims about other games are sourced in `first-game-options.md` (Sources).

## A family across generations

### A life and its heirs (option 4)

**The game.** You play one resident, born around 1971 into a family in the old town, through acts years apart. When your character dies, or the next generation comes of age, you go on as a child: Persona's calendar inside an act, Pentiment's jumps between, Crusader Kings' succession. This is the family across generations, played as a role-playing game.

**Core loop.** An act is a season of evenings. Each evening is spent on a person or a place: your father at the Anchor, a friend's band, overtime, the house. What you do is an event, remembered by whoever saw it. Between acts the world runs, and you return to what your choices and other lives made.

**Dials.** Control: one person's acts (work, evenings, moving, the house's works). Influence: family and friends, through what they see you do. Knowledge: a resident's, with the family's own stories, which may be wrong.

**Time.** Acts of a season, years apart (1989, 1995, 2003, 2011, 2020); generations across the game.

**The game in it.** Without set goals this is The Sims, which Juul counts as borderline. So every act carries a dated goal.
- *Goal:* each act's: the mortgage due in March, a parent who needs care, a job to keep, a child through school. Across the game, a family and a house that last.
- *Resources:* a season's evenings, money, health, goodwill.
- *Opponents and pressure:* the calendar, the town's economy (the cannery, oil), rivals at work, the family's own wants.
- *Fail states:* the house sold, a bond broken, a parent left alone, debts, the family gone from the town.
- *Mastery:* seeing what each person needs before they say it; spending evenings where they count; planning a decade ahead.

**Needs.**
- *World:* interiors (step 8, pulled forward); people ageing on screen (step 5); the family house changed live by its works.
- *Engine:* the player's character with many commands (none exist yet); homes (#59); facts as dated intervals (33); body and work over a life (34); rites with who came (31); wants for later life (round 43); the two clocks settled, so an evening commits back to the month.
- *Interface:* a calendar, relationships as acts with counts, a family journal.

**Only here.** Your own traces in a street you watch age. A parent growing old, and a child who looks like you. A consequence that lands a decade later where you can see it.

**Risks.** The largest scope. The engine's own weakness hits it hardest: thirty years move a person only about 3% toward being someone else, and nearly half of adults pursue nothing in ten years (Understory's summary). Written scenes make conversation the expensive part.

**Cost on top of the plan.** 24 to 40 weeks.

**Criteria.** 1 strong (your own house); 2 strongest; 3 strong; 4 some; 5 little.

**Greybox.** The family in house C: six evenings, then a jump to 1995. Do players know their family again from silhouette, place and habit? Does an evening spent with someone show later?

**Is there a role-playing game here?** Yes: this one. The role-playing games bring four things the town can use. Time is a budget (Persona, Pathologic); consequences come late and visibly (The Witcher 3); companions have goals and judge you (Divinity); one dense place lasts across eras (Yakuza). They also bring a cost the town can't pay: reactivity authored line by line (Baldur's Gate 3). Ours comes from the engine's memory of who saw what. Any of the six options can also be played as a role with a body in the town; the dials change, not the world. The paper playtest's role concept is in `roles.md` (A role in the town).

### The family saga, followed

The same family idea seen from outside, as one of four games the real run supports (`stories-in-the-world.md`).

**The loop.** Follow a household across decades: who leaves for the city, who comes home for the funeral and stays, who sells the house.

**What carries it.** Homes (request 15); facts as intervals (33); things that recur with their people. A family saga needs homes first.

### The acorn *(old game)*

From *Fable*'s acorn, promised and never shipped (2004), and *Harvest Moon: A Wonderful Life* (2003). Run a small place, a boatyard or a café, from 1989 to 2030. What you did stays: the tree you planted, the extension, the kids you coached who now run the town. It sharpens option 4. Its place is also a venture (`groups.md`, The venture).

### A child growing up

**The loop.** Live from 1989 into adulthood. The town looks big, then small. Family and friends' lives shape yours.

**What it uses.** Lives, the family saga from inside, time.

**Fit today (world 9989).** Medium: a child's view needs people close up (a later step).

**Risks.** Heavy on people and animation.

**Nearest games.** Night in the Woods, Boyhood.

What the town must give growing up (the beach, Watch Knoll at night, the bus station, the cinema, the youth club, the ferry to the islands) is in `docs/town-plan.md` (A city to play in).

### Up the Hill: the ambitious Jones *(old game)*

From *Jones in the Fast Lane* (1990). Øystein, 22 October: "is there a jones in the fast lane varioation, but much more ambitous".

- *The geography is the ladder.* You start in Tin Town; the goal you can see is a house on Crest Lane in the Heights. Where you live sets your school, contacts and commute in hours.
- *Jones is the whole town.* You compete with simulated people for the apprenticeship, the flat, the partner. The engine decides and remembers; it already records the one not chosen for a place.
- *The week, then the years.* Close up, Jones's week of hours; then a jump, with the family run on from your choices.
- *Generations.* When you are old you play your child. The score is how far up the hill the family got over three generations, and what the town remembers of it.
- *The era is the market.* Oil rising, the cannery dying. Reading which streets are on the way up (scaffolding, fresh paint, skips) is the skill that pays.
- *Multiplayer.* Two to four families, orders given at the same time as in Diplomacy, so two of you can want the same job. Marriages between families; one player's café is where another's kids first work.
- *Needs:* family background, class, upbringing and several schools, none of which Understory has yet (its limit 8); homes (#59); a player's commands. An origin is evidence, never a verdict (`docs/town-plan.md`, TP6).

**Class of '89** *(old game)*, its simple form. Four players grow up in different neighbourhoods (`docs/town-plan.md`, TP6) and race to a life goal across decades, in a real town economy. Simple rules, multiplayer from the start.

**The living board: Monopoly that remembers.** A connection of Up the Hill, the restorer and the wager (`fun.md`; `docs/research/playtesting.md`). Families buy houses and businesses where value comes from real lives: a school opens, the cannery shuts, old money moves in. You win by reading which streets will rise. Your speculation evicts tenants who remember you, and their kids grow up. A campaign over game nights, as in legacy board games (*Risk Legacy*, 2011; *Pandemic Legacy*, 2015), the board-game genre closest to our engine: the town keeps every scar between sessions.

### The anthology and the relay

**The many-lives anthology.** Short chapters as different people in the same town, years apart. A choice made as one person becomes another's background. The street you left is the street you return to: the return to the first rung, at the scale of a whole town. In the interface study it is one of the games the three dials set: knowledge per character, the journal marks what was learned in another life, and the player's acts are never marked in the world (`docs/research/interface-and-inspection.md`, section 7). The experience panel's hybrid of the role and the family is an anthology too: each chapter with a goal and a temptation, each living with what the last one did (`README.md`, Combinations).

**Relay: the town as a chain letter.** Friends each play one decade of the same town and pass it on (a connection of option 4 and the anthology). The next player starts by reading what you did from the streets, not from a summary. You can leave secrets: a hidden debt, a box under the floor, a lie in the paper. Each is scored on how well they read the last. It needs no one online at the same time, and it tests criteria 1 and 4 directly.

**Heirs: friends inherit one town.**
- *Pitch.* Friends take turns playing one town, each a generation later, and inherit what the last player did. Each heir's first act is to walk the town and read what changed.
- *The moment.* You find your friend's broken promise in your grandmother's diary.
- *Why it works.* Boatmurdered's handover, where each player blamed the last, made true by the engine.
- *Engine.* Saves that carry the player's history, player commands, generations.
- *Weakness.* The longest path of the seven games built on reading a world (`first-game-options.md`, section 7).
- *Cheapest test.* Two players, one fixture town, one handover with a written note. Is the second player's walk interesting?

Inquest's hand-over (`inquest.md`, Other shapes) is the same chain with a death in it: one player causes the death without knowing it, by living a year in the town, and a friend investigates it thirty years on.

## The house itself

**The loop.** Play as a house across generations. You can't act, only notice and nudge, as families come and go.

**What it uses.** Time, the fabric, the family saga.

**Fit today (world 9989).** Strong as an art piece.

**Risks.** It may be an experience rather than a game.

**Nearest games.** What Remains of Edith Finch, Here.
