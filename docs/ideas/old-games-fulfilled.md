# Old games, fulfilled

Ideas, not decisions: no game is chosen (`first-game-options.md`). Øystein, 22 October: "i feel like there are so much more ideas. like think very nostalgic. any games that because of tech or something else that did not fulfill its potential"; "is there a jones in the fast lane varioation, but much more ambitous"; "Now look at connections - could we combine stuff for a truly unique but potential amazing experience?".

Each idea starts from an older game that reached for something its tech or its time could not give. A town of developing people whose history stays is the missing part in each. Details of the old games are from memory and are checked before any idea is built on.

Before any of these is chosen, gameplay needs much more research: balance, difficulty and engagement (Øystein: "we can do it later"). `docs/research/fun.md`, `play-craft.md` and `playtesting.md` cover fun, feel and testing. Balance and long-term engagement depend on the game, so they are studied once one is picked.

## The ideas

1. **The Week** (*The Last Express*, 1997; *Majora's Mask*, 2000). One week in 1989 repeats, and something terrible happens on Sunday. Each loop you learn more routines and use them to change the ending. Understory can copy a world and run it on (`clone`, `whatif`), so the loops are exact. A replayed week is dense, which answers the arithmetic risk in `fun.md` (about one notable event a week).
2. **Shenmue, fulfilled** (*Shenmue*, 1999). The harbour town of 1986 had routines for everyone, and the town was only a backdrop. Here you arrive looking for someone who vanished years ago: odd jobs at the harbour, asking around, people who remember you and talk to each other. The town is the plot.
3. **The acorn** (*Fable*'s acorn, promised and never shipped, 2004; *Harvest Moon: A Wonderful Life*, 2003). Run a small place, a boatyard or a café, from 1989 to 2030. What you did stays: the tree you planted, the extension, the kids you coached who now run the town. It sharpens option 4.
4. **The movement** (*Republic: The Revolution*, 2003). A country of individually simulated citizens was promised, and the game shipped late and cut down. Here you start with nobody and win the 1991 council election, recruiting person by person through who knows whom, across neighbourhoods of different class. Multiplayer: rival candidates.
5. **Casing** (*Thief*, 1998; *Hitman*). Stealth needs routines, but those cities were stages. Here you read from the street which houses are empty, what is worth taking and who would notice. The town remembers: a new lock, a dog, a neighbourhood-watch sticker. Multiplayer: a crew, or one side plays the police.
6. **Class of '89** (*Jones in the Fast Lane*, 1990). Four players grow up in different neighbourhoods (`docs/town-plan.md`, TP6) and race to a life goal across decades, in a real town economy. Simple rules, multiplayer from the start.
7. **The paper round** (*Paperboy*, 1985). You are 13 in 1989 with a paper round. The route is the daily loop, and you see everything: who stopped taking the paper, whose car is outside at six. Over the year, a mystery only a kid would notice.
8. **One of them is a player** (*Space Station 13*, 2003). One player lives as a resident among hundreds of simulated ones, keeping a believable routine, while the others hunt them with the casebook (`casebook-detective.md`).
9. **Up the Hill**, the ambitious Jones:
   - *The geography is the ladder.* You start in Tin Town; the goal you can see is a house on Crest Lane in the Heights. Where you live sets your school, contacts and commute in hours.
   - *Jones is the whole town.* You compete with simulated people for the apprenticeship, the flat, the partner. The engine decides and remembers; it already records the one not chosen for a place.
   - *The week, then the years.* Close up, Jones's week of hours; then a jump, with the family run on from your choices.
   - *Generations.* When you are old you play your child. The score is how far up the hill the family got over three generations, and what the town remembers of it.
   - *The era is the market.* Oil rising, the cannery dying. Reading which streets are on the way up (scaffolding, fresh paint, skips) is the skill that pays.
   - *Multiplayer.* Two to four families, orders given at the same time as in Diplomacy, so two of you can want the same job. Marriages between families; one player's café is where another's kids first work.
   - *Needs:* family background, class, upbringing and several schools, none of which Understory has yet (its limit 8); homes (#59); a player's commands. An origin is evidence, never a verdict (TP6).

## Connections

Each joins ideas above with the six options, the casebook and the other idea pages.

- **The long inquest: time is your lever.** A death in 1989 that the town will not talk about (option 6, the casebook). You can investigate now, or jump years ahead. The man people feared dies and tongues loosen; letters surface in an estate sale; there is a deathbed account. But the house was renovated, the witness emigrated, and memories fade and drift. Choosing *when* to look is the core decision, and no other game has a town that truly goes on living. Multiplayer: two investigators in different decades, as in *The Past Within* (Rusty Lake, 2022). What the 1989 player files, photographs or tells someone is what the 2009 player can find.
- **Relay: the town as a chain letter.** Friends each play one decade of the same town and pass it on (option 4; the many-lives anthology in `worlds-on-screen.md`). The next player starts by reading what you did from the streets, not from a summary. You can leave secrets: a hidden debt, a box under the floor, a lie in the paper. Each is scored on how well they read the last. It needs no one online at the same time, and it tests criteria 1 and 4 directly.
- **The Week, together.** Two to four players live the same looping week as different residents (the postman, the barmaid, the kid, the priest), each seeing a slice, and pool the notebook between loops. Optionally one player causes Sunday, loops too and learns too, as the invader does in *Deathloop* (2021): cat and mouse across loops.
- **The living board: Monopoly that remembers.** Families buy houses and businesses where value comes from real lives: a school opens, the cannery shuts, old money moves in (Up the Hill, option 3, the wager in `fun.md`). You win by reading which streets will rise. Your speculation evicts tenants who remember you, and their kids grow up. A campaign over game nights, as in legacy board games (*Risk Legacy*, 2011; *Pandemic Legacy*, 2015), the board-game genre closest to our engine: the town keeps every scar between sessions.
- **The youth team.** You coach the town's under-15s in 1989, kids from the Heights and from Tin Town (option 1, `talent-discovery.md`, TP6's schools and stadium). You find talent where nobody looks. Over twenty years you see who made it, who didn't, and who never forgave you for dropping them.
- **The ghost** (new; *Murdered: Soul Suspect*, 2014, wasted its premise; *Ghost Trick*, 2010). You died in 1989. You cannot act, only be near and nudge: a draught that opens a letter, a light that flickers. Years pass, and grief moves through your family and the house (`stories-in-the-world.md`, A death, and grief). The goal: the truth found before the last person who knows it dies.

What the strongest share: time as a lever (loops, jumps, relays), reading as the skill, and records that can be wrong. Those are what only this engine has. They also share most of their systems: inspection and the casebook, jumps, records and sifting.
