# The experience panel: feeling, pacing and wonder

Panel input for paper playtests round 1 (`../method.md`), written 22 October 2026 on Opus 5.5 by the experience designer, before any session ran. Numbers about world 9989 come from my own queries of `fixtures/engine/understory-world-9989.json`, re-dated by −37. Everything here is a hypothesis for the sessions and the greybox.

## The lens

I look after the feeling a session leaves, the rhythm that brings a player back, and the wonder of a world that was there before you. None of them may become the game. Journey's wonder works because you are climbing toward the mountain; Outer Wilds' because the sun explodes in 22 minutes and knowledge is all you keep. Beauty is what you see while trying to do something hard. So each concept gets one question first: what is the player trying to do while the world shows them its history?

## What the run says about pacing

- A household's year holds about 13 events (median 12), half of them private. Ben Reed's and Olive Shaw's 1989 holds 16 between them; only 8 are local or public, things anyone outside could know.
- Across the whole town, about 54 events a year are both visible and of notability 1,000 or more: one a week. At 1,500 or more it is 18 a year. A corner of five households gets about two of those a year.
- In 46 of the 120 months, nothing of notability 2,000 or more happens anywhere in town.
- 61 per cent of events have no earlier event among their causes, and 76 per cent are never cited as a cause. About 8 per cent sit in chains three or more deep, some 20 a year. The deepest is eleven events of one feud over the skipper's job, 1990 to 1998, whose lines keep saying "Nobody was surprised any more". Arcs loop; they rarely turn.
- The cast holds: about 58 of the 76 people in town in 1999 were there in 1989.

Two rules follow. A game played week by week on one street is a walking simulator by arithmetic: the street is quiet most weeks, so the player waits or the game fakes it. Survivors choose a territory (the whole town) or a clock (months and years, with jumps) that fits the engine's density. And the climax must come from the game's structure (a deadline, an inquest, an edition), because the engine's stories plateau.

## 1. The club (football)

*Loop.* Week in town (scout, visit, two training evenings) → team sheet → Saturday match → the Anchor after → next week. Seasons run March to October; winter is the jump.

*Rhythm.* A session is a match week. Tension builds from Monday: who is on shifts, who is ill, whose child is christened on Saturday. It peaks at the team sheet and breaks in the match; the Anchor releases it and seeds the next week. The jump should land on the first training evening in March, under the floodlight, as a roll call. Who came back is the news.

*The game.* Goal: the board's target, then promotion. Stakes: the club, your job, promises to players. Fail: relegation, the sack, a forfeit, the club folded. Mastery runs from reacting to absences, to forecasting them, to building a squad years ahead. The arc is belonging.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* At hour 1 the wonder is small and real: the centre-back is the sawmill man you saw at the gate on Tuesday. By hour 10 a life has cost you a player (storm shifts, a new baby). By hour 100 the squad has aged out. World 9989 has 20 men aged 16 to 40 in 1989, 16 of them past 30; by 1994 it has four aged 16 to 35, and the run has seven births in ten years. A squad of real lives is a veterans' side that dies within five seasons. Wonder wears off first in the match, once the result is a roll the town didn't touch.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* The clearest start of the seven. The chore is the town as a commute between matches. The sheet repeats emptily when absences arrive unannounced, because then a cause changes nothing.

*Best at each part.* Football Manager owns the match week and scouting by ranges; Stardew Valley a town's learnable week. Take FM's ranges as evidence; beat it only on lives you can watch. Leave the match alone.

*Only here, and lives off.* The squad's week is the town's life, readable in the street before it reaches the sheet. Lives off keeps the absences and drops their causes. I expect the smallest gap of the five arms here, because the peak is what the town feeds least.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Lives make forecasts. Measure: Saturday availability predicted, scored by arm. Kill: under 15 points between arms.
2. The town half holds. Measure: pull in town rounds against match rounds. Kill: town rounds two points lower in both arms.
3. The winter jump lands. Measure: at the roll call, who is missing and why. Kill: under one in three right.

## 2. The band

*Loop.* Hear music through a window → invite (accepted or refused, with a reason) → rehearsals in the loft (step in or stay out) → a gig at the Anchor → the night after (talk, posters, a row) → rent and the next booking. An 18-month album cycle is an act.

*Rhythm.* A session runs from rehearsal to gig. Rehearsals build a tension the player can't fully see, because chemistry is hidden. The gig releases it, read through the crowd as one body; the night after brings consequences. The jump should land at the Anchor's poster wall: your band's poster under three newer ones, and in the front row the one you didn't pick.

*The game.* Goal: the members' own ambition, set with them. Stakes: the band, money, members' lives. Fail: the split, debt, the loft lost, a member damaged by the push. Mastery: reading chemistry before it shows as a row, booking the right night, knowing when to stay out. The arc is a family made by choice, and its fracture.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1 has the best first find of any concept: a guitar through a window at midnight, and a stranger's life behind it. Hour 10 brings the first credit row; the run has one ready: in 1994 "Edward Baker let people think the best of Andy Shaw's song was Edward Baker's." Hour 100 is the reunion. Wonder wears off at the gigs. The run has 32 shows in ten years, all at the Anchor, told in about eight phrasings; "Your Father's Boat" gets the Night Shift "called back twice" five times. The venue ladder has one rung, and by the third gig the crowd's verdict is a template.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* "Manage a band" is clear; influence without control is not. The first refusal needs a reason the player can act on, or it reads as dice. The chores are booking and rent.

*Best at each part.* Darkest Dungeon does strain in a party better than any music game; Kentucky Route Zero's bar song in Act III, where the player chooses the lines, is the best gig in games. Take both; beat them with members whose lives go on when you aren't looking.

*Only here, and lives off.* The band's history is written on the town: posters stacked, the loft lost to flats, the rival who wasn't picked. Lives off makes quits and rows causeless, and kills the line every band game wants: "I knew that guitarist was trouble."

*Hypotheses.*
1. Chemistry can be read. Measure: predictions about members, scored by arm. Kill: no better than lives off.
2. Gigs hold. Measure: marks on gigs one to three. Kill: half the players mark the third bored, in both arms.
3. Refusals feel like people. Measure: "cheat" marks and think-aloud on refusals. Kill: half read as dice in lives on, as in lives off.

## 3. The restorer

*Loop.* Find a house (a sale board, a death notice) → read it (original, changed, rotten; neighbours; the archive) → bid → choose works and crew → months of works and what they uncover → let or sell → the street answers (tenants, neighbours, the band that loses its loft) → the next house.

*Rhythm.* A session is one house. Reading is suspense with money on it, the bid is the peak, and the works release it slowly until the rot behind a panel. The jump should land at your first restored house: your paint six years weathered, a pram at the door. The feeling: pride or regret at your work ageing like anyone's.

*The game.* Goal: a portfolio and a name, a street kept or sold. Stakes: money and houses. Fail: bankruptcy, a house condemned, a protected front stripped and fined, tenants who turn the street. Mastery: dating a window from across the street, seeing rot under paint, knowing when an owner will sell. The arc is stewardship.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1 puts money at risk within ten minutes: house D, a budget, an asking price. Wonder comes from the layers, like the 1979 café front in the old bank's face. Hour 10 is three houses and the first tenant you priced out; hour 100, you are the developer. Wonder wears off when houses repeat: once the player knows the signs of rot, inspection is a checklist and every house is the same house.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* Clear from minute one; House Flipper taught the verbs. The chores are paperwork, crews and the inspection pass.

*Best at each part.* Papers, Please owns inspection under a clock with a moral cost; Unpacking a life read from things. Take Papers, Please's pressure. Beat House Flipper, whose houses have no past.

*Only here, and lives off.* Criterion 1 as the core skill, with condition caused by lives (care falls after debt or grief). There is no lives-off arm, and that is the warning: rot, dates and prices are ours, and the people are sellers and neighbours. This could be a fine game that doesn't need Understory.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Reading pays. Measure: each player's reading score against profit. Kill: no relation; money comes from the market, not the eye.
2. Inspection stays a skill. Measure: distinct inspection acts and marks, third house against first. Kill: half mark the third bored.
3. People matter. Measure: share of decisions citing a person. Kill: under a fifth.

## 4. A life and its heirs (family)

*Loop.* An act is a season of evenings with a dated goal (the mortgage in March, a parent who needs care) → each evening a person or a place → a short scene, remembered by whoever saw it → the goal met or missed → a jump of years → the return home → a new act. At death or coming of age, you go on as the heir.

*Rhythm.* A session is an act. Persona's calendar gives tension as evenings run out. The jump is the strongest beat any concept has: the same kitchen table at the same hour and season, the parent older in the same chair. The feeling is Boyhood's, a shock at plain time passing.

*The game.* Goal: each act's own; across the game, a family and a house that last. Stakes: the house, a bond, a parent left alone. Fail: the house sold, debts, the family gone from town. Mastery is thin: seeing a need before it's said, spending evenings where they count. The arc is love, regret and legacy.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1: six evenings, and the first one spent away from your father. Hour 10: two jumps, a funeral, and who came. Hour 100: the grandchild's act, and the roof patch your first character put on. Wonder wears off twice over. The engine moves how a person chooses about 3 per cent in 30 years. So the father at 70 acts as he did at 45, and the jump promises change but shows a costume. And blows are rare: 33 illnesses and four deaths in ten years among 119 people. Many jumps land on "not much changed".

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* Evenings become a checklist once the player spreads them evenly to keep everyone content: Persona min-maxed, with no dungeon to pay it off. The act's goal must be on screen in minute one, or this is The Sims.

*Best at each part.* Persona owns the calendar; Crusader Kings succession; Wildermyth scars kept between chapters; Firewatch's prologue the opening, years of a marriage in a few minutes of choices. Beat Edith Finch by growing the house by system, not by hand.

*Only here, and lives off.* Your own traces in a street you watch age; a child with your father's walk. No lives-off arm; without causes, act goals would arrive as weather, and regret would have nothing to hold.

*Hypotheses.*
1. The return reads. Measure: what changed in the family and why, named at the jump. Kill: under two of three right.
2. Evenings cost. Measure: uneven allocation and voiced regret. Kill: evenings spread evenly, no regret.
3. The heir carries on. Measure: pull before and after succession. Kill: a drop of two or more.

## 5. The paper (journalist)

*Loop.* Leads from the town (a shop dark two Saturdays, talk at the Anchor) → choose two or three; days are the budget → watch, ask, read records → write each claim with its source → Thursday's edition → the town reacts → next week's leads, some caused by your edition.

*Rhythm.* A session is an edition, the best beat of the seven. The deadline gives tension weekly, as the day's clock does in Papers, Please. Printing is the peak; the town's reaction is the release or the sting. The jump should land in the archive: your 1991 front page, yellowed, beside what the town now knows. The feeling is a reckoning.

*The game.* Goal: sales, the story before the rival, a record that stands. Stakes: the paper, each source's trust, your name. Fail: a false claim printed (a correction, a burned source, a libel threat), a story missed, the paper closing. Mastery: whom to trust and how far, finding the lead before anyone tells you, knowing when to wait. The arc is the power of a record and its cost to the people in it.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1 is a lead and an edition. Hour 10 is the first correction and the source who stops talking. Hour 100 is the archive as a career. Wonder comes from your words becoming the town's furniture: quoted at the Anchor, pinned in a window, misremembered. It wears off on supply: one visible notable event a week across the town is one lead per edition, and a front-page story comes every three weeks. Filler must be honest (notices, letters, the hat at the Anchor) and quick.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* Clear: a reporter with a deadline. The chores are filling the edition and the same interview loop.

*Best at each part.* Lucas Pope's The Republia Times and Headliner: NoviNews do the edition that steers a town; Her Story the search. Take Headliner's consequence loop and beat its meters: our readers are people who remember.

*Only here, and lives off.* A record the player made, living in the world and scored against the truth. In lives off, leads have no why; the reporter fills slots. I expect the biggest gap between arms here and in the mystery. The risk is the page: the output is text, so the screen must win at finding the lead.

*Hypotheses.*
1. The world makes better reporters. Measure: printed claims scored true, by arm. Kill: under 20 points between arms.
2. The street finds stories. Measure: share of stories begun from something seen. Kill: under a quarter; the page wins.
3. A printed error has teeth. Measure: source checking after a correction. Kill: no change in behaviour.

## 6. A death in the town (mystery)

*Loop.* The death, shown only as aftermath (the empty seat at the Anchor) → days to the inquest → each day three questions and walks, every question heard of → the case board → the account, given to the inquest, the family or the paper → they decide → the record scores it fact by fact → years later, a cold case.

*Rhythm.* A session is a case-day. The clock gives tension; a contradiction found is a small release. The inquest is the climax and the scored close a second one, an aha or a sting, confirmed in rationed batches, never per clue. The jump should land at the foot of the gully steps with the 1989 photograph held up: the place changed, the witnesses older, some gone. The feeling is loss.

*The game.* Goal: an account that holds. Stakes: other people; whoever is wrongly blamed carries it for years in the engine. Fail: the innocent blamed, a source burned, the case closed open. Mastery: reading routines and accounts faster, asking without tipping anyone off, against a sifter that answers with harder chains. The arc is grief and answerability.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1 is an empty seat, the first account, and a life opening out the way Edith Finch's rooms do. Hour 10 is the inquest, the score, the first cold case. Hour 100 has a supply problem. World 9989 has four deaths in ten years, aged 103, 76, 77 and 85, all of illness or the heart. Only Andy Shaw's carries a rich chain: money owed to two friends, a disputed song, a feud ended three months before he died, a roof another man mended. That is one good case a decade. Wonder wears off at the board, if the board becomes the game.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* The danger is the frame. Players expect a killer, and an old man's illness reads as a cheat unless minute one says the question is "what happened, and who let him down". The optimiser will ask everyone everything; three questions a day, each one heard, is the counter.

*Best at each part.* Return of the Obra Dinn owns confirmation in threes; Paradise Killer the accusation you must prove; Pentiment the choice under time with no right answer; Bad News a death without a crime. Beat Shadows of Doubt, whose templated cases wear thin, with cases that are lives.

*Only here, and lives off.* Evidence that is the town's history: the swimmer missing from the steps since 1990, a window changed, a path no longer walked. Lives off kills the why: a generic death in a town with no history ends a story instead of starting one.

*Hypotheses.*
1. A case without a culprit holds. Measure: pull through round 4 against calibration; "cheat" marks at the reveal. Kill: three of six mark the natural death a cheat.
2. Lives make it solvable. Measure: account facts scored right, by arm. Kill: under 25 points between arms.
3. Traces are evidence. Measure: cold-case claims resting on a change in the place. Kill: no player makes one; it's a document game.

## 7. A role in the town (RPG)

*Loop.* A role with a goal (a deckhand who wants the skipper's job) → a working day, then an evening → acts, each seen by whoever was there → people remember, tell and bend the telling → doors open or close → the goal moves or slips → a jump of years → the people you wronged, older.

*Rhythm.* A session is a week of days. Baldur's Gate 3 answers an act within minutes; our engine settles social consequence monthly, and telling travels one hearer at a time. That lag is the concept's biggest pacing problem. Staged well, it is its best beat: a lie told in March reaches you as a friend's coolness in June, and you know why. The jump should land where you made your worst choice. The feeling is consequence.

*The game.* Goal: the role's own. Stakes: your place in the town. Fail: found out, shunned, leaving. The engine already has the teeth: skimming, lying to cover, letting someone take the blame, and secrets that get out (5 of the run's 16 thefts were found out). Give the player those temptations with real need behind them, as Papers, Please gives you a family to feed. Mastery: managing who knows what about you. The arc is belonging and guilt.

*Hours 1, 10, 100.* Hour 1 is arrival and the first act someone sees. At hour 10 a lie from hour 2 comes back bent, through a third person. Hour 100 is a decade: a friend's child grown, a feud that outlived its reason. Wonder comes from being remembered. It wears off when reactions repeat and the day is routine with no combat or dice to carry it.

*Chores, and the first ten minutes.* A Baldur's Gate player asks where the quest is: give one in minute one, with a clock. The chores are the schedule grind and relationships gamed as meters.

*Best at each part.* Disco Elysium owns failure as content; Kingdom Come witnesses who report you; Pathologic 2 time that runs whether you act or not. Take the witnesses and the clock; beat them with memory that passes between people and bends with liking.

*Only here, and lives off.* Reactivity from who saw what, never authored line by line. Lives off turns reactions into noise: the player can't model the town, so no choice has teeth.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Witnesses change choices. Measure: decisions whose think-aloud names who could see. Kill: under a quarter in lives on, or no gap to lives off.
2. Delayed consequence is recognised. Measure: tellings traced back to their act. Kill: under half.
3. The lag is bearable. Measure: pull in rounds with no visible reaction. Kill: a drop of two or more in lives on.

## What the paper test will get wrong

Text flatters the paper and the mystery and starves the band and the club, whose peaks are a gig and a match. A tie on paper between the paper and the band is a win for the band. And model players can't be bored over real hours, so every hour 100 here is reasoning.

## Ranking

1. Mystery: the best structure (a clock, rationed confirmation, an inquest, a scored close), and the cold case is the strongest use of one place across years. Risks: the natural-death frame; one rich case a decade.
2. Paper: the best weekly beat and the cleanest stake on misreading, on the mystery's systems. Risks: the page wins; one lead a week.
3. Band: the deepest feeling and the engine's best support, but gigs template by the third and the music has no answer.
4. RPG: the highest ceiling for agency, held down by the monthly clock and no core challenge yet.
5. Restorer: the clearest money stakes and criterion 1 as a skill, but people are scenery and it may not need the engine.
6. Family: the best jump and the weakest game; people barely change.
7. Club: the most legible and the least served. The town can't field a squad past 1994, and the match is what the town feeds least.

## Hybrids I'd try

1. The obituarist (paper and mystery). The Courier's reporter writes the death notice and the life behind it, to deadline, and the family can contest it. Nobody expects a killer, because you are writing a life. Deaths, retirements, arrivals, departures and weddings (about 40 in ten years) feed one desk. Bad News's frame on Papers, Please's clock.
2. Fielding eleven (the club rebuilt). The game is the secretary's week: get eleven players to the Mudbowl or forfeit, lose points and fold. Reading lives is the challenge, the match resolves in a minute, and the town's size becomes the pressure. It still needs more than 76 people.
3. The anthology (RPG and family). Short chapters as different people, years apart, each with a goal and a temptation, each living with what the last one did. It keeps the family's jump and the RPG's teeth.
4. The loft (band and restorer). The band's room is a building with a landlord; the property market is the band's pressure, and a jump shows the loft as flats.
