# Panel: the systems designer

Paper playtests, round 1: loops, economies, pressure, exploits, and whether the engine can feed each concept, in the line of Football Manager, RimWorld and Paradox. Written 21 October 2026 on Opus 5.5, before the sessions. Numbers come from world 9989 (`fixtures/engine/understory-world-9989.json`) by small queries, re-dated by −37 years. "Knowable" means local or public; "notable" means a notability of 1,000 per mille or more.

## The bottom line

- **The engine feeds the outer loop and starves the inner one.** The median adult in town is part of twelve notable, knowable events in ten years, about one a year. Concepts whose minute-to-minute play is reading lives (the family, the role, the mystery on natural deaths) starve. Concepts with a domain loop the game owns (the edition, the match, the works) survive, with the engine as RimWorld's storyteller cutting into their middle loop.
- **Every inner loop is GM-simulated today.** No match, no edition, no works, no prices, no scene below a month, and no player command. The domain model is the build; the lives are what sets it apart.
- **The control arm will be kind to the club and harsh to the paper.** A match plays the same with cause-free events. Checking a source means nothing when nobody has a reason to bend the story.
- **The town is too small and too old for two cards as written.** Its sixteen youngest adults in 1989 are aged 17 to 39, four of them women. It makes one richly caused death every five years.
- **The engine's best mystery is in its small wrongs.** Sixteen skims and eight blames in ten years, each hidden by someone. Each blame comes with its scapegoat already cast.

## 1. The club

*Loops.*
- Inner (minutes, match day): the team sheet → the match as staged moments → who tired, who went missing → a change at half-time, a word after.
- Middle (a week): the town's week (shifts, the Anchor, a row at work) → two training evenings, and your own spent at a door or on a scout → Saturday's availability and form → the team sheet.
- Outer (seasons): the board's target → recruit in an emptying town → promotion, survival or folding → the next budget, and the 1989 captain's son.

*The game.* Goal: the board's target, then promotion. Resources: a budget, training evenings, the manager's evenings, promises. Pressure: other clubs, the board, shifts, and age. Fail: relegation, the sack, the club folding for want of players. Mastery: seeing who will hold up before form shows it.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: a squad from the town, a first match. Hour 10: a season, with a life cutting in about once a week, which the engine can supply. Hour 100: ten seasons, which it can't. The sixteen youngest adults are the whole squad in 1989; by 1998 the town has twelve people aged 16 to 40. Every match, skill, injury and form value is GM-simulated: the engine has no sport.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* The weekly admin when the town says nothing, which is most weeks. The optimiser picks the best eleven by form and reads the town as an availability list. It promises freely, because a broken promise costs a grudge that never reaches the pitch. Fix: the engine's states (strain, grief, drink, a betrayed confidence) must move form, and evenings must be scarce enough to hurt.

*Best in class.* Football Manager: ability as ranges until scouted, the inbox, the match as highlights. Take all three. Beat it off the pitch, where its players' lives are thin.

*Only here.* Your centre-half becomes a father in June. Your barman is found out the same month letting an older colleague take the blame, and the whole Anchor knows. Your fisherman founds a band in December 1990. Lives-off keeps every screen and loses foresight: an "upset" with no cause can't be seen coming or headed off. Since the match carries the fun in both arms, the lives may add nothing measurable.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Lives-on players predict Saturday's absences and poor games better. Measure: scored predictions by arm. Kill: under 15 points between arms; the town is then a skin.
2. The town costs the manager something real. Measure: the optimiser's town-blind strategy against players who spend evenings on people. Kill: the town-blind strategy finishes level or better.
3. The stakes reach people. Measure: the stakes probe names a person or a promise. Kill: four of six players name only the table.

## 2. The band

*Loops.*
- Inner (a rehearsal): set the session (who, how long, whose song) → the room's acts (a song brought, a row) → step in, stay out, or speak to one member.
- Middle (a gig, weeks): book, rehearse, play → the night, and who stood where → money, the next booking, who stays.
- Outer (an album cycle, years): the band's ambition → jobs, partners, departures → the split, a legend, or a reunion at the same address.

*The game.* Goal: set with the members (the big room, a record, lasting). Resources: money, bookings, evenings, promises. Pressure: rivals, members' lives, one venue. Fail: the split, debts, the loft lost. Mastery: reading chemistry before it shows. Of 42 shows in the run, four fell apart, so booking is nearly always rewarded and the outcome barely varies.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: a player found at the open night, a first rehearsal. Hour 10: the first gig and a credit row. Hour 100: the reunion. Driftwood gives an event every three months and a gig about every eighteen; a weekly rehearsal loop needs about thirteen times that. The player's commands would raise it (each booking is an event), but there are none yet (#22), the band year hasn't run, and there is no venue but the Anchor.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* Book the Anchor every month, since nearly nine gigs in ten go well. Never intervene, since what intervening does can't be read. The chore is rehearsal after rehearsal with nothing in the room.

*Best in class.* Darkest Dungeon for a group under strain that you manage and don't control: stress shown as acts. Game Dev Tycoon for an era clock. Take both; beat Darkest Dungeon's rolled afflictions with causes and memory.

*Only here.* The credit chain is the engine at its best. In December 1994 Edward Baker lets people think Andy Shaw's song is his. Andy finds out and swallows it "for the others' sake", and in March the song gets them called back twice. The two not picked for Pirate Radio in 1998 lose the place to Andy, and Sarah Lane shouts at him in the Anchor. Lives-off loses the chain: rows become weather, and chemistry can't be learned because it has no causes. The gap shows only if a session runs long enough for a chain to finish, which takes months.

*Hypotheses.*
1. A refusal reads as a person deciding. Measure: players give a refusal's real reason. Kill: under half right, or "dice" in two exit interviews of six.
2. The rounds aren't empty. Measure: share of turns with no outcome the player valued. Kill: over 40% by round 4.
3. Lives-on retellings carry more named people and reversals (Kreminski's codebook). Kill: no difference between arms.

## 3. The restorer

*Loops.*
- Inner (an inspection): a front from the street → inspect (a window's era, paint over rot, a newer patch) → a guess at condition and worth.
- Middle (a project, months): buy or pass → works and a crew → what was hidden comes out → let or sell.
- Outer (years): a portfolio through booms and slumps → a street kept or sold, and a name.

*The game.* All six features hold, and money keeps score. Mastery is criterion 1 as the skill that pays: dating a window across the street, seeing rot under paint.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: house D bought or passed over. Hour 10: a project lost to deeper rot. Hour 100: a street. The engine supplies none of the core: no buildings, owners, prices or rents, and six houses bought in ten years, none sold. Lives enter only as owners who must sell, tenants and neighbours, and the run's six purchases and four deaths make a thin market.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* The flip: buy cheapest, do the cheapest works, sell in the boom. Unless the market punishes a bodge (a protected front fined, a buyer who reads it), reading history is optional. The chore is crews and paperwork.

*Best in class.* House Flipper makes the works a toy; Dealer's Life makes buying under hidden condition a haggle against the seller's need. Take the haggle. Beat both with condition as a dated fact readable from the street, and works that change a street other people live in.

*Only here.* A front's history read right is money. A lives-off arm (not run) would lose owners' reasons to sell and neighbours' reactions, which I expect to matter little. That is the warning: this concept tests buildings, not people, and its economy would make Overstory the engine.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Reading pays. Measure: correlation between a player's trace-reading score and money at the jump. Kill: r under 0.3, or the optimiser's blind flip earns as much.
2. People enter the decisions. Measure: share of turns on owners, tenants and neighbours. Kill: under a fifth.
3. The works stay interesting. Measure: bored marks on works turns. Kill: over half by round 4.

## 4. A life and its heirs

*Loops.*
- Inner (an evening): choose where to spend it → a scene with one person → a small act, remembered by whoever saw it.
- Middle (an act, a season): a dated goal (the mortgage due in March, a parent's care) → evenings spent → the goal met or missed.
- Outer (generations): a jump of years → what choices and other lives made → succession to a child.

*The game.* Borderline. Each act's goal gives an outcome; the family's fate has no value the game can score. Effort barely moves people: thirty years shift how someone chooses by about 3%.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: six evenings. Hour 10: a jump, a parent older. Hour 100: your child's act. A clan of ten (the Tanners) gets about 34 events a year, 7 of them notable and knowable, so an act of sixty evenings sees about two things worth an evening. The engine steps a month at a time, one situation per person per month, and can't hear an evening. And 38 of 51 households at the run's end are one person (by the derived rule), so the family house is mostly someone alone.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* Persona's social-link arithmetic: spread evenings to keep every bond above its threshold, then spend the rest on money. The evenings become a checklist within a few hours.

*Best in class.* Crusader Kings III makes succession the act break; Persona 5 makes the calendar the budget; The Sims 2 ages people and passes on their looks. Take succession and the calendar. Beat them with a town that remembers your father.

*Only here.* Parents who age for stated reasons, a child who looks like you, an evening kept by its witnesses. Without lives this is The Sims without needs, so the concept is lives-on only.

*Hypotheses.*
1. There is something to lose. Measure: the stakes probe. Kill: three of six name no loss.
2. Evenings change the record. Measure: the GM's count of outcomes that differ from a run without the player, visible after the jump. Kill: under two per player.
3. The family is recognised across the jump. Measure: members named right. Kill: under 80%. A pass shows reading, not a game.

## 5. The paper

*Loops.*
- Inner (a lead): watch or ask → an account with its teller → check it against another source or a trace.
- Middle (a week, the edition): choose stories, claims and sources against column space and the deadline → print → sales, a source's trust, a person's reaction.
- Outer (years): the paper's survival → a printed mistake remembered in the archive and in people → a career.

*The game.* All six hold, if sales and trust trade. Mastery has a hidden structure worth learning. A told account bends with the teller's liking, and an insincere teller names the wrong person 15% of the time. Private acts travel only through confiding and betrayal. An expert can learn those rules from play; a novice can't see them.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: a lead and a first edition. Hour 10: a printed mistake comes back. Hour 100: the archive. Public events (14 to 18 a year) and notable local ones (about 42) give roughly one newsworthy event a week, the right rate for a small weekly's lead story. Scoops come from the 25 hidden wrongs in ten years, nine of which came out on their own. The rest of the paper (the festival, the harbour day, shows, hires) comes round every year.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* Print only what two sources confirm. It must cost sales or the rival's scoop, or there is no dilemma. The reverse exploit is to print every rumour and absorb the corrections, unless a false claim costs sources who never come back. The chore is filling the edition with routine copy.

*Best in class.* The Republia Times (Lucas Pope, 2012) sizes stories against readership and loyalty. Papers, Please checks documents against a clock, with a family to feed. Headliner: NoviNews lets headlines change a town. Take the sized layout and the clock. Beat them with sources whose errors have causes, and a printed record people act on for years.

*Only here.* Verification against a town with motives. Lives-off loses the whole skill: cause-free events have no teller, no bend and no chain, so checking a source is checking noise. I expect the largest gap between arms of the five concepts run both ways. The risk is the page: the output is text, so the screen must matter for finding the story.

*Hypotheses.*
1. Source judgement can be learned. Measure: share of printed claims that are true, rounds 1 to 3 against 4 to 6, by arm. Kill: no rise with lives on, or the same rise with lives off.
2. The edition's trade is real. Measure: each player prints at least one unconfirmed claim under pressure, and its consequence lands in the session. Kill: the optimiser's confirmed-only paper loses nothing.
3. The town finds the story. Measure: share of leads found by watching places and people, against records. Kill: under a quarter; the page wins.

## 6. A death in the town

*Loops.*
- Inner: a place, a person or a record → a claim with its source on the board → a question, which others hear of.
- Middle (a case, weeks): days to the inquest → an account given → the finding, then the record.
- Outer (years): cold cases across a changed town, harder chains as the player improves.

*The game.* It holds only with the scored close. Nobody is answerable for an illness at 85, so a wrong account hurts no one, and "an innocent blamed" has nothing to be blamed for.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: the death found. Hour 10: the inquest. Hour 100: the cold case. One rich death every five years: a career of two cases a year needs a town about ten times larger. The engine's small wrongs, though, are cases with an opponent today, about 2.5 a year, each hidden by someone. In June 1989 Peter Scott let Olive Shaw take the blame for his mistake; an investigator who accuses Olive has blamed an innocent by the engine's own record.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* The board becomes admin. The optimiser asks everyone everything before word spreads, or names whoever has the most motives. Questions others hear of (option 6's rule) answer the first; accounts that harden against the asker would answer both.

*Best in class.* Return of the Obra Dinn confirms fates in threes. Paradise Killer lets you accuse anyone and makes you prove it. Pentiment gives too little time for every lead. Shadows of Doubt's generated cases go stale. Take the batched confirmation and the short clock. Beat Shadows of Doubt with cases from chains.

*Only here.* Evidence that is the town's history, and a cold case read through a changed town. Lives-off loses solvability, since cause-free events form no chain. That gap is certain and proves little; the test is whether lives-on plays as a game.

*Hypotheses.*
1. A natural death holds as a game. Measure: pull and agency against each player's calibration. Kill: the sceptic and the optimiser score 3 or more below calibration and can't name a risk.
2. Living secrets pull harder than the death. Measure: which threads players chase. Kill of the reframing: players ignore the secrets.
3. The board stays a tool. Measure: share of turns spent arranging claims. Kill: over 30%.

## 7. A role in the town

*Loops.*
- Inner (a scene): talk, help, take, tell → the person's answer → the next act.
- Middle (a season or a quest): a promise, a job, a feud → kept or broken → word spreads from who saw it.
- Outer (years): a jump → the town remembers who you were to whom → new roles.

*The game.* The weakest valued outcome of the seven. Without a goal it is The Sims, which Juul counts as borderline.

*Hours and the feed.* Hour 1: arriving, a first favour. Hour 10: a name at the Anchor. Hour 100: a life. Baldur's Gate 3 answers the player many times an hour, authored line by line. The engine answers once a month, and the median adult does one notable, knowable thing a year. All reactivity inside a scene is Overstory's or the GM's.

*Chore and the dominant strategy.* Reputation farming. The cheapest kind acts recur (the hat, the roof, "count me in"). Chip in every time, bank the goodwill, then spend it on one betrayal. Since telling stops when the person is away, a spell out of town launders a reputation.

*Best in class.* Baldur's Gate 3 for reactivity, Kingdom Come: Deliverance II for witnesses, Pathologic 2 for a clock that runs whether you act or not. Take the witnesses and the running clock. Beat them with residents who remember each other, not only you.

*Only here.* An act told on through the town and bent by who likes you. Lives-off keeps first-hand reactions and loses why a stranger already dislikes you. Over six rounds a player may not tell the arms apart; the gap shows over years.

*Hypotheses.*
1. The world remembers. Measure: turns per round where someone reacts to an act they didn't see. Kill: under one per round with lives on.
2. There is a goal. Measure: by round 3 the sceptic states a goal and a risk. Kill: no.
3. The arms differ. Measure: engaged marks from players 2 and 5 by arm. Kill: no difference.

## Ranking

1. The paper, with the mystery's cases as its investigations. The engine's real asset (accounts with tellers and motives) is its core skill, it has a loop of its own, and the control arm can't fake it.
2. The club, re-scoped: the most proven game and the right rate of intrusions, but the engine has no sport and the town can't field a squad.
3. The mystery, rebuilt on small wrongs: real opponents and scapegoats today; on natural deaths it is a biography.
4. The band: the engine's richest domain, a thirteenth of the density a weekly loop needs, and no answer yet for the music.
5. The restorer: the cleanest economy and the best fit for criterion 1, but the engine adds almost nothing and the market would make Overstory the engine.
6. The role: better as a dial on the first three (a body in the town) than as a concept; the engine answers monthly.
7. The family: the weakest game and the weakest fit, with lone households, people who barely change, and the largest scope.

## Hybrids to try

- **The Courier's beat.** The reporter's weekly edition is the inner and middle loop. The cases are the engine's small wrongs and its deaths (an obituary is a biography with sources), and the band and the club are beats with reviews and match reports. One board, one notebook, one set of source marks; the printed record goes back into the engine (request 9).
- **The club that keeps the town.** The antagonist is the town emptying. The manager's levers are jobs (the engine's arrivals already come "for a job"), homes and the next generation; a regional squad, seasons as the middle loop, generations as the outer. The club, the family and the town's ageing in one game.
- **The loft's landlord.** You own cheap old space after the crash. Whom you let it to (a band, a café, flats) changes the street and the people in it: money against the town's life. The band becomes a tenant you hear through the floor, which sidesteps the music.
- **The role as a dial.** Any of the three above played with a body in the town, as `living-world.md` sets the dials.

## Must improve, from systems

### Engine

- *Must:* player commands, accepted or refused with the top reason (#22). Without them every influence in every concept is GM fiction.
- *Must:* who knows what, with tellers and first or second hand (request 1). The paper, the mystery and the role depend on it.
- *Must:* scale. A town of 76 can't feed a few hundred buildings, a squad, a league or a weekly paper. Run several neighbourhoods of about 80 people each (request 25), and measure knowable, notable events per week for a concept's cast; aim for at least one per player-week.
- *Must:* places for events, and a settled contract between the two clocks (request 28; `living-world.md`). Every inner loop happens in evenings.
- *Should:* domain kinds: a club with matches and ageing skills, the band year, records (9), harm within Rule 8, places that close (4), homes (15).
- *Should:* fold repeats and widen the shapes. The 25 commonest sentence shapes are 18% of the run, and a player at hour 100 has seen them all.
- *Should:* failure in domains: four failed gigs in 42 is too few to teach anything.
- *Could:* the starting record's weighty events exported, so the old bank's nineteen years have reasons.

### Overstory's systems

- *Must:* a storyteller that paces the engine's chains into each concept's middle loop, as RimWorld's does, and never invents a fact (request 7, used on our side).
- *Must:* a scene clock: evenings derived from habits and the month's events, with only commitments sent back.
- *Must:* each concept's domain model given an owner in the facts contract before it is built: the match, the edition, the works and prices.
- *Should:* the counterfactual twin at the jump (`clone.ts`, `whatif.ts`): show players what their acts changed, as a difference between two towns.

### Interface

- *Must:* the knowledge store, with source marks, counts and "?", as one board and notebook shared by every concept.
- *Must:* a visible time budget. Every concept's scarce resource is evenings.
- *Should:* prediction as a verb, scored at the jump, so reading turns into felt competence.
- *Should:* the refusal card: a command's answer with its reason.

### Content

- *Must:* real domain depth per concept; Understory's `WHY.md` warns that rich people can't carry a shallow game.
- *Should:* an answer for the music; 1989's documents (the Courier's page, posters, notices).
- *Could:* a region beyond the town for away matches, gigs and the rival paper (request 12).
