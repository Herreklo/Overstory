# Panel: narrative and role-playing design

The narrative and RPG designer's read of the seven concepts for paper round 1 (`../method.md`), written 21 October 2026 on Opus 5.5 before the sessions. Its evidence is `docs/ideas/first-game-options.md`, the world pack and small queries of `fixtures/engine/understory-world-9989.json`, with engine years shifted by −37. Every number comes from the run.

## What world 9989 can carry

- **The chains are specific; the sentences are stock.** With names, numbers and titles stripped, the 2,635 events fall into 899 sentence shapes, and the commonest repeat 14 to 50 times ("skipped the drink afterwards, again": 50). Ken Scott, the Courier's editor, has 33 rows with Sylvia Waller (married 1992) in ten years, in four sentence shapes. The chains never repeat. Sarah Lane (born 1977) practises every night from 1994, falls apart at the open night in 1996 and 1997, and takes from the timber account in 1997. In June 1998 she loses the Pirate Radio place to Andy Shaw, aged 85, and shouts at him in the Anchor. He dies in October.
- **The engine is a dilemma generator.** 1,571 events (60%) are decisions, in 35 kinds: withdrawal 303, anger 198, duty 138, dishonesty 46, courage 14. The export keeps each decision's kind and drops its options and odds.
- **What the town knows is thin.** 161 events in ten years are public, 1,271 private.
- **Deaths leave little.** Four in ten years, all natural. Andy Shaw, the town's best songwriter, is followed by three events: his band goes on, two friends take up a habit. No funeral, nothing for his songs, nothing from Sarah.
- **The young are blank and few.** Nobody born after 1983 has more than two events by 1999. About 26 people in town are aged 16 to 38 in 1989.
- **Bands repeat their room.** All 32 shows are at the Anchor, in seven sentence shapes. The 37 song titles share a small pool: "Behind" six times, "Fence" four.
- **The text leaks.** 130 event texts and 418 cause labels carry engine years ("it had been happening since 2014"). 25 habits begun, dropped or resumed are marked private, though one of them, "John Holmes is now at the Anchor every Friday without fail", is seen by the whole bar.
- **People hold still.** Thirty years move how someone chooses about 3%. Arcs come from circumstance (deaths, jobs, places won and lost), never from growth: Crusader Kings' model of character, not Disco Elysium's.

So the player should rarely read an engine sentence. They see the act staged, hear it as an account bent by its teller, or read it in a record. The engine's lines are the game master's truth, and story rides on chains and on people who recur.

## 1. The club

**Loop.** fixture list → two training evenings (who turns up, and why not) → team sheet → Saturday in staged moments, the result simulated → the Anchor after → visits, promises, scouting → next week; the winter is the jump. Story enters where lives touch availability, in authored match staging, and above all in the player's retelling: Football Manager players write their saves as sagas.

**The game.** The board's target, then promotion; the job, money and promises at stake; relegation, the sack or folding as failure. Mastery is seeing who will hold up before form shows it. The costly choice is dropping someone, and the engine's memory makes it bite. John Holmes, passed over for skipper, ran Ivan Tanner down in 1990, 1993 and 1998, and turned down Ivan's peace offers in 1993 and 1995. A dropped captain here can be a ten-year enemy, where Football Manager's morale recovers.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** Hour 1 holds: a lad found, a first match. At the run's density a squad of 18 appears in about seven events a month, mostly minor. A few count, the right dose for hour 10: John Holmes starts drinking the month his mother Ellie dies, and drops Tuesday cards. Hour 100 breaks: the run ends after ten years, and "the captain's son" has no events. The engine carries availability and grudges, never the match.

**Reactivity.** Real: absences, drink, grief, grudges, the hat. Faked: the match, the league, rival clubs (no other town exists), the board. Chores: weekly admin, and fifty "skipped the drink afterwards" dilemmas.

**Who does it best.** Football Manager: the match, scouting, the long save. Take its ranges until scouted, as acts with counts; beat its personal issues, which are templates on dice.

**Only here.** The striker's week at the sawmill shows on Saturday, and the squad's grudges are the town's. Lives off turns every absence into an injury roll. Worse, the pool: with 26 people aged 16 to 38, a squad is most of the town's young adults, and half would have no engine life. The lives-on arm would contain the lives-off arm.

**Misreading.** Grief and drink both show as withdrawal. Drop a grieving man as a drinker and you make a real enemy, fair only if the death (public) is a short walk away.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lives-on managers predict who misses Saturday better | prediction hits, on against off | no gap between arms |
| Lives enter the decisions that matter | share of team-sheet choices citing someone's life | under one in five: Football Manager with flavour |
| A grudge bites and reads as caused | the dropped player's later acts named and attributed in the probe and exit | not noticed, or read as random |

## 2. The band

**Loop.** the open night → recruit → rehearsal in the loft (shorten it, bring someone in, talk to one, stay out) → a song brought, and whose it is → book the Anchor → the show, read from the room → accounts and posters → rent; jumps of years back to the same room. The engine's output is most designed for story here: Driftwood's six years (1990 to 1996) run from formation to "the night the room went quiet". Authored: the landlord, the venues, shows staged without songs.

**The game.** The band's ambition, set with its members; a split, debt or the loft lost as failure. The costly choices are who gets the place and whose song leads. Credit is the currency, and the engine already trades it. "Edward Baker let people think the best of Andy Shaw's song was Edward Baker's"; Andy found out, then "swallowed it and stayed in Driftwood, for the others' sake".

The manager has no verbs. The engine has no player commands (#22 is paused), and its band decisions belong to members: seize or shy away from the open night, stay for the others, claim, back or resent a song. Until commands land, the manager watches.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** The first rehearsal and first gig are strong, because the chains are rich: a member lost, a song stolen, the one not picked. By hour 100 repetition wins: the Anchor every time, seven ways to describe it, no venue ladder.

**Reactivity.** Real: credit fights, places won and lost, splits with reasons, the open night. Faked: music, venues, records, radio, money beyond rent. Chore: booking the Anchor again.

**Who does it best.** Kentucky Route Zero's bar performance stages a song as a scene: the player picks its lines, and the story doesn't change. Take its staging; beat it with the one who wasn't picked turning up in a rival band, which no authored game can afford.

**Only here.** Lives off loses every chain: a split becomes creative differences, and Sarah Lane's year (practice, failure, theft, rejection, a shout, a death) becomes noise.

**Misreading.** A poster crediting "Christmas Fence" to Edward is a record that lies, and fair, because Andy's friends say otherwise. The danger: Sarah shouts at Andy in June; he dies in October "after a long illness". A cut from the row to the funeral claims a cause the record lacks.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choices feel costly | exit lines of the form "I shouldn't have…" naming a choice | most say they can't tell how their choices mattered |
| People are recognised | memory probe: three members and the one not picked | members remembered only by instrument |
| Repetition shows by hour 10 | "same as before" in marks and needs | more than half flag it: venues and records come first |

## 3. The restorer

**Loop.** a house seen from the street → inspect (traces, records, neighbours) → bid against a rival → choose each part: keep, mend, replace → months pass (rot, market, neighbours) → let or sell → next house. Story comes from the house's dated diary (ours, `authored` or `derived`), the people who sell and object, and the player's "my street".

**The game.** Money is the score, so Juul's six features hold. Costly choices: buying D, boarded since 1986; stripping a 1970s window; putting a tenant out. The bite needs homes (#59) before a tenant put out becomes an engine grudge. Today the engine has no buildings, owners or prices, so every number the player optimises is ours: the pre-mortem's story of Overstory becoming the engine.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** Hour 1 is the best of the seven: three inspections of D, a price, buy or pass. Hour 10 is a project's profit. Hour 100 needs an economy we would have to design. The engine's lives are context: who sells, who complains.

**Reactivity.** Real in the house's diary and the condition rules. Faked in prices, rivals, the council and crews. Chore: choosing works and clearing rooms, House Flipper's whole game.

**Who does it best.** Return of the Obra Dinn for reading as deduction; House Flipper for the toy we must not become. Take Obra Dinn's confirmation in threes for a house's dates.

**Only here.** Criterion 1 with money on it. No lives-off arm runs, so the round can't say whether lives pay; I think they barely do. Rot is rot whoever's grief caused the neglect, so the score is blind to people.

**Misreading.** Tired paint read as grief when the cause is debt costs the restorer nothing.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading pays | correlation between inspection accuracy and profit | none |
| People matter | buy, sell or works decisions that turn on someone's life | condition and price alone |
| It stays a reading game | share of turns spent reading after hour 1 | inspection falls away once works open |

## 4. A life and its heirs

**Loop.** an act, a season of evenings → each evening spent on a person or a place → events, remembered by whoever saw → the act's dated goal (the mortgage, a parent's care) → a jump of years → the return → succession, as the heir. The engine gives what others do and the player their own character; conversation needs the most authored writing of the seven. The retelling is Crusader Kings' "my dynasty".

**The game.** Juul calls this borderline without each act's goal. Yet the engine gives it the best stake of the seven, a want with a deadline that death enforces. A want to make things right turns into "never made things right" when the other dies (a pack life in `lives-a.md` shows it; 9989 has no case in ten years). If the player's father dies before they make peace, the engine writes the regret and keeps it. That is Pathologic's absence as a cost, with no writer.

Three things work against it. Heirs are blank. Parenting changes nothing when people move 3% in thirty years. And couples loop: cast the player's household as Ken Scott and Sylvia Waller, and an act is 33 rows in four sentences.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** Hour 1 is Persona's calendar and works. Hour 10, a jump and a parent older, works if the regret lands. Hour 100 is your child's act: a page the engine hasn't written and a face Overstory hasn't made.

**Reactivity.** Real: grief, regret, visits ("kept turning up at Kate Holmes's door with something or other"), the hat, couples drifting. Faked: the player's acts (no commands), conversation, the house. Chore: evenings as a checklist, and rows that "went the same way every time".

**Who does it best.** Crusader Kings III for succession, Persona 5 for the calendar, Pathologic 2 for absence as a cost. Take Persona's evening budget and Crusader Kings' heir; beat its thin courtiers with neighbours who remember each other.

**Only here.** A parent growing old in a house you watch age. No lives-off arm runs.

**Misreading.** The family's own story may be wrong, and could be the game. Anyone who learns of Andy Shaw's cancer (1996) dates it back to the empty harbour steps; the record says his swimming "just lapsed" in 1990.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An evening shows later | after the jump, players name one consequence of an evening | none can |
| Regret reads as caused | players who neglect a relative attribute "never made things right" to their choice | read as dice |
| The heir holds | after succession, players carry on a goal from the first life | a new game among strangers |

## 5. The paper

**Loop.** leads from the town (a dark window, a council notice, talk at the Anchor) → the chase: watch, ask, read, days to the deadline → write each claim with its source → print: a record in the world → reaction: sources cool, people believe, the archive keeps it → next edition. The engine supplies facts and institutions; the editor and the rival paper are authored. The player's retelling is the edition itself, scored against the record.

**The game.** Sales, scoops and corrections give a clear outcome, and 9989 hands the reporter a first case. Terry Sutton worked at the council office from 1958 and was chief officer when he left. All thirteen summer festivals he proposed from 1963 to 1992 were approved; the only festival refused in the run was Neil Lyons's, in 1980. Who decided on Terry's own festivals isn't in the export: a lead, not yet a story. In June 1990 he took from the expense claims and was found out (local). In April 1991 he announced he would step down, and the engine's reason is "64, and tired". "Chief officer quits after expenses" joins two true facts with a false cause.

The desk has its own conflicts. The editor's partner works at the council office, and she and her father are named in public for the 1993 washout ("Toby Waller and Sylvia Waller never did their part"). Her sister's husband, Colin Berry, was found out taking from the expenses in 1992, a year after he joined the Courier.

A printed claim should become a record people act on. With no records or publication command in the engine (request 9), that reaction is the game master's today.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** A lead and an edition make a strong first hour. Hour 10 brings a consequence and a source burned, if publication is a command. Hour 100 is a career's archive, your 1991 mistake in people's memories. With one public event every three weeks, the edition is filled by turning local and private matters into print, which is the job.

**Reactivity.** Real: council decisions with reasons, hires, skims found out (5 of 16), accounts bent by tellers. Faked: what publication does, the rival paper, sales. Chore: pages of hires and arrivals.

**Who does it best.** Orwell for committed fragments that become official truth; Headliner: NoviNews for editions that change a town. Take Orwell's committed fragment; beat its scripted consequences with the engine's.

**Only here.** The town's memory includes what you wrote. Lives off removes the causes, so there are no false joins to make or avoid, and the game becomes Headliner. Misreading is the concept's mechanic; fairness follows the death.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Checking pays | false causal claims printed, by whether a second source was sought; on against off | right or wrong at chance either way |
| The edition costs | stories held or softened to keep a source, per session | the edition is only filled |
| The town answers | players name who changed toward them because of a piece, and why | nobody can |

## 6. A death in the town

**Loop.** the death found → walk, watch, ask (each question heard), read → the board: source and reliability apart → days to the inquest → an account given → the record scores it fact by fact → the cold case, years later. The engine gives the life around the death. Its circumstances (who found him, where) are the game master's, since events have no place or finder.

**The game.** The outcome is weak without the scored close. Andy Shaw's last year is the richest case in the run. He asked Ben Reed and Oliver Brook for money. Edward Baker claimed his song. Ben helped with his roof. His feud with Karen Holmes ended three months before he died. He won a band place over Sarah Lane, who shouted at him. Nobody killed him; the case is what each owed him and who let him down. Each question is heard, and an accusation follows its target for years.

Supply is the hard limit: four deaths in ten years, where a detective's career needs dozens of cases. The town must grow to several hundred lives, or cases must come from other chains (a skim found out, a stolen song) or the starting record.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** The death and first accounts are strong. Hour 10, the inquest and its score, is the crisis: an explorer who learns there is no killer may call it a cheat. Hour 100 needs the supply above.

**Reactivity.** Real: telling bends. A teller who dislikes the subject hardens the story, and an insincere teller names the wrong person 15% of the time. Faked: the inquest, police, the finder, the scene. Chore: the board, as in Shadows of Doubt.

**Who does it best.** Return of the Obra Dinn for fair deduction confirmed in threes; Paradise Killer for proof without confirmation; Bad News for a death without a crime. Take Obra Dinn's batch confirmation, for facts only. Beat Shadows of Doubt's templates with chains.

**Only here.** A cold case read through a changed town, and a rumour traced through the engine's own tellers. Lives off gives suspects without causes, and the case becomes a lottery: the strongest arm contrast of the seven.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A case without a culprit holds | pull in rounds 3 to 6 against calibration, once players learn there may be no killer | pull falls three or more, or "cheat" surprises |
| Lives make accounts right | facts scored right in final accounts, on against off | no gap |
| The screen is fair | causes of wrong claims: screen or record, against the player's own leap | more than a third trace to the screen |

## Fairness for the paper and the death

The run already holds false joins: Sarah's shout and Andy's death; Terry's expenses and his stepping down; John Holmes sober the month after his whispering came out (1993), which reads as shame, though the engine's reason is "likes things done properly and on time". Five rules keep both games fair.

1. Score only what a witness could know. A private act nobody saw (Harry Myers's theft from the trip money) is never scored or hinted.
2. Every scored fact is reachable by two independent routes. Two sources agree only when neither heard it from the other, which needs who told whom (request 1).
3. The screen never joins. No cut, adjacency or strip of years runs from a row to a death, and a timeline never looks like a chain.
4. The close shows the record's causes with their weights, so a wrong join is seen as wrong ("64, and tired").
5. The score keeps apart what the player couldn't catch (an insincere teller's wrong name) and what they leapt to.

## 7. A role in the town

**Loop.** a day of work and an evening → act on someone (help, promise, confide, tell, keep away) → witnesses, gossip, bent telling → each person's belief about you → wants as goals, rivals for the same place → a jump → the payoffs, in the street. Story comes from the engine and the player's character; conversation needs authored writing.

**The game.** The risk is the walking simulator: there are no quests. The engine's wants give goals, and its contests give rivals. Sarah Lane, played from 1995, is a ready premise. She works at the mill, in debt, and wants to play. Her foreman tells her how to do everything. There is the timber account, where she thinks nobody would notice; the open night; and an 85-year-old who takes the place she wanted.

The engine already runs a social combat system: a confidence is kept, told or thrown back, and a told secret gets back half the time and ends the friendship. Crusader Kings' secrets as currency, with tellers.

**Hours 1, 10 and 100.** Hour 1 is strong if the premise is sharp. Hour 10 is the confidence that came back and the grudge it started. Hour 100 is recognition without growth: only the player's character moves.

**Reactivity.** Real: memory between residents, which Baldur's Gate 3 lacks; it authors reactivity per combination, and ours arrives per witness. Faked: the conversation, and the player's acts, since there are no commands. The felt problem is pace: engine reactivity is monthly and mostly private ("kept Lily Lyons at arm's length for months"), where Baldur's Gate 3's is immediate and voiced. Unless the screen stages it (the back way round, the far side of the room), the player won't feel it.

The fix is in the run. The player's character should hold its own decisions: the engine poses the situation ("Tess Lane asked how things really were") and the player chooses where the odds would. The commands already exist as decision kinds, so this is reactivity without a written line per combination. Handed raw, withdrawal and anger dominate and the player meets "skip the drink afterwards?" fifty times, so a sifter picks which dilemmas reach them.

**Who does it best.** Baldur's Gate 3 for reactivity; Disco Elysium for inner voices as fallible reads; Citizen Sleeper for daily dice as a visible budget. Take Disco's voices, with counts ("you've seen her be cold twice"), as reads in the player's head, never the game's verdict. Beat Baldur's Gate 3 on memory between people.

**Only here.** Residents remember each other, and a secret passed on can get back to the one who confided it. Lives off keeps the verbs and loses the consequences.

**Misreading.** The engine's beliefs can be wrong ("Eric had decided Amelia never liked him… Amelia, who did, never understood why", `lives-a.md`), and the player's character can stand on either side. A read is a voice, and it can be wrong.

| Hypothesis | Measure | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Secrets bite | players who tell a confidence see it return and name it as their doing | not noticed, or read as random |
| Goals form from wants | rounds spent on a self-set goal, three or more | wandering: the walking simulator |
| Attitudes change for reasons | players name two people whose stance changed, and why; on against off | no gap |

## Ranking

1. **The paper.** The run's strongest material (the council, the festivals, skims found out, an editor with family on every side), and misreading is its mechanic. Needs publication as a command.
2. **The death.** The strongest arm contrast and Obra Dinn's fairness, capped by four deaths in ten years and the crisis of no culprit.
3. **The role.** The engine is already a dilemma generator and a secret economy; monthly, private reactivity is the costliest thing to make felt.
4. **The band.** The run's richest arcs, a manager with no verbs, and the Anchor every time.
5. **The club.** A proven game whose deciding part the lives never touch; the squad is the town's whole youth.
6. **The restorer.** The best first hour and the cleanest score, blind to people.
7. **The family.** The best stake (regret enforced by death) on a blank cast that doesn't change.

## Hybrids to try

- **The reporter who covers deaths.** The paper and the death as one game, and my first choice. The edition replaces the inquest as the account given, cases come from every chain so supply holds, and the observer's verbs are the tools.
- **The band as a role.** Play a member, not the manager: the engine's member decisions become the verbs, and the bandmates are the party. Pirate Radio in 1998, from Sarah Lane's side.
- **The reading as an inner voice.** One tool for the reporter, the detective and the role: Disco Elysium's fallible voices, in counts and hunches.
- **The family story as a cold case.** The heir inherits the family's accounts and tests them against the town: the family's best game, and cheaper than generations.
