# Exit interview

## Marks for round 3
- Turn 3.1: bored — the cardigan "must be a visitor's", which is one more thread closed pleasantly. June says "You live here now" before I've done anything: belonging is handed over, not earned. And everyone in this town is one repeated gesture plus a number of years: 41 years of ferrying, 31 of wiping the table, 11 of climbing the steps, born in the back bedroom. That's a template, not a set of people. Surprise: none
- Turn 3.2: engaged — but as a reader, not a player. The lantern walk is the best writing in the game: the gap in the band where the tuba should be, two rows of lights, one on the granite and one in the water. I carried a jar someone else lit and put it down where the row ended. It happened to me. (One small thing: the screen gave me "Good morning, ladies" in the evening. I never wrote that line.) Surprise: delight — the tuba gap.

## 1. Overall pull
2. The walk lifts it above the 1 I gave at the bench, but the walk is a cutscene.

## 2. Best and worst
**Best:** the observation is first-rate. Mooring rings polished on top where the ropes work them. A handrail bright in the middle and rough at both ends. Wilf handing me the rag without looking up, the one moment anyone asked something of me. The tuba gap. Whoever wrote this can see.

**Worst:**
- Nothing is held back, so nothing can be found. Every question I asked closed kindly: the peephole is rope, the plaque is for someone who "liked it here", the chandler went to his daughter's, the cardigan belongs to a visitor.
- I tried to get something wrong by needling Rob about his father. The line bounced off, and Belonging still went up.
- The rewards came whatever I did: a geranium, a cat, a fern, and 51% Belonging in nine turns.
- I made 0 of 48 sketches. The game's own collecting verb never gave me a reason to use it.
- It trains you to stop looking, and that's the worst of it. By the end I did notice things: a FOR SALE house with flaking paint, a darned cardigan found on the steps and unclaimed since August, a stranger with binoculars off the last ferry. Two sisters who "see everyone who goes up and everyone who comes down", which makes them a witness. I didn't care, because three rounds had taught me that details here lead nowhere. The game wastes its own best material.

## 3. Chapter Two? $14.99?
No and no. Possibly as a 20-minute read on sale, for someone who wants a mood. The store page is honest that nothing is at stake, so I'm not its audience. Even for that audience, though, a bar that fills whatever you do isn't belonging. What would bring me back: the cardigan, the house for sale and the sisters' window turned into something I could work out by watching, with a cost for getting it wrong. The ingredients for that are already on the stair.

## 4. What I was trying to achieve, and what I risked
I was trying, first, to find one question worth answering (the chandler's window), and then to find out whether anything could go wrong (Rob). Neither paid off. I risked nothing, literally: the store page promised that and kept its word.

## Compared with Papers, Please
- In Papers, Please the stakes melted by mid-afternoon because the economy was soft. Here there were never any stakes to melt.
- The best moment in both was a person leaning in to ask something of me: Danik there, Wilf's rag here. Papers, Please had one a round. Saltlight had one in the whole game.
- Pull: 6 against 2. Saltlight writes better than Papers, Please plays. It's still not a game.
