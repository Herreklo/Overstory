# Exit interview: X4, Saltlight

## 1. Overall pull
2. The writing would keep me reading a page. Nothing in the game would keep me playing one.

## 2. Best and worst
**Best:**
- The surfaces. The mooring rings are polished on top where the ropes work them. The plaque's edges are polished by shoulders. The calendar stopped at March 1988. The cardigan's darns are two pale islands in nearly the right grey. Above all, the Pennock house: white over green over ochre, a front you can read like a map of its owners.
- Those are real clues, the same kind as the *Hesper*'s stitches. The world knows how to show time.

**Worst:**
- Every clue ends in a kindly full stop. Tolley retired to his daughter's, Mrs Pennock went to her son's, the cardigan is "a visitor's, we expect" (said twice, by June and by the sisters, in nearly the same words). The crab comes back, and "pretty, isn't it, all those colours coming through." Nobody disagrees, nobody hides anything, and nobody remembers anything wrongly, so there is nothing to set one account against another.
- The one possible contradiction was that the sisters "see everyone who goes up and everyone who comes down" and still don't know whose cardigan it is. I couldn't tell if it was a secret or the game not caring, and the game never told me. That's the worst kind of ambiguity: the kind I can't act on.
- The journal writes my feelings for me. It explained away my one-plank question before I could ask it.
- Belonging went up 16, 16 and 25 whatever I did. It rewards attendance, not attention.
- Rob, the man "who doesn't talk to the passengers", turned out to be a ferry button with one line: "Island?" His silence was a mechanic, not a secret.
- My own words were ignored once in favour of the menu line.

## 3. Chapter Two, and $14.99
No to Chapter Two. "New sketches and keepsakes to find" is a list, not a question. No at $14.99, for me. I'd buy it as a gift for someone who wants a warm hour of rain and fiddle. I'd never play it myself.

## 4. What I was trying to achieve, and what I risked
I was trying to find the story under the town. I chased the reason for the one plank, the rubbed window, March 1988, the darned cardigan, Rob's silence and the boarded house. I wanted one thing that had happened here and that the town didn't agree about.

I risked nothing, because nothing could be risked. There are no wrong answers, so my readings never mattered. "You can't miss anything", so my choice of where to spend the day cost nothing. No fail states, so no stakes. My attention was the only thing I spent, and the game didn't repay it.

Next to the *Hesper* the same material does opposite work. A dated object, a worn surface, a witness: there, each was evidence with a fate hanging on it and a chance of being wrong. Here each is a vignette that ends where it begins. Nothing would have needed to change but this: one lie, one disagreement between two people, one date that doesn't add up and pays off when I catch it.

## 5. Marks for round 3
- Turn 3.1: bored — the silent ferryman had one line, "Island?", and a single menu option. What I'd hoped was a man holding something back was a ticket booth. Surprise: none.
- Turn 3.2: bored — another neighbour gone to a grown child inland, and Sal told me how to feel about the decay ("pretty, isn't it"). Surprise: none.
- Turn 3.3: curious — white over green over ochre. The only moment I read the town's history off a surface myself, and it was good. Then a FOR SALE card, no name, and nothing to follow. Surprise: none.
- Turn 3.4: would quit — as many jars as people, and every jar lit. I'd predicted the set piece and named the one thing that could have saved it, a jar no one lights, and the game ruled it out in a sentence. It was beautiful, and it confirmed there's nothing under the surface. Surprise: none.
