# Round 3

**Turn 3.1. June, in the Kettle.**
The bell over the door, and the warmth hits you: toast, steam, the radio low on a dance band. Six tables, cakes under glass domes, and by the till a photograph of a woman squinting into the sun outside this window in 1962. A grey cardigan hangs on a hook by the door. June looks up from the table by the window, cloth in hand, and her face opens. "There you are! You'll want this." She takes a key on a wooden fob from her apron pocket and puts it in your palm. "Side door, up the stairs. Blue window."
You: "Could I have a spare jam jar for tonight's lantern walk? And is that grey cardigan from the steps here? I might know whose it is."
She glances at the hook. "The cardigan? Been there since August. Must be a visitor's." She goes back to the table. "Sit yourself down. Tea's on the house: you live here now."
The cloth goes round in slow circles. "It was Mum's café, then mine. I've wiped this table every morning for thirty-one years."
The cardigan stays on its hook.

*June, next time:* 1. "Is my room ready?" 2. "What's good here?" 3. "What a lovely place."

**Turn 3.2. The Marigold.**
Up close she's all overlaps: clinker planks of larch gone the colour of honey, each one lapped over the one below, and the copper rivets in rows, green as old pennies. The third plank up from the keel is glossy and wet-looking; the rest are dull and waiting. Wilf's brush goes on along the third.

**Turn 3.3. Sketch: the Marigold.**
You sit on the end of the trestle and draw her upside down, the planks stepping down to the keel like the roofs of the town.
*Sketchbook: "Marigold. No nails in her, Wilf says."*

**Turn 3.4. Up Wren Steps to the chapel.**
The steps are still wet and the handrail cold, polished bright in the middle, rough at both ends. At step 20 a house is flaking in layers, white over green over ochre, one window boarded. At step 40 the Hales' window: Dot and Ivy look up together and wave, and you wave back. At step 50 a marmalade cat lies on the stone where the last of the sun has warmed it, and rolls over as you pass. At step 60 a new red roof stops dead at the party wall beside its mossy neighbour. Then the top, and Chapel Lane, and the Seamen's Chapel, white boards going gold, the jam jars waiting in their row along the wall.

## The lantern walk

They come up the lane in ones and twos as the light goes. June with her apron off. Wilf, varnish on his knuckles. Teddy, his bucket empty; Admiral went back at teatime. Sal out of uniform, in a red jumper. Dot and Ivy arm in arm, a step at a time. Rob Garrow, who nods at you. A man with binoculars on a strap round his neck, off the last Puffin, whom the others call Cyril. Skipper, awake for once, on a lead. The brass band, four of them: cornet, two horns and a euphonium, and a gap where a tuba would stand.

You have no jar, and before you can mind, June presses one into your hands, the candle already lit. Someone strikes up "Abide with Me", and the town goes down Wren Steps, the lights bobbing between the houses, past the Hales' open window, past the cat, down to the Strand. At the quay's edge everyone sets their jar down in a row, yours among them, and stands back; and the harbour holds two rows of lights, the one on the granite and the one in the water underneath it.

Nobody says much. The band plays one more, quietly. The Puffin knocks against the steps. Up on the hill your blue window is dark, and it occurs to you that you know which one it is.
