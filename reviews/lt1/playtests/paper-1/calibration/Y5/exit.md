# Exit interview, Y5: a paper version of Papers, Please

## Marks, round 3
- Turn 3.1 (Moravec): engaged. Her answer, "Nobody in that queue has been spoken to all day. Should they have?", is the only reply in the whole game that sounded like a person managing me. The question cost 15 minutes and told me nothing I could act on, but it bought a character. Surprise: delight, small. She turned my question round.
- Turn 3.2 (Soler): curious. He took the coins first, one at a time, then the papers, and said nothing more about his mother. I predicted a plea and was wrong, and the silence was better than the plea. I will never know if the mother was real. Surprise: delight, small.
- Turn 3.3 (Volkov): bored. A date, a stamp, a man saying "yesterday" again. I predicted Danik would walk over and he did not. Nothing came back. Surprise: none.

## Exit questions

**1. Overall pull for this game: 5.** I would play a day 8 only to see whether Danik does anything. If day 8 is a fresh set of single-fault documents and the bills fit again, I quit at the first entrant.

**2. Against the real Papers, Please.** I know the real game. The paper version keeps the bulletin rule, the arithmetic of bills against wages, and the bribe-and-pressure offers. It loses what actually creates the pressure in the real game: speed. In the real one I am fumbling, the queue is moving and a missed discrepancy costs me while I look. Here I read every document at leisure, and by round 1 I had worked out the whole day on a sheet: nine slots, 5 each, 55 in bills. From that point the pressure was a spreadsheet, and by round 3 the spreadsheet had gone slack, with 35 spare minutes and 9 spare credits. The real game keeps squeezing because mistakes pile up and the family gets sick, and here nobody got worse. Every entrant also had one obvious fault or none, so the work was lookup, not detection. The real game has ambiguous cases and rules you half remember. This one never made me doubt a call except Halbert, and I never found out whether I was right. What the paper version does better: the Danik offer and the newspaper are sharper than the real game's equivalents, because they connected. The ten credits for framing a journalist, then her column in tomorrow's paper naming no one, is a consequence I did not see coming and could not have read off the rulebook.

**3. Best and worst.**
- Best: Danik's offer, and the headline the next morning. Refusing him cost nothing today, but it left a mark on the world (she wrote the story because I let her through). Second best: Brasko, where the door shut and nobody explained.
- Worst: the risk never became real. The two free warnings were never touched. The bonus loosened the sums on the very turn the moral test arrived, so my refusal of Danik cost me nothing and proved nothing about me. Questions had a price and no information value, so a rational player never asks, and I asked twice and learned nothing that changed a stamp. Every denied person simply leaves and the world goes silent, so none of the consequences I was told about (cold, hunger, sickness) were ever shown as anything but a table at the end. The family is four words and a tick. "Nine credits in the tin" is a reward screen, not a life.

**Would I quit?** Not today, because Danik hooked me. Tomorrow, yes, unless something he or the paper does reaches me at the window.
