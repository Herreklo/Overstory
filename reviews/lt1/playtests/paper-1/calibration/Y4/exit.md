# Exit interview: Y4, Return of the Obra Dinn (paper), the *Hesper*

## 1. Marks, round 3

- Turn 3.1: engaged. The short, wide, clumsy shroud held a stout man in a blue coat with a slit in the left side, and the watch gave "This is for George." My prediction (Carr, killed by Thomas Pell) was right, and the motive closes the loop between the bread room and the cable tier cleanly. It does not say why Thomas was chained afterwards, and I like that. Two witnesses are frozen on the quarterdeck (sketch 12 pockmarked, sketch 10 ginger beard and bonnet) and I want their names. Surprise: delight. The stiff brown cloth round the slit matched the knife in the memory, so I could check the body against the scene.
- Turn 3.2: engaged. A long-haired man with an earring and a nose stitch is Jens Holm (sketch 11), and he is the first death with no villain: wet to the chest, then cold. The stove scene is the richest so far. Sketch 3 is the Swedish voice, so Ekström. The Finnish voice is Rautio (6). The older Hull voice is Whitlock (1), who tells Ekström and Rautio to "try again at first light". The neat stitch through the nose shows the sailmaker was still working on 15 January and had stopped by 9 February. That is a real chain of inference. Surprise: delight. I am slightly unsure whether the book wants "froze" or "drowned" for him.
- Turn 3.3: curious. No paper ash in the stove, so the log was taken, not burnt. The chart line is ruled firm to Carlö with no dates, so it is a plan. The bunk is stripped, and Ned's pinned cat drawing ties the boy to the captain. These are good loose ends, but the cabin hands me no owner for any of them. Surprise: delight. The cat drawing is a small object that asks a question. I got no cheat anywhere this round.

## 2. Overall pull

8. I want round 4 and I have a list of things to chase: the captain's departure, the ice trip at first light, who chained Thomas and why the key hangs where it does, and the two quarterdeck witnesses. What holds the score down is that nothing has been confirmed. Three rounds in, I have written no fate and the book has told me nothing. If six more rounds passed like that I would stop trusting my own list and start to drift.

Where I stand, tentatively:
- Sure: 1 Whitlock, 2 Carr, 3 Ekström, 4 Dunning, 5 Morgan, 6 Rautio, 7 Moss, 8 George Pell, 9 Thomas Pell, 11 Holm, 13 Ned, 14 Lind.
- Probable: 10 is Reid (a bonnet is Scots, he is from Leith) and 12 is Quill, by elimination.
- Fates I would write: Carr stabbed by Thomas. George clubbed by Carr. Dunning crushed, by the ship. Holm froze. Thomas froze (or starved, by whoever chained and left him). Nine people are still unaccounted for.

## 3. Compared with the real game

The paper version beats it in density. Every turn gives a clue and I never wander empty decks. The shroud stitching, the cook's trussing needle, the empty peg and the bright key among old ones are the kind of planted detail I would have missed in the 3D game or found by luck. It loses in three ways.

- The game master answers "look closely" with a full inventory. In the real game I choose where to look and I find things by scanning. The thrill of spotting a tiny figure in the corner of a frozen scene is gone, and I only learn what I think to ask.
- Writing fates costs a turn out of three. In the real game it is free and constant. Here every turn spent on looking is a turn not spent on writing, so I keep choosing to look and never get the confirmation beat (three at once), which is half the real game's rhythm.
- The scenes are short voice lists plus a tableau. I miss the audio laid over a replayable moment, and the timeline gaps are filled in for me where the real game makes me work them out.

The voices-to-names matching ("Mr Carr", "George", "Mr Ekström") is also more generous than the real game, where names are rarer.

## 4. Best and worst

Best: the bodies cross-check the scenes. The slit coat matches the knife, the nose stitch matches the sailmaker, and the shroud stitching dates the death. Each one lets me deduce something instead of being told. A close second is the cable tier: the ankle scab, the dry cask, the untouched bread and the key just out of reach tell a story that no memory has told yet.

Worst: no feedback. I cannot tell whether my deductions are right or whether I am piling guesses on top of a wrong one. The three-turn cap also stops me from following a lead and writing it up in the same round. A smaller gripe is that the "who" of the Thomas imprisonment is withheld across three rounds, which is good tension but starts to feel like it is being saved for the end.

I would not quit. I would ask for a way to write fates without spending a turn.
