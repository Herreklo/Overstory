# Round 2

## Marks
- Turn 1.1: engaged — the whole lip on the floor man and the white scar on the ladder man settle which brother is which, and the hairs on the pin give me the weapon. I got the answer by looking, which is what I wanted. Surprise: none — it confirmed what I had worked out from the voices.
- Turn 1.2: engaged — Carr's mouth is set, "nothing startled", his belt empty and his cheeks already fallen in. Moss holds a bright new key among three old ones, and Lind has a cracked lens and a brown card book. None of it is explained and all of it looks planted. Surprise: delight — small items that ask a question are what I came for.
- Turn 1.3: engaged — the man in irons is the ladder brother, not Carr, so my prediction was wrong. Surprise: delight — it was fair, since the scar was in the sketch, and I had simply not followed my own lip. The chain just short of the ladder, the key just out of reach and the dry cask are a cruel arrangement, and the nine marks make no sense against 73 days.

## Pull
9 — I now have a killing, a surviving brother who died chained 73 days later, and no idea who left him there. I want the why.

## Thinking aloud
Settled: Sketch 2 is Carr, who clubbed George Pell (Sketch 8) with a pin. Sketch 9 is Thomas Pell, the ladder man, found chained on 23 April, calling for "Ned" with no answer. The crust is untouched with its cloth still folded, the cask is dry and its bung is out, and the key hangs beyond the chain. Someone chained him and left, and I don't know whether that was cruelty or carelessness. The nine marks cannot be days since the killing. They may be days since he was ironed, so about 14 April, or days since Ned last came. Thomas was calling for the boy because the boy used to bring him water, which would mean Ned was kind to him.

Carr was guarding "the boy's share, and the clerk's". Why those two? The clerk is a paying passenger, and I'd bet Whitlock set that rule. I also want to know who sewed the shrouds. George's is clumsy, so the sailmaker was probably not sewing by then, which could be a way of ordering the deaths. Fates are still unwritten. I'll write George and Carr once I have a third solid one, and the cause for Thomas is only "froze" so far.

## Prediction
The key on the nail will be the bright, square-bit kind like Moss's new one, not one of his old three. So the same hand that cut a spare for the bread room made the key for Thomas's band, and the key was hung out of reach on purpose.

## Needs
- Who chained Thomas, and when. Nothing yet separates 9 February from 23 April.
- Whether the key on the nail fits the band, and whether it matches Moss's new one.
- Whether the rest of George's body or clothes show a key or a file. The room was locked, so how did he get in?
- Which shroud is whose, and who sewed the neat ones and who the clumsy.
- Where Ned is now. Nobody here is the boy.

## Turns
1. Stay in the cable tier and look closely at the key on the nail (bit shape, new or old), the band's lock (forced or turned), the cloth round the crust (what it is, any mark), the cask bung, and the nine marks (what cut them, and whether a knife is in his reach). Check the jacket pockets and collar for a name. — Expect: the key is the bright one, the lock is turned not forced, and the cloth is a boy's neckerchief or a scrap of nightshirt.
2. Go aft to the sail locker. Look at George's opened shroud: pockets, a key, anything in his clothes. Compare the stitching on all four shrouds side by side, and check the sailmaker's bench and kit for when it was last used. — Expect: George has a key or filed brass on him, and the neat stitches match the palm-and-needle work of the man in the fur hat.
3. Cut open the other clumsy shroud and hold the watch to it. If the key in turn 1 was not the bright one, take the neat shroud nearest the door instead. — Expect: the second clumsy shroud was sewn by the same hand as George's and is a death from about the same weeks.
