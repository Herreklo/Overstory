# Exit interview: Saltlight

## 1. Marks for round 3

- Turn 3.1: curious. I asked my own question about step 20 and got a real answer ("Mrs Pennock went to her son's"). Dot calling the peeling paint "pretty, all those colours coming through" was a nice touch. Then the screen had me say "Anything in the paper?", which I didn't say, and the sisters did their "we see everyone" line again, word for word. So it is a loop. Surprise: delight. Ivy's sailor in Montevideo who married a girl in Valparaíso, and "Best thing that ever happened to her." That was the warmest moment in the game, and I didn't do anything to get it.
- Turn 3.2: engaged. The paint in layers like tree rings, white over green over ochre, and the brighter rectangle of wallpaper where a picture hung. That's the best writing in the game: a house that tells you someone lived there a long time and has gone. Surprise: none. I guessed FOR SALE. And there was nothing I could do with it. No one to tell, nothing to fix or keep.
- Turn 3.3: engaged. Sal pressing a lit jar into my hands before I could take one is the first time anyone in Wrenmouth did something for me without being asked. Everyone coming, Teddy swinging his jar too hard, a band with no tuba. Surprise: delight, Sal's jar. Rob came and stood with his hands in his pockets, so my prediction was half right, and the game did nothing with him.
- Turn 3.4: would quit. I asked Wilf the one question the game had set up for me, why Rob doesn't talk to the passengers, and the screen swapped it for "Tell me about the Puffin." That's the fourth time my words became a menu line. Then it got confusing: he says his father built "her", no nails, larch on oak. The Puffin is a blue motorboat and the Marigold is the wooden boat on trestles, so which did his father build? Then the walk happened around me. I set my jar down where everyone set theirs. It was beautiful, two rows of candles, one of them in the water, and I watched it. Surprise: cheat. My own words were thrown away again, on the question that mattered most.

## 2. Overall pull

3. It started at 7 and went 6, 4, 3. I said I'd give it the lantern walk, and that if I only watched it I'd be done. I mostly watched it.

## 3. Best and worst

**Best:** the places. The mooring rings polished on top, the handrail worn bright in the middle, the chandler's window rubbed clear at a child's nose height, the flaking house at step 20, the new red roof at step 60. Things in this town look like they've been used and lived in, and they made me wonder more than any person did. Also Admiral the crab, Ivy's sailor, Sal's jar, and my room in the rain with the water going pewter.

**Worst:** nothing I did mattered, and the game didn't listen. In order:
- My own words were replaced by menu lines four times, though the store page says I can "put it in your own words". The menus never changed either: June still offers "Is my room ready?" after giving me the key.
- The cardigan was the only small job the game handed me, and it was locked to a hook ("You can't take that with you"). Then four people gave the same answer in nearly the same words. The sisters who "see everyone who goes up and everyone who comes down" didn't know whose it was. The darning looked like a clue and wasn't one.
- Belonging went 20, 37, 57 whatever I did. It's a clock with a cosy name. The room filled up with things I didn't choose: a geranium, a cat, a fern. The little empty shelf was the most exciting thing in my room because I thought I'd fill it. I never got to.
- Nobody needed anything from me. Everyone hands over a pretty line and goes back to wiping or knitting.
- Rob, May and the cardigan were all set up and dropped. Cyril appeared at the walk off the last Puffin, someone I'd never met, which felt like a story I wasn't allowed into.
- Sitting ate my morning, and the 10.30 ferry left without me getting a say.

I never sketched anything and never found a keepsake. Partly that's on me. But the game never gave me a reason. 0 of 48 and 0 of 20 felt like a checklist I hadn't been shown how to start.

## 4. Chapter Two? $14.99?

I might open Chapter Two to see the allotments, because allotments mean growing things, and growing things is what I came for. But "new sketches and keepsakes to find" sounds like more of the same, and if the first conversation there swaps my words for a menu, I'd close it. I wouldn't buy it at $14.99. I buy cosy games easily, and $14.99 is cheap, but I'd be paying for the writing about places, and I'd stop within an hour once I saw that the people don't remember me. If it were on sale for a few pounds, maybe, as something to read on a rainy evening, not to play.

## 5. What I was trying to achieve, and what I risked

I was trying to do one small kind thing: get the darned cardigan back to whoever mends their clothes. And I wanted to get to know people well enough that they'd tell me something they wouldn't say on the Strand (Rob), and to make my room mine.

I risked nothing. The game promised that up front ("no fail states, no wrong answers"), and it kept that promise to the point where my choices didn't count either. I'm a cosy player and I don't want to be punished. But in Stardew I still put something on the line: a day's energy, money spent on seeds, crops that die if I forget, a friendship that doesn't grow if I miss a birthday. That's why it feels good when things go right. Here, kindness and doing nothing earned the same 20%. The only thing I lost was the morning ferry, and I didn't choose to lose it.

## Compared with the last game

The paper Stardew was too agreeable: everything I predicted came true and nothing went wrong. Saltlight's problem is worse. The world doesn't listen to me at all. In Stardew people answered what I did. Here they answer a menu.
