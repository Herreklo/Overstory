# Round 3

## Saturday evening

**Turn 3.1. Sketch: Tolley & Son's window**
You stand on the cobbles with the sketchbook on your forearm and draw the chandler's front as the light goes: the gold letters on the fascia, the whitewashed panes, the one clear corner at the height of a child's nose. Inside the corner, small, the coil of rope on its hook.
*Sketchbook: Tolley & Son. Through the clear corner, the rope still waits on its hook.*

**Turn 3.2. June Askew, in the Kettle**
You ask her for a jam jar for tonight.
You say: "What's good here?"
"The lemon cake. It's always the lemon cake." She lifts a glass dome an inch, as if to prove it. "Sit yourself down. Tea's on the house: you live here now."
She goes back to the window table, wiping. "You'll come to the lantern walk tonight? Everyone goes. You'll love it."
*Next time, with June:* 1. "Is my room ready?" 2. "What's good here?" 3. "What a lovely place."

**Turn 3.3. Dot and Ivy Hale, at step 40**
The window swings open before you've knocked. Two faces, very alike, one rounder.
You say: "Anything in the paper?"
Ivy shakes out the Wrenmouth Gazette and reads, to Dot and now to you: "'The council has repainted the railings on the Strand.'" She lowers it.
Dot, not looking up from the blue scarf: "We see everyone who goes up and everyone who comes down." Ivy: "And we wave at all of them."
"We were born in this house," Dot says. "Both of us, in the back bedroom."
Ivy waves at you, though you're right there.
*Next time, with Dot and Ivy:* 1. "Good morning, ladies." 2. "What are you knitting?" 3. "Anything in the paper?"

**Turn 3.4. To the Seamen's Chapel**
You climb the last of the steps in the dusk to Chapel Lane. The chapel door stands open and candlelight comes out across the grass. The jam jars that waited on the wall all day are going one by one into people's hands.

## The lantern walk

They come up from every door: June with her apron off; Wilf with varnish still on his knuckles; Teddy, Admiral let go at teatime, a jar held out in front of him in both hands; Sal out of uniform, in a green anorak; Dot and Ivy arm in arm, a step at a time; Rob Garrow, saying nothing; a man with binoculars round his neck, off the last Puffin; Skipper on a lead at the back. The brass band is four players and no tuba.
Someone in the crowd presses a spare jar into your hands, a candle stub already lit inside, and the procession starts down.
Wren Steps in the dark is a river of small lights. The band plays something slow that everyone half knows. At step 40 the Hales stop to rest and wave at the people behind them; at step 50 the cat watches from under his eave. At the bottom the jars are set down one by one along the quay's edge, yours among them, and the harbour holds two rows of lights: one on the stone and one in the black water under it.
People stand about in twos and threes, talking low, while the band packs up.

The candles burn down. One by one people pick up their jars and go home up the steps. You stand a while longer on the Strand, then let yourself in at the side door of the Kettle and go up to the room with the blue window.
