# Round 3

## Marks
- Turn 2.1: engaged — Teddy owns the rubbed corner and says "just rope", which matches what I saw, so two of us agree on one thing. Admiral the crab, caught every morning and let go at teatime, is a small picture of a boy who repeats his day. But the whitewash is on the inside, so he can't have rubbed it from the quay, and I want to know how he did. Surprise: delight — Admiral, who "always comes back".
- Turn 2.2: engaged — my repair guess was wrong: same wood, same grain, same age, only the varnish differs. Being wrong sent me somewhere better: he isn't mending a plank, he's keeping one busy. The word-for-word repeat of "She'll see me out, this one" read like a recording. Surprise: delight — a negative result that still counts as a find.
- Turn 2.3: curious — plain answers: Arthur Tolley retired inland to a daughter in '88; May Ord sat on the bench every afternoon. But both got "Lovely" and nothing else, and the dodge I predicted never came. Either May is nothing, or June's smoothness is the thing. Surprise: none.

## Pull
7 — I have more threads than at the start (cardigan, photograph, the sisters, Rob's silence) and a clock. But Belonging pays +14 for visiting, not for working anything out, and the journal keeps deciding for me.

## Thinking aloud
Everyone here does one thing over and over: Wilf the same plank, Rob the same rope, June the same table for thirty-one years, Teddy the same crab, Sal the same letters. Either the town is stuck on something, or the game is lazy. I'll bet on the first and look for whoever breaks the pattern. The sign says Tolley & Son, but Arthur went to a daughter, so where is the Son? The calendar stopped at March 1988 and the shop has been shut six years with its rope left on the hook. The journal wrote "I think he's right" about the chandler's holding nothing but rope. I don't: Teddy left out the empty shelves and the calendar. I'm in the Kettle at five, in good light, with a dusk walk ahead. Two things within arm's reach: a grey cardigan on a hook, which the noticeboard says was found on the steps, and a black-framed photograph by the till. I'd rather work out whose the cardigan is than be handed it.

## Prediction
The grey cardigan has hung on that hook for weeks, June knows exactly whose it is, and it belongs to one of the Hale sisters. The notice is a polite way to make one of them come down the steps. June keeps things where passers-by will see them and doesn't say more than she's asked.

## Needs
- How long a turn takes. Four turns got me from 8:10 to midday, three from midday to five. I can't plan to be at the chapel at dusk.
- Did the 13.00 and 15.30 sailings run? Rob is in the stern at midday and at five.
- Can I pick up and carry the cardigan, or only look?
- Is the whitewash on the inside or out? How did Teddy rub it from the quay?
- Can I write or fix a line in my own journal?

## Turns
1. Inspect the grey cardigan on the hook (not taking it): name tape, darns, wear at the elbows, pockets, a dust line on the hook, how long it has hung there. — Expect: hand-darned, smells of mothballs or lavender, worn at the cuffs like an old woman's. A detail that points to one of the sisters.
2. Inspect the black-framed photograph by the till. — Expect: an old shot of the Strand with the chandler's open, maybe May Ord or Arthur Tolley in it, with a date or caption.
3. Talk to June in my own words: whose is the cardigan, and how long has it hung there? If the photograph showed someone, ask who. If she'll let me, ask to carry it up to ask around. — Expect: she answers too quickly, and a name comes with a smile instead of a reason.
4. Walk up Wren Steps to the geranium window and talk to Dot and Ivy in my own words, about the cardigan if I have it, otherwise about what they see from that window. — Expect: they've watched everything on the stair, and one of them goes quiet at the cardigan.
