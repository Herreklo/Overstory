# Exit interview · The Courier (harbour town, June 1990 to July 1991)

## 1. Marks for round 6

- Turn 6.1: curious. She was in her shed with something half mended on the bench, and she turned back to it when I asked who looks after her. That's a person, not a source. She gave me her husband's stroke and kept it off the record, and he turned out to be Mark Holmes, who was drinking at the Anchor the next night. I still don't know why she rang us. Surprise: delight at the shed and the bench. And she was eerie for me: I sat the hearing on her death in another game.
- Turn 6.2: engaged. At last someone on Ken's list put his name to all three answers: "Terry was found out over the claims." But "thirty-odd years he's given that office" is now word for word from four people. And after a year of chasing, the claims come to "a few pounds, how many I couldn't tell you." Surprise: delight that he stood by it. Cheat on the echo.
- Turn 6.3: bored. "There was a mistake in the claims. That's all I'll say," exactly as a year ago. On its own it was a dead turn. Later the five weeks used the repeat as the point of the front ("word for word the same"), which was clever. But I didn't get to do that. It was done for me. Surprise: none.
- Turn 6.4: engaged, then flat. Ken said "Hold it" and I held, as my plan said. That was fair, and it was the only real choice of the round. Mary's front came out as two bullets again, I left inside 1 empty again, and sales fell. Surprise: none.

## 2. Would the five weeks hold me?

No. Pull 3. The story I chased for a year, the one with Ken's own family in it, finally ran on 11 July, and the payoff was "Nobody complains." Terry didn't react, and neither did Toby Waller, Sylvia, Lily or the readers. Patience went down one and back one, a wash. Then the tip book was empty for a week. The race "goes on" with no one saying who decides, and I asked who decides three times. Trust is 10 and the job is mine for good, so nothing is at risk. And the game played my character for five weeks. It played me well, it argued the way I would, but a game that can summarise me that accurately has nothing left for me to do.

## 3. Keep playing, buy it, come back?

I wouldn't keep playing this build, and I wouldn't buy it as it is. If I came back after a week, it would be to read next year's bound volume and see what the town did, not to play another week of interviews. That page is the hook. The play around it isn't one yet.

## 4. What kept me

- **Ken.** A compromised editor with a veto over his own family. "Take her name out." I argued, and "Run it, then." It was the only scene where I stood in the room and it went my way because of what I said.
- **The tip book as a weapon.** Sylvia and Jonah rang an hour apart, each about Lily, and Lily got the silent counter. Three people were using my paper on each other.
- **The town remembers me.** "You've been asking about me." Gary was angry that we ignored him until the county paper ran him. Greta said "It's in the paper now. Nothing to be done about it."
- **Answers that broke my theories.** Holmes: "I knew. The whole yard knew. Her work was still poor."
- **The year page.** Gary walked out and turned up at the sawmill, Greta came through it, Terry stepped down. It's history I can read.
- **Evidence discipline.** I was never caught out, and that was earned.

## 5. Where I nearly quit

- In round 0, at Ken's family tree: nine names in one breath.
- In round 3, a whole turn to learn the council's office hours.
- Rounds 4 and 5: Lily, then Terry, then Lily again, then a watch where "nothing happens". Four dead turns out of six.
- The five weeks, at "Nobody complains." That's where I'd actually quit.

## 6. What was hollow

- **The interview slot machine.** No comment, no comment, then one line when I ask for print. Every wall had the same shape, and the same sentences ("Not about Terry. He's my chief officer") came from different mouths.
- **Gossip only ever punished.** Being talked about killed my turns and never opened a door. And source trust sat at 2 of 3 whatever I did: whether I apologised, burned someone or got a door shut in my face. Jonah went 0, 1, 0 and I was never told why.
- **No self, in the sixth round of asking.** No name, no past, no want, no tie to anyone. My colleagues cost a turn to talk to. Toby wants Ken's job and Susie left, and none of it touched me. In BG3, camp is free and it's half the game.
- **Ethics with no weight.** I printed a woman's cancer, taken from a workmate's mouth, without asking her. I printed "gone to pieces" next to it. Trust went up.
- **Stakes that ran out.** I had the job by round 3, so rounds 4 to 6 were a sandbox with a sales number that moved for reasons nobody gave.
- **The edition is a receipt.** Two bullets stand for a 500-word front, I never see the page, and the readers never speak.
- **A year's chase ends on nothing checkable.** "A few pounds", no amount, no record, no decider.
- **Names.** There are two Tobys, two Terrys, two Colins and two Ivans, and Tanners and Holmeses everywhere.

## 7. Which game does this better

Pentiment: a newcomer in a small town over years, where people remember and resent your questions, you pick whom to blame and live with it, and you have a self. Disco Elysium for interviews where how you ask, and who you are, changes the answer, and where failure gives content. Papers, Please for the desk under orders, with your family in the balance. Of the games I've played here, this beats Saltlight and Answerable: my choices moved numbers, I was in the room with Ken once, and the town remembered me. But it is well below Hob's Ford, which had companions and a dilemma where every answer cost someone.

## 8. What I would change

1. Give me a self: a name, a reason I came, a want the town can press on.
2. Make the newsroom my camp. Talking to Ken, Toby and Susie is free, they have agendas and approval, and they react to my pages. Make me choose between Toby's bid and Ken.
3. Break the template. Answers should depend on what I bring and how I ask. A "no comment" should leak something: a look, a name. Trust should move visibly, and I should be told why.
4. Printing should cost. Letters, complaints, a correction demanded, a source who won't speak to me again, Greta's family at my desk. "Nobody complains" should be rare and earned.
5. Keep stakes after the job: a rival at the county paper, a date and a decider for the chief officer's chair, a story that costs Ken his partner if it runs.
6. Show the edition as a page, and the town reading it at the Anchor the next night.
7. No dead turns: a bare notice board is free, and a watch always shows who comes and goes.
8. Fewer shared names, or a person card I can pin.

## 9. What I'd lose if I stopped now

Little, and that's the verdict. I'd lose next year's volume, and whether Ken finally breaks between his partner and his brother. I'd lose Mary Tanner while she can still talk. I wouldn't lose a story of my own, because I never had one. Nothing of mine is at risk.

## 10. What I believe happened

- **Terry Sutton.** He made a small mistake in his expense claims, a few pounds, and nobody would say what for. His own office used it. Jonah tipped us in June 1990. Lily raised it in front of the office in February. Sylvia, Jonah and Lily all want his chair. He admitted "a mistake" twice, word for word, and gave nothing more. In April he said he'd go next year, "64, and tired." I believe he isn't a crook. He's being worn out by three subordinates, and the claims were their lever. Medium confidence: I never saw a record.
- **Greta Brook.** She had fifteen months of bad terms with John Holmes, who told her how to do everything. She was diagnosed with cancer in February 1990, her work slipped, and she snapped at him in the yard. He knew she was ill, as the whole yard did, and he let her go for poor work anyway. She had no one close and walked the shore alone. By January 1991 she'd come through both. I believe the feud was the cause, the illness was what made the work slip, and Holmes used the work as his reason. He's hard, not lying.
- **Gary Hughes.** He came to the garage in February 1990. In June he was passed over for Sally Baker, who had been there two months and had "the stronger record". He believed he'd done the better work. Our paper ignored him until the county paper ran it. He walked out in July, stayed in town, and in May 1991 took the sawmill job as "the only one looking for work", likely the gap Greta's going left. I believe pride drove him out, and I don't know whether my piece helped push him.
