# Exit interview · Thursday's Paper

## 1. Marks for round 6

- Turn 6.1: engaged — I'd asked about kin for six rounds, and Susie finally answered: Neil is her son, and Leo is his father. But she answered about Neil and Leo and left Lily out. Then "Not through me" closed the only door I had. Surprise: delight, a small one — kin at last, from a person, not a hover.
- Turn 6.2: would quit — three bare "No comment"s. Everyone else here gives a reason when they refuse ("that's my family", "I work with her"). Neil gives none, and that difference is the only clue the turn produced. I can't tell whether it's a tell or just a refusal, and the game gives me no second way in. Surprise: none.
- Turn 6.3: curious — the scoop was the most interesting thing in the round, more than Peter. Mary Tanner's husband is Mark Holmes, so surnames don't mark families, and my Tanner and Lyons chains were built on sand. "Another stroke" sits oddly against Eve's December tip, which said Mark was the one who had a stroke. Either both of them had one or someone got it wrong, and I can't tell which. The husband told the county paper what everyone tells me is "not for print: that's my family". I held the strokes back and never asked the family, so that loss is mine, and fair. Peter, though, answered "why next year" with the notice, and "both want it, and it shows" is the sentence Terry used in May. Surprise: cheat — "and it shows" is a reused sentence, not a man talking.
- Turn 6.4: bored — my front was one quote that repeats the March notice, plus a date. Trust rose to 9 for it. I'm paid for not being wrong. Surprise: none.

## 2. Would the tenth hour hold me?

No. The summary says "you play on as you have", and it can, because once I had found my policy (principals about themselves, on the record; soften when asked) the game could play it for me. Five weeks go by and nothing moves. In the race "nobody has given way since May", trust tops out at 10 and patience sits at 4. The one event that touches Ken's family, Colin, comes in Gary Hughes's exact words ("to start again somewhere else"), and a second Gary arrives at the garage. If an hour can be summarised without my losing anything, it wasn't play. Pull for those weeks: 3.

## 3. Would I keep playing, buy it, come back?

I'd play on to see who gets the chief officer's post, but that's "next year", and nothing suggests I'd learn anything on the way. I wouldn't buy it as it stands. I might at a small price if the county paper's source could actually be found by working it out. After a week I wouldn't come back, unless the decision was a round away.

## 4. What kept me

- The county paper beating me in round 2. The world moved without me and it cost me, and that made every later turn matter.
- Ken's rule meeting a real story: his brother and his partner after the same post. I asked him what was wrong and he said "Nothing". It was the best decision I made, and it cost me patience.
- Dates I lined up myself: Lily's "In February" against Jonah's call, the office a clerk short for eleven months, Terry's "eleven festivals".
- Learning a rule. People go on record about themselves, and about those they're no longer tied to, but never about those they are. It held for six rounds, and I found it myself.
- The jump to 1991, which tied loose threads (the claims, Lily, Terry going, the race) into one.

## 5. Where I nearly quit

At round 2's empty yard, with nothing to print. At round 1's third turn, when the news reached me through the counter whatever I did. And at round 6.2: I spent two turns on the only question that was mine and got "Not through me" and three no comments, and then the summary said "Nobody has told you who spoke to the county paper." That is where I'd stop.

## 6. What was hollow

- People are a sentence each. Gary says "I'd done the better work" three times. "Whoever's (record) is the stronger" comes from Trevor, Gary, Jonah and Terry. "And it shows" comes twice, and so does "to start again somewhere else". Answers slide off their questions.
- "The record" decides every post, and "nobody keeps it on paper". The thing that decides the town can't be read.
- There was nothing to see. Every watch turn was empty. There's no evidence that isn't a person: no claim form, minutes, ledger, postmark or phone log. A mystery made only of people who won't talk can't be deduced, only waited out.
- The open questions (what the claims were, who found Terry out, who fed the county paper) had no path to an answer that I could find. In the Hesper every gap had a clue in it somewhere.
- My rule means nobody ever accuses anyone on the record, so the paper never carries a real conflict. The one contradiction, Jonah's, was not for print.
- The game rewards attributing, not finding out. My best-selling front was a man saying "That's all I'll say". Trust topped out on fronts that reprinted notices.
- Ken's rule bit once and he gave way at once. Toby wanting the editor's chair never did anything. Eleven months of my notebook were written for me.
- Names leak from Answerable: Driftwood, Sean Hale, a Tanner, a Holmes. If it's the same town, I bring knowledge I didn't earn here.

## 7. Which game does this better?

For deduction: Return of the Obra Dinn, and the paper Hesper I played before this one. The clues are placed and every gap can be answered. For a small town that remembers your choices over years: Pentiment. Its people talk at length and disagree, you choose on partial evidence, and the town tells you what it made of your choice. Here "a year on" was a volume with no correction in it and one line, Jonah's "that's in your paper". For deciding what gets through under pressure, Papers, Please makes its rules cost more. Among the paper games, this sits a little above Answerable for pull, because the stakes bit and the jump paid off. It sits below Answerable for deduction, because Answerable at least had a question to read the evidence against.

## 8. What I'd change

1. Give me evidence that isn't a person: the expense claims, the council minutes, the county paper's reporter, the Courier's own telephone log, who was where and when. Then "who fed the county paper" becomes a deduction, not a wait.
2. Let people speak in their own voices, at length, and let them contradict each other on the record. Drop the shared sentences.
3. Make "the record" a real thing I can read, or show what fills it.
4. Make watching pay: let me see something nobody says.
5. Add kin to the notebook as I learn it, and don't let surnames mislead unless misleading is the point.
6. Reward finding over attributing. A front that tells the town something new should beat a reprinted notice, and a careful but empty front should cost me.
7. Let Ken argue and Toby act. Let a source turn cool on me because of what I printed.
8. Never summarise weeks "as you have". If one policy can play the game, the decisions cost nothing.
9. Bring the payoff within reach: decide the post inside the game.

## 9. What would I lose if I stopped now?

The decision on the chief officer's post. And the chance, which I don't believe in, that someone tells me who spoke to the county paper. Little else.

## 10. What I believe happened, and why

- **Terry Sutton** claimed for something he shouldn't have, and the whole office knew. Lily said it out loud in February 1990, and Jonah thought it was "done with". By June someone at the office had given it to the county paper. I think it was Sylvia. She wanted his post, and as the editor's partner she could never give it to the Courier. My second guess is Toby, who wants Ken's chair and takes every tip that comes in. Lily's denial reads true. Terry kept the post, said "I was found out" in his accuser's words, and ten months later announced he'd go, at 64. I don't believe the claims had nothing to do with it, but I can't show it. My confidence is low.
- **Greta Brook**, at 66, was slowing at the mill. John let her go instead of letting her retire, and she snapped at him for it. When I put "poor for months" to her she didn't deny it. She said "John let me go", and that she'd been in this town all her life. It was pride, not a wrong done to her. Her place stood empty for eleven months.
- **Gary Hughes** did the better work at the garage, as Trevor said privately, but lost the post to Sally on a "record" nobody could show him. He walked out in July. I don't think he ever left the town. The May notice says only that he "started work at the sawmill", not that he moved to the town, and John says he was "the only one looking for work". So he spent ten months without work, took the job of a woman of 66, and still says the one thing he has: "I'd done the better work there." The garage stayed short for a year until another Gary came.
