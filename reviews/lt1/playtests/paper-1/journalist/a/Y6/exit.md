# Exit interview: Thursday's Paper (Y6, optimiser)

## 1. Marks, round 6
- Turn 6.1: bored. Audrey confirmed the ankle in nearly Eve's words and gave no reason for handing over harbour day ("Harry Myers took it over last year"). My prediction came true almost word for word. Surprise: none.
- Turn 6.2: curious. An empty quay on harbour day's Saturday looked like the first scoop I'd found myself, news from an absence. The rest was two roll calls (fourteen swimmers, thirteen on the boats) and Sylvia's washing. Surprise: cheat, in hindsight. Harbour day ran on the 22nd, so my front was wrong, and it cost nothing: no correction, and trust rose to 9 that same week.
- Turn 6.3: would quit. Sam Brook gave "Not me", "I wouldn't know", "No comment" and the fourth "I couldn't tell you. It's done." The turn bought nothing. Surprise: none.
- Turn 6.4: engaged. The print-wrong probe finally fired. Ken's mother rang, a correction is booked, and patience fell from 4 to 1, the biggest move in six editions. She's the first person in the game who told it her way. Surprise: delight, with two complaints. I can't split the −3 between the override, the guess, the mother's call and the 53 lost copies. And readers' trust didn't move, even though a woman credited me with the festival that morning. That breaks my lag model, unless the guess cancelled it out.

## 2. Would the next five weeks hold me?
No. They prove the game is solved. The game master ran my three-line policy on autopilot: comply with every cut, visit Eve, print every for-print line. It took readers' trust from 8 to 10, the maximum, at about 897 copies a week, and patience climbed back from 1 to 3 without a single decision. A policy I wrote in round 5 as a probe beats the game.

Three things happened that the policy didn't choose, and they're the best of the game. The harbour day proved my front wrong. The Greta piece sold 932, the best week, and cost me five sources. Colin left the clinic, and I can't print it. All three arrived in a summary, not on a turn where I decided. The game's one real social cost was paid by autopilot.

## 3. Keep playing? Buy it? Come back after a week?
- **Keep playing:** one or two rounds, to chase Colin and find out whether Greta fell a second time. No further.
- **Buy it:** not as it stands; I solved it in six rounds. I'd pay a small-game price if sources had reasons and trust could fall.
- **Come back after a week:** no. The meters are maxed and the people give the same lines, so another week means the same town.

## 4. What kept me?
- **The meters, as a puzzle.** Sales track novelty: old news cost 9 copies, and a fresh fall sold 917. Trust looks like lagged vindication, since every rise came with a reader at the counter saying an old story was right. Patience is the only thing I can lose, and I'm still fitting it. Reverse-engineering the three was the real game for me.
- **The editor's family.** Ken's mother, partner and brother are in nearly every story, so the conflict of interest is built into the town and every override costs. That's the best idea here.
- **Cross-referencing the page doesn't do for you.** Audrey ran harbour day in 1989 and Harry in 1990, and Audrey's ankle came in May, between them. Sutton "first spoke of" the 1989 festival in May, and it was a washout in December; here he is in May again with "this summer". I made those connections myself.
- **"Yes, Mother."** That one telephone call did more than six rounds of interviews.

## 5. Where did you nearly quit?
- **4.3, Mark Holmes:** a third person saying "I couldn't tell you. It's done." word for word. That line kills any reason to ask why, and asking why is a reporter's job.
- **The year-on page (pull 4):** I'd won the job without knowing why. The promised look at "what the town made of your pages" was "No corrections. Nobody wrote in." Nothing new was set.
- **5.1, the Anchor:** I watched the obvious place on the obvious night. I got twenty-one names and "Nobody argues, wins anything or plays", and the county paper had the raffle next morning.
- **6.3, Sam Brook:** the same line, for the fourth time.

## 6. What was hollow
- **People are lookup tables.** Answers don't follow the questions: "Which steps?" "The steps." "The day?" "May." Unprompted items come from a template ("X took me out on the boat", "X and I danced at the Anchor"). Three people said the same six words four times. Nobody has a reason to tell it their way, though that's the store page's first promise.
- **No source is ever wrong, and no two disagree.** Every account in six editions matched. So checking a story bought nothing, and the dominant strategy was to print every for-print line and check none. The autopilot proved it.
- **Trust only goes up.** It never fell once in eleven editions, not even for a wrong front page.
- **The punishment is backwards.** My one honest line, marked *inferred, mine*, drew a correction. My unmarked inference in a front-page headline, "No harbour day this year", was wrong and cost nothing. The exploit: never mark a guess, and put it in the headline.
- **"For print" is a rule by subject, not a source's choice.** Coworkers and family are locked whatever a source's trust. So the nurse can phone in a man's operation for print and his wife can't, though Thea told me on day one that patients don't go in a paper. There's no model of harm until the Greta backlash, and that happened offscreen.
- **Sources don't grow.** Eve went to 3 of 3 after one visit; everyone else sat at 2 for a year. The source economy is one tap.
- **The edition is a fill, not a choice.** I never had more good stories than slots. My only decision each Wednesday was whether to override or comply on Ken's family.
- **Watching is a roll call.** Fourteen swimmers named, thirteen boat crew named, and nothing happens.
- **The formulas are hidden.** In the Slay the Spire port I could check every sum; here one −3 has four possible causes.
- **The look back was empty.** The best promise on the store page gave nothing.

## 7. Which game does this better?
- **The Republia Times (Lucas Pope):** the same frame, an editor choosing stories with family at stake. Its two meters move both ways and every choice costs, all in ten minutes.
- **Headliner: NoviNews:** what you print visibly changes the town, and your family and neighbours react on screen. That's the consequence this game buries in a summary.
- **Return of the Obra Dinn:** deduction against a truth you can check. You learn you're right in threes, and a wrong guess is possible and costs. Here the truth arrives as a stranger at the counter.
- **Her Story:** questions that come back with real answers.

## 8. What would you change?
1. Give every source a version of events and a stake in it. Let two accounts of one event disagree, with a third thing (a record, a trace, a witness) to settle it. Without that, accuracy is free, and the only way to print wrong is my own guess.
2. Make answers answer the question, or refuse with a reason. Drop "I couldn't tell you. It's done." Make a refusal information in itself: who won't talk, and about what.
3. Let readers' trust fall. A wrong front should cost. An unmarked inference should be the riskiest line on the page, and a marked one should cost less.
4. After each edition, show what moved and why, for example "trust +1: a reader confirmed Audrey's ankle". An optimiser engages harder with rules he can audit.
5. Make "for print" a negotiation. Let me spend a source's trust, promise to leave a name out, or bring a second source to move an item onto the record. Then sources become an economy, not a tap.
6. Put more stories on the desk than there are slots, so the edition is a real choice.
7. Put the consequences on my turns. The Greta backlash and the mother's call are the two best moments in the game: make them land where I decide, and show them in the bound volume a year on.
8. Set a goal after the job: a long story (the steps, the festival, Colin) that lands only if it's built over weeks, and that changes something in the town when it does.
9. Tell me when things happen, like the raffle night or this year's harbour day. A watch should catch an event if I read the clues right.
10. One Toby per town.

## 9. What would you lose if you stopped now?
Not much. Trust is at 10, the job is kept, and I've solved the meters except patience. What's left is Colin Berry leaving the clinic, the first story I'd actually want to print, and it's Ken's family. Then there's whether Greta fell a second time on steps nobody repaired, whether Sutton's festival is 1989 again, and the last term in the patience formula.

## 10. What I believe happened, and why
- **Terry Sutton.** He had an operation in the city in August 1990 and was home within the week. Three sources agree (Mona Sayer, his wife; Eve; and Sutton himself), so I believe it. He fell out with Mark Holmes before Christmas and had words with Jonah Scott in May 1991. He's announcing a festival on the quay "this summer" with no date and nobody in it: Sam and Audrey both said "Not me". He had no comment on what will be different from the 1989 washout. I believe it's 1989 again: the festival slips and fails, or never runs. I think the row with Jonah is about the festival, or about who follows a 64-year-old chief officer just back from an operation. That why is my inference; nobody ever gave me a reason for anything.
- **Greta Brook.** She fell on "the steps" in May 1990 and was laid up for a month. She never said which steps: Audrey's fall was on the gully steps, but Greta's was never placed. She finished at the sawmill at the start of June 1990: "They knew at the sawmill straight off. I'm not at the sawmill now." I believe the fall cost her that job, though nobody said "because". In July 1991 Eve rang the same fall in identical words. Either Greta fell again, or the 1990 fall came in as new and the paper printed old news beside her sawmill line. That pairing reads as the "because" I refused to print in round 3. Her people going quiet afterwards says the piece hurt. I put a second fall at even odds, because the wording is too exact to trust.
- **Colin Berry.** Thea's husband since February 1991 and Andy's father. I believe he has left the clinic, because Eve has never been wrong. I don't know why: nothing outside the clinic confirms it, and Eve won't put it in print. My guess is that his "busy week in May" 1990 was the two falls. His leaving may be a rift with Thea, at work or at home, but I hold that loosely: the only evidence for any why is that he married the woman who runs the clinic in February and left it in July.
