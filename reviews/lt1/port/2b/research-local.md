# Local photographs and the kneeler rule

Research for the stone redraw, 22 October 2026, read online only. Part 1 lists local photographs that no one has fetched. Part 2 checks the kneeler rule against the old textbooks.

**No image was downloaded or opened.** What each photograph shows is read from its record's title, caption and subject tags, so every line in the table says what the record claims, not what the picture shows. Reference images need Øystein's yes, file by file, before anyone fetches them. The files already held, `docs/references/stone/` 01 to 27, are not listed again.

## In short

1. **Twenty candidates, best first.** Sixteen are old photographs (1910 to 1990) from two archives:
   - twelve from Stavanger byarkiv (stavangerbilder.no), nine of them in Gamle Stavanger and Kleivå;
   - four from the University of Bergen's Marcus, of Bergen smau.

   Four are modern Commons photographs with clear licences: three in Bergen and one in Ålesund. The Stavanger records give their full size: archive TIFFs of 2,200 to 4,700 px on the long side. Marcus and DigitaltMuseum records give no pixel size.
2. **The kneeler at the foot of a raked coping is confirmed.** Rivington (1904) names it "the stone at the foot of a coping on a gable or ramped wall". A stair's raked side wall is a ramped wall. Mitchell (1902), Gwilt (1888) and the 1911 Britannica say the same for gables. The stone:
   - has a level bed;
   - tails well into the wall, with a base wider than its height;
   - has the coping's section worked on top.
3. **Corrected: kneelers stand at intervals too, not only at the foot.** Every source puts more of them along a long slope: level-bedded stones that tail into the wall and carry the coping's section. None gives a spacing.
4. **The knee stone at a flight-to-landing break is still unsourced.** No source describes how a stair wall's coping turns from rake to level. The nearest evidence:
   - BTCV builds a head "at a sharp upward break in slope".
   - Gwilt's "ramp" is a concave sweep worked in a coping that joins a higher part to a lower one.
   - Handrail joinery names the concave turn the ramp and the convex turn the knee.

   One stone carrying both a raked and a level top is still our own reasoning. It is plausible at the concave break, where a flight rises from a landing, and has no source at the convex one.
5. **New evidence on the coping itself.** BTCV says slab copes "are always set to a true level", with a stepped top on steeper slopes. Listings show stair walls "stepped in stages" as well as "ramped". A phyllite or slate slab coping on a Stavanger lane may well be stepped, not raked. The Stavanger photographs below are the way to settle it.

## 2. The kneeler rule

**Our rule.** Where a flight rises from a landing, and at the foot of every raked coping, a kneeler stands: a big stone with a level bed, its top cut to the rake. At each flight-to-landing break, one knee stone carries both the raked top and the level top. Until now this was reasoned from gable practice and from BTCV ([research-masonry.md](research-masonry.md), section 2).

### What the sources say

**Rivington's *Notes on Building Construction*, Part I (new revised edition, 1904), pp. 53–55, figs. 132 and 146.**
- The kneeler, or knee stone, is "the stone at the foot of a coping on a gable or ramped wall" (p. 54).
- A stone cut off vertically below the coping is "objectionable". It is "better that the kneeler should tail into the wall", so that it has "a base much greater than its height" and the walling above holds it down (p. 55).
- On a steep ramp, the coping is dowelled to the wall or worked "with a horizontal bed", deep enough to enter the wall. This is done not with every stone but "only at intervals in the length of the coping" (p. 53).
- Coping stones with triangular backs cut "vertically downwards" are common but worse: on a steep pitch they "tilt over" (pp. 53–54).
- Scottish footnotes: a coping in this position is a "skew", and the skew corbel is a "club-skew" (p. 54).

**Mitchell's *Building Construction and Drawing*, First Stage (6th edition, 1902), pp. 74–77, figs. 216–225.**
- The skew corbel is a projecting stone "at the lowest part" of the gable. It carries the first coping stone and resists its sliding; it is often tied in with long iron cramps.
- A kneeler is "a long stone, tailing well into the gable wall".
- Steep copings need support "at their lower ends and at intervals". The foot gets a shoulder or a skew corbel, and the "intermediate supports are obtained by kneelers": stones with a part worked as coping and the rest tailing into the wall.
- Mitchell's 1898 elementary course calls the kneeler "the lowest stone" of the gable's triangle (p. 45).

**1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica*, "Masonry", vol. 17, fig. 7** (Wikisource scan page 863).
- The skew corbel is placed at the base of a sloping coping "to resist any sliding tendency".
- Stones "placed for a similar purpose at intervals along the sloping side" are kneelers. They tail into the wall and have "the section of the coping worked upon them".

**Gwilt's *Encyclopaedia of Architecture* (1888), glossary.**
- *Summer stone* (another name for the skew corbel): the first piece of the coping is worked "in the solid" of the lowest stone, which "so becomes an abutment and support for the rest" (p. 1364).
- *Ramp*: in handrails, the concave rise over a landing, which often brings "a knee above the ramp". The word is also used for "any concave form, as in coping": a continuous line joining a higher part to a lower one (p. 1318).

**Rivington Part II (1901), p. 129, handrails.** The ramp is the sudden concave rise. The knee is "the convex part of the sudden rise"; a swan neck is both together. This is joinery, not masonry, but it names the two breaks in our stair: concave where a flight rises from a landing, convex where it arrives on one.

**BTCV, *Dry Stone Walling*** ([Walls on slopes](https://conservationhandbooks.com/?p=584); [Coping on slopes](https://conservationhandbooks.com/?p=585)).
- A wall head is built at the base of a steep slope, or "at a sharp upward break in slope". The head "helps support the weight of the wall above it".
- Lay stones "on the true horizontal, whatever the slope".
- Long stones that project hold the coping in place.
- Slab copes "are always set to a true level". On steeper slopes the wall takes "a stepped top" built as a series of partial heads.

**Purchase's *Practical Masonry* (1904), a terrace stair with a raking balustrade (Plates XVI–XIX).**
- The joints of the raking plinth and capping are set "at right angles to the line of rake" (pp. 42–44).
- The plinth starts with a line parallel to the nosings.
- A small level seat is worked on the raking stones for each baluster, "in order that a level bed may be obtained" (p. 48).

**Steps built into the wall.** This was not asked, but it bears on the redraw.
- McKay, *Building Construction* vol. 2 (1944): external steps are "built solidly into a wall at one end" (p. 115). Spandrel steps have a square seating built in, with a wall hold of 4½ to 9 in (p. 116). "The weight of wall tailing down the fixed ends" must hold them (p. 117).
- Purchase, p. 137: "stooling is left on the end for pinning into the wall".

### Verdict

- **Kneeler at the foot of a raked coping: confirmed.** Rivington names the "ramped wall" as well as the gable, and a stair's raked side wall is one. No text names a stair wall as such, so that step is a small inference. Shape, as the sources give it:
  - a level bed;
  - tailing into the wall;
  - a base wider than its height;
  - the coping's section worked on its top.

  It need not project. In Mitchell, the projecting starter stone is the skew corbel, and the kneeler is the tailed stone.
- **Corrected: add intermediate kneelers.** All three textbooks put level-bedded, tailed stones "at intervals" along a long raked coping, and BTCV adds long projecting stones. None gives a spacing. We should choose one from the coping's length and pitch and mark it as our own choice.
- **Knee stone at a flight-to-landing break: still unsourced.** No text or listing found describes a stair wall's coping turning between rake and level.
  - At the *concave* break, where a flight rises from a landing, a stop is supported: BTCV's head at "a sharp upward break in slope", and the kneeler's role at any foot. Gwilt's ramp shows that copings did carry a concave turn worked in stone. So one shaped stone carrying both tops is plausible there.
  - At the *convex* break, where a flight arrives on a landing, nothing pushes. A plain joint on the break is as defensible as a knee stone.
  - The listings in research-masonry.md show the other answers in real stair walls: "coped and ramped down" (Bury Manor) and "stepped in stages" (49 Marine Drive).
- **Open question for the reviewer.** The rule assumes a raked coping. BTCV sets slab copes level and steps them on steep slopes, and one stair listing does the same. A phyllite or slate slab coping in a Stavanger lane may be stepped, not raked. Photographs #1 to #9 can settle it once Øystein approves fetching them.

**Not found.** No Riksantikvaren or other Norwegian guidance on stair walls turned up. Searches for vangemur, trapp and dekkhelle found only forums. Lueger's *Lexikon der gesamten Technik* (1904, vol. 4, p. 191, "Freitreppe") says only that a stair's ends are bounded by Wangen, and that frost is guarded against by iron ties or the cut of the joints. For more it refers to *Handbuch der Architektur* III.6, p. 136 ff., which was not read. Historic England and HES listings use "kneeler" only for gables in what was found.
