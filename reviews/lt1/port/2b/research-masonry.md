# Masonry: how masons lay steps, cheek walls, copings and courses

Subject craft for the stone redraw, 22 October 2026, read online only. Claims from search summaries, forums or memory are marked (secondary). Claims no source confirmed, and our own arithmetic or reading, are marked (unverified). It skips what `docs/research/stone.md` already holds: steps resting on the step below, fall on treads, batter, through-stones, slab copings.

## In short

1. **Every bed is level.** No course follows the pitch. "Lay the stones on the true horizontal, whatever the slope" (BTCV). Only the stepped footing and the coping follow it.
2. **A course starts on the footing and ends in the coping.** "On a slope, each layer starts with a footing stone at one end and finishes with a topstone at the other" (BTCV). Through a change of pitch the courses run on unchanged; only their ends move.
3. **Footings step** in level steps once the ground rises more than about 1 in 20 (Fine Homebuilding). A stair, about 1 in 2.3, is far past this.
4. **A stop at every upward break.** Where a flight rises from a rest, build a head (dry) or set a kneeler (mortared): a big stone with a level bed that takes the foot of the raked coping and resists its push. Carry the coping across the break (BTCV).
5. **Copings step or rake, never on a sloping bed.** Slab copes are set level and stepped. A raked coping sits on level-bedded stones, stopped at its foot, with rebated, joggled, dowelled or cramped joints. Upright copes usually lean downhill (BTCV; EB 1911; Purchase).
6. **Steps bear front on back, rebated, built into the wall** about 15 cm (McKay 1944).
7. **A rest is two to four large slabs, joggle-jointed**, or setts inside a granite kerb.
8. **Bond by numbers:** no perpend through more than two courses; at most three joints meeting; one header per two stretchers; no stone longer than four to five times its height (DIN 1053-1).
9. **Granite marks are few and large:** split faces, half-round drill holes on some arrises, hammer-pointed or axed tops, slightly rounded nosings.

## 1. Courses beside a stair and through a change of pitch

**Dry walls.** BTCV's dry-stone handbook is the clearest source. Footings are stepped so each stone is level along and across the slope; the steeper the slope, the more they overlap. Building starts at the bottom, or stones slide. Courses lie on the true horizontal, each beginning as a footing stone uphill and ending as a topstone downhill. Some wallers set their lines along the slope, which is easier for grading but harder to keep tight: a minority practice. The Peak District says facing stones go in horizontal courses on sloping ground, and on steep gradients "horizontally and stepped" (secondary).

**What decides it.** Gradient: under 1 ft in 20 ft a wall may follow the ground; steeper, step the foundation, "otherwise, the stones will gradually slide downhill" (Fine Homebuilding). Dry or mortared does not change the rule, since sloping beds slide either way. Coursed and squared work show the level courses plainly; random rubble hides them but keeps level beds (unverified; DIN puts beds square to the load).

**Change of pitch.** BTCV: "Build a wall head at the base of a steep slope, or at a sharp upward break in slope", tie it in on both sides, "and carry the coping across the break". On a long steep run, a head about every 20 m. In a lane, going up, the rest-to-flight break gets a near-vertical joint of ties and runners, and the flight's coping bears on it (unverified, applied to a stair). At a flight-to-rest break nothing pushes, so a plain joint will do (unverified).

**Real stair walls.** St Wenn, Cornwall: granite steps between low rubble walls with chamfered granite copings, "stepped up" at the entrance. Bury Manor: side walls "coped and ramped down" to piers. 49 Marine Drive, Edinburgh: snecked rubble, saddleback coping "stepped in stages". Eggleston: a mid-19th-century flight between coursed dry rubble walls with rubble copings. Edinburgh's Scotsman Steps: copes stapled with stainless cramps. No source shows courses bending with the pitch.

## 2. Copings on a sloping wall

**Types** (BTCV). Upright copes: random, level-topped, locked with driven pinnings, or buck and doe (cock and hen: tall and short alternating). Tilted copes. Flat slabs, which "must be quite heavy". Coverbands. Shaped copes (half-round, triangular), "normally set on a level top" in mortar. On slopes "a tilted coping is easier to build and more secure", each stone holding down its neighbour.

**Lean.** Written sources prefer uphill. Downhill, built from the bottom up, is "probably the most widespread method". Vertical copes suit only very steep slopes where tilted stones cannot grip (BTCV).

**Stepped.** Slab copes are set level: "Place each stone so that the top corner at the downhill end touches the line". On steep slopes the steps grow too big, and an extra course is added. Garden walls take "a stepped top" of partial heads (BTCV). A stepped coping one course high and k goings long tracks the stair: a 30 cm course over two 15 cm risers needs two 35 cm goings, 70 cm (unverified, our arithmetic).

**Kept from sliding.** The gable is the documented case. A kneeler is "a large approximately triangular stone at the foot of a gable, cut to have a horizontal bed and a top conforming ... to the slope" (Nottinghamshire HER); in Scotland the skewputt carries the skew copes (Historic Scotland). Kneelers tail into the wall. Coping joints are rebated (Purchase), or cramped, dowelled and joggled (EB 1911). A head's topstone "must be heavy enough to resist the push of the rest of the coping" (BTCV). That raked stair copings work the same way is unverified.

**At a rest** the coping runs level. At the knee either a joint falls on the break or one stone carries both a raked and a level top (unverified).

## 3. Stone steps

**Kinds.** Solid rectangular (block) steps are strongest; tread-and-riser steps are thin slabs; spandrel steps are roughly triangular, ends built into the wall, meeting at a splayed rebate (McKay; EB 1911). A lane is block steps (unverified).

**Lap.** Each step rests "upon the back edge of the one below", on newel stairs "two or three inches at least" (EB 1911). Modern makers give 2 to 3 cm (secondary). A common modern Norwegian granite step is 37 by 15 cm, 100 to 200 cm long, about 150 kg a metre (sale listings; secondary).

**Nosing, fall, joints.** "The top edge of the nosing should be slightly rounded off", sometimes chamfered; external treads get "a slight fall" (McKay): about 10 mm forward (Swedish stone federation; secondary). Old steps were bedded in mortar (McKay); modern open joints are about 5 mm (secondary).

**Ends.** Steps are pinned into the wall at one or both ends (EB 1911), 6 in. (15 cm) for entrance steps (McKay). An 1884 German handbook seats step ends on stone cheeks 15 to 20 cm wide and two to two and a half risers high (secondary). Or the wall rises from the steps "to a convenient height and finished with a coping" (McKay). A retaining wall that a stair passes through ends "to a head at either side" (BTCV). Built together, step ends tail in; a stair laid later butts the wall (unverified).

**Wider than one stone.** At most two stones across; no running joint up the flight; never piece a tread in depth (Mother Earth News; secondary). Joints staggered towards the sides, not the middle (Pavingexpert forum; secondary).

**Rests.** A half-space landing of four joggle-jointed slabs built into the walls, a quarter-space of two; joggles stopped at the free ends so only butt joints show (McKay). Landing joints: joggle, rebated, or bird's-mouth with grout and slate dowels (Purchase). Helston: "wide cobbled steps edged with granite kerbs".

## 4. Wall stones and bonding

**Kinds** (EB 1911). Random rubble: irregular, under about 9 in. thick. Coursed rubble: "levelled up in courses 12 or 18 in. deep". Irregularly coursed squared rubble: courses "jump abruptly from one level to another", gaps filled with snecks. Regular coursed: one height per course. Ashlar: joints "about an eighth of an inch or less", length at most three times height. Polygonal work, *kyklopebinding*, forms "a network in the wall face" (SNL).

**Rules** (DIN 1053-1, via bauprofessor.de). Stones on their natural bed. Rubble levelled at least every 1.5 m. Roughly squared coursed work: beds and joints worked square 12 cm deep; course height may change within a course. Irregular coursed work: joints up to 3 cm (secondary). Norway's V220: bond along the wall, and across it where stones do not span it; no continuous vertical joints (secondary).

**Bond stones.** Headers "about two-thirds" through, "used frequently in every course" (EB 1911). Dry double walls: throughs at 0.5 and 1 m, about 90 cm apart (Peak District; secondary).

**Ends.** Heads alternate ties and runners, "half on one and one on half", use "the biggest, most regular stones", finish with two runners and a large square topstone, and are not face-pinned (BTCV). Rubble quoins "serve as a gauge for the rest of the walling" (EB 1911).

**For our town, 1850 to 1960** (unverified). Dry walls of split granite or gneiss, roughly squared and coursed, largest low, wedged from behind. From about 1890 the same walls mortared or concrete-backed (`repairs.md`). Polygonal work plausible on rock. Copings of large split slabs or long blocks. Ashlar only at the best addresses.

## 5. Marks a mason leaves

**Splitting.** Plug and feather leaves half-round holes about 10 to 20 cm apart (Wikipedia; secondary); on Dartmoor it replaced wedge and groove about 1800. Hidden faces and back arrises keep the holes; seen faces were dressed (unverified).

**Dressing.** Aberdeen masons dressed granite with a pointed hammer, "nidged-work" (Gwilt 1842, via the Scots dictionary). "Single axed work is done with an axe or hexagonal headed hammer" (McKay). The bush hammer: many thin plates give a smooth face, few thick ones a rough face (Aberdeen museum; secondary). Rock-faced blocks have a rough middle (McKay). Flamed and sawn faces are modern (dating unverified).

**A Norwegian step of 1900** (unverified). Fevik's granite firms hired men for "trappestenshuggning" (stair-stone hewing) in 1882, advertised granite stairs in 1894, and were reported in a Stavanger paper in 1889. Expect few large facets: split sides and bottom, a pointed or axed top and front, a slightly rounded nosing, perhaps a drafted margin on the front arris. Texture fine and even within each facet.
