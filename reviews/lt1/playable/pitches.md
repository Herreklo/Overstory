# The playable corner: the creative round

Phase 0, step 1 of `brief.md`, run on 22 October on Opus.

**Method.**
- **A scout** read world 9989 through the atlas, the records and the raw export. It wrote the verified case material that every case designer built on (Appendix A).
- **Five case designers in roles** wrote 21 pitches: deduction, systemic sim, narrative, management, and an inventor from outside games.
- **Three testing designers** wrote 18 pitches: a tools lead, a user researcher, and an experimental designer.
- Every agent worked from the same constraints: the game rule, `first-game-options.md`, the paper playtests' interim note, and the six studies' bottom lines.

**Selection.** The main session checked the shortlist's key events against the export and built a lean packet: four case pitches and the proposed testing design. Fable picked from it (`pick.md`).

The pitches in full are in Appendices B and C.

## The testing design as proposed to Fable

This is the brief's strong option, weighed by the three testing designers. Fable trimmed it in `pick.md`.

**Kept from the strong option:**
- one game core in plain C# (facts, clock, knowledge store, telling rule, scorer);
- no model speaking at play time;
- agents playing the real build;
- Øystein playing himself;
- other humans only through the engine's human test.

**Changed:**
- **The port is lockstep**, with one client, `tools/ovs.py`, run from a sandbox folder.
- **Verbs work at intent:** go, look, lean, ask, tell, note, wait, commit.
- **The game clock moves only by commands.**
- **The agent gets interface text every turn**, and a frame only when the view changes or on a look, at the player's size.
- **Two modes:** tester mode adds the records in view; reader mode gives frames and text only.

**Added:**
- exact replay by a hash chain, Øystein's sessions recorded with takeover, and golden sessions;
- a leak tracer;
- a gate before any model plays: the blind solver; the text-only solver, which must do worse; the ask-everyone and wait-out bots; an eye exam;
- a RITE round of three agents and Øystein's three sessions;
- the cross-examined account, the frame budget and sliding doors;
- the larger pre-registered round later, as its own experiment.

## Findings from the round worth keeping

- **Every `late` event in world 9989 is a woman:** six of six (Molly Scott in 1989 and 1998, Kate Holmes 1991, Sylvia Waller 1993 and 1995, Tess Lane 1996). The `redo` events are mixed. Six is small, so this may be chance. But any case that asks "who failed" over this run would keep landing on women, and that is why the festival case was set aside (Rule 8). It goes in the next milestone note to Understory, as a finding that bears on its method, not a request.
- **Where the atlas and the export disagree,** trust the export. John Holmes's running-down of Ivan Tanner came out in 1988, not 1993. The engine calls every festival "the summer festival", but 1989's falls in December.
- **The export keeps only each habit's latest run.** Rosters for 1989, such as Hannah's Fridays, must be rebuilt from `habit:broke` and `keepaway` events and checked by script.
- **Private visits are the corner's best evidence.** They sit on no page, they are on the steps, and the engine makes them again: Andy Shaw's last September (e-8881 to e-8883: Trevor Lane, Ben Reed, and Oliver Brook of house B) has the same shape as Agnes's last November, so it is the case's second seed.
- **The engine has 21 quiet gifts to 14 people,** 1990 to 1998, a renewable source for a later case built on attention.

## Appendix A: the case material (the scout's verified digest of world 9989)

For the five pitch writers of the playable corner (Phase 0). Read on 22 October 2026, with read-only queries, from four sources: `fixtures/engine/understory-world-9989.json` (Understory `af02971`, seed 9989), the atlas, the records and the corner fixtures.

- **Dates are re-dated by −37:** the engine's 2026 to 2036 is the town's 1989 to January 1999. (`engine-rules.md` says −40, but that was for the old packs.) Months are the engine's. Days and hours are always ours.
- **Labels.** *Canonical:* an engine event, given by its id. *Authored:* our choice. *GM-simulated:* produced by rule during play. *Derived:* computed from engine facts by a stated rule.
- **Visibility.** *Private:* only the people in `who`. *Local:* those people, plus their partners and coworkers, plus by chance anyone who works with people (the Anchor, the clinic, the school, the Courier). *Public:* the whole town, faintly.
- **Notability** is in thousandths: 3000 is a death, 200 is texture.
- **Protected acts.** Private acts that nobody ever found out are left out of this digest.

- **Market Square and the cathedral**, at the Basin's head.
- **The Penny Ferry**, which crosses from Harbour Row to Barrel Quay every twenty minutes.

### C. Engine limits that bind every case

**Death.**
- There are four natural causes and nothing else: no killing, accidents or drowning. Accidents only injure, for two to six months.
- A death's `who` may include a partner or a child. That is grief, not finding.
- **Nobody is recorded as finding or certifying the dead.** There are no funerals or wills (request 31).

**Crime.**
- No police, courts or inquests.
- Dishonesty is small: skimming, covering up a mistake, letting someone take the blame. It becomes known through nine `caught` events and one `scheme:found`.
- Hostility is words and coldness, never violence. Anything harder is GM-simulated fiction outside the engine.

**Kin.**

## Appendix C: the testing pitches

### Greybox testing from the tools side (T1, test-automation lead)

In a deterministic game the unit of testing is the command log: Age of Empires sent commands, not state (Bettner and Terrano, 2001); Factorio hashes its state every tick and reports the first that desyncs. Our world adds a record of what is true and who could know it. So the build is the only game master, models only play, and every word a model is told traces to a fact it may have. Examples use option 6.

#### 1. The lockstep port

**What.** Every input, from Øystein's mouse or an agent, becomes a `Command` handed to one game core in plain C#. Agents drive the real Mac build through a localhost port; the world holds still between turns.

**The port.** `-agent-port 47811 -seed 1989 -session <dir>`, windowed, drawing on demand. JSON lines over TCP on 127.0.0.1; a small client, `tools/ovs.py`, is the only command an agent may run. Verbs: `go` (a route on the ways graph, never keys), `look` (a handle, or a pixel of the last frame, via the id render), `lean` (a full-size crop), `ask`, `note`, `choose`, `wait`, `account`. A game clock in whole seconds moves only by commands: `go` costs the path at 1.4 m/s (55% on flights), `ask` five minutes and one of the day's questions. People stand where plan and clock put them. The reply: clock, position, interface text, what was heard, a frame path if the view changed, and `h`. The frame is the 2560×1440 player render after eight warm-up renders, saved at 1333×750 (about 1,300 tokens); `lean` gives a 512×512 full-size crop (about 350). The agent reads a frame only if it chooses.

**Determinism.** `h_n = hash(h_{n-1}, command, clock, knowledge, case, pose)` on the repo's spec (`Hash.cs`). `ovs replay --verify` reruns the log and must meet every `h`; `--frames` compares pixel hashes, bit-exact on the M4 with URP's dither held fixed or off (it changes every frame: `docs/measuring.md`, motion sheets).

**Facts.** A leak tracer. Every emitted string is assembled from claims carrying a fact id and a channel (seen, heard, read). The log keeps the provenance; the agent sees opaque handles (`r7`, never `ev-death-cause`). `ovs audit` proves each claim was in view, in the knowledge store, or told by a witness whose record lets them know it. Answers to `ask` are templates over records. No model phrases anything.

**Finds:** dead ends, unanswerable asks, unreachable facts, leaks. **Cannot:** feel, pace, motion, where people look.

**Cost:** 4 days, if the ways graph and the people's movers come with the case: command layer 0.5, server and client 1, perception text from the id render 1, frames and chain 0.5, tracer and audit 1.

**Risk:** the visible-records list makes the frame redundant, so agents never look; hence reader mode (pitch 4).

#### 2. Golden sessions

**What.** Øystein's and chosen agent sessions, in the port's format, replayed against every build. A changed outcome names the first turn or second to differ.

**How.** Øystein plays in real time. The build records his commands by 60 Hz tick, the walker's pose every tick (poses, not inputs; Fiedler), a hash each second, and note-key snapshots. Replay drives the camera from the poses and reruns the commands; drift stops at the first diverging second with a state diff. Anyone can take over at turn n, like Tekken 8's replay takeover. Note-key frames are re-compared by `golden_compare.py`. The set: the solver's shortest win and a designed loss, Øystein's first session, one per persona; a design change re-accepts one with its diff. The build writes a one-page report on quitting; Claude reads that, not the log.

**Finds:** regressions in rules, facts and staging (the stone's port can't silently break the case); what Øystein saw when he noted "confused". **Cannot:** say whether a changed outcome is better.

**Cost:** 2 days: recorder 1, replay 0.5, runner and report 0.5.

**Risk:** goldens every design change breaks. Compare the logic chain; pixels only at notes.

#### 3. The blind solver and the evidence bisector

**What.** A search bot with no model plays the case thousands of times, headless. It proves the case winnable from what a player can reach, and finds the evidence a win rests on.

**How.** Unity batch mode without graphics runs the core (dotnet would be faster but needs an install). Visibility is a bake: from each node and heading on the ways graph, the id render records what is in view and its screen share, hashed. Beam search over the verbs within the clock (three days, three questions a day). Delta debugging (Zeller, 2002) removes perceptions from each win until it breaks, leaving the minimal evidence. Three checks: solvable; no single clue gives it away (option 6's rule); winnable from text alone, with every perception that exists only in pixels (a worn tread, a renewed board) removed. If the text-only solver wins, reading the world doesn't pay and the case is redesigned before any agent plays. Then exploits: ask everyone, wait out the clock, reload for foreknowledge. Each solver log must give the same chain in the build.

**Finds:** fairness, load-bearing traces, dominant strategies, unreachable facts. **Cannot:** say a person would see what is in view; the bake is an upper bound.

**Cost:** 2.5 days: runner 0.5, bake 1, search and bisector 1.

**Risk:** the search space, bounded by the clock. We found no studio that delta-debugs a player's evidence: it needs every perception to be a fact with an id.

#### 4. Players with an eye exam

**What.** Three personas play the build through the port, each on two models, after an eye exam measures what each model can see at our pixel size.

**How.** The sceptic, the explorer and the optimiser, on Opus and Sonnet, so persona and model aren't confounded; Haiku smoke-tests the loop. Testers get the visible-records list (hover names, positions, shares) and frames on request; readers get frames and interface text only, so pixels carry the history. Each in-game day is a fresh agent reading only its own journal, so context stays near 40k (the Pokémon runs degraded past 100k). Agents run in a folder holding only the client and their journal, with Claude Code permissions allowing `ovs` and nothing else: the paper's players kept out of the files on honour, and a shell can read the fixture. The account is a form (who, where, when, why, each citing evidence), so code scores it against the sealed key. The exam: 40 build frames with known answers (is this tread newer than its neighbour, is this yesterday's figure).

**Finds:** unmet needs, misread evidence, where reading stalls, which chains are harder. **Cannot:** fun, calm, the wish to return; two Anthropic models are not two opinions.

**Cost:** 1.5 days: scripts, sandbox, persona cards and scorer 1; the exam 0.5, from pitch 5's frames.

**Risk:** tokens. My estimate, unmeasured: 25 turns and 12 frames a day, about 0.6 million input tokens a day (mostly cache reads), 2 million a session. The first run measures it.

#### 5. The same eyes, another history (no studio has done this)

**What.** Any session, Øystein's or an agent's, replays along its exact poses with the history changed: layers off (`OS_HISTORY_OFF`, one keyword), the fabric date moved (one uniform), or one record's history swapped for a labelled test twin. Blind readers judge what history's pixels added along real paths.

**How.** Pairs: wherever a key trace is in view on a real path, render both ways; a reader says which holds the trace. Same eyes: a fresh agent sees the frames Øystein saw at each of his notes, in one world or the other, then gives the account. The difference is what the traces gave someone looking where he looked.

**Finds:** per trace and pose, whether history reads at all (criterion 1). **Cannot:** speak for human eyes. **Facts:** swapped frames are test material, never play.

**Cost:** 1.5 days: overrides 0.5, per-record swap 0.5, pairs and scoring 0.5.

**Risk:** history off changes every surface, so readers spot the arm, not the trace; hence the per-record swap. Studios can't: their history is baked art; ours is a function of a date, with a record behind every pixel.

#### The strong option, point by point

- **One core, rules and facts in code: keep,** command-first: the interface emits the port's commands, so Øystein's and agents' sessions share one log.
- **Agents play the real build: keep, not for everything.** Solvability, exploits and coverage run headless at no tokens.
- **A frame every turn: change.** About 1,300 tokens a turn, re-read every later turn, and agents play better from state (TITAN, BALROG). Text every turn; a frame when the view changes, read by choice; crops to lean in.
- **How the agent sees: add** the size (1333×750, which hides shimmer), the crop and two modes; without reader mode the text spoils the legibility test.
- **Turn granularity and clock: add.** Verbs at a player's intent, routes on the ways graph, a game clock that holds between turns (paused, VideoGameBench's best score tripled); pressure is days and questions, not wall time.
- **Exact replay: define it** (the chain, recorded poses), and **add** Øystein's recorder, note key, report and takeover, and golden sessions.
- **Personas across models: keep, smaller.** Three personas, two models, one run each plus two repeats. Another maker's model isn't worth asking for in Phase 2: with code as game master, nothing needs auditing. Ask later if readers and Øystein disagree.
- **Telemetry: keep.** Path and per-record screen share: per turn for agents, twice a second for Øystein (that id render costed first). Agent paths show coverage, never attention.
- **Missing:** the leak tracer, the sandbox, the solver before any agent, the account as a form, the eye exam.

#### The combination

All five, lean: the port with its tracer (4 days), golden sessions (2), the solver with the text-only check (2.5), three personas on two models with the exam (1.5), the same eyes with history off and one swap (1.5). **About 11.5 working days** for one Opus builder, beside the case; dropping the swap and the bisector saves 1.5.

By the end of Phase 2 it shows:
- whether the case can be won, and won without looking at the world, before any model plays;
- eight agent sessions replaying to the hash, with path maps, scored accounts, needs and a measured token cost;
- an audit with zero leaks, or the list;
- what Opus and Sonnet can see at our size, and whether each key trace reads along real paths;
- Øystein's sessions recorded and replayable, his path beside the agents';
- a golden set the stone's port runs against.

What would change our minds: models failing the exam (reader agents go; Øystein alone judges pixels), or the text-only solver winning (the case is redesigned first).

### Greybox testing of the playable corner: the user researcher's pitch (T2)

Phase 2 has one human, who wrote the brief, and agents from one maker. It can't show that strangers enjoy the case. It can show, by numbers no judge produces, that the case is built like a game, that reading the world pays for the players we have, and where play stalls. Its best verdict is "ready for strangers", never "fun".

**The shared base:** the port in step mode, the session file with exact replay, id-render telemetry, and the sealed key with its scorer, in 7 to 10 days. The account is committed in slots (who, where, when, why, who is answerable), each citing journal entries, so a script scores it; a free-text "step by step?" follows. A *case-play* (one 15 to 20 minute case) is about 100 commands and 40 to 60 frames. My estimate: about 150k new tokens, 3 to 4M processed with the context reset from the journal every 20 commands, probably near a paper run (1.2% of a week). Run one measures it.

#### 1. Six pitches

**1. The reading ladder.** Fresh agents play the same case and seed in three arms that differ only in what can be read: the full screen (S), the screen with history layers off (S-off: people and routines visible, traces gone), and interface text with no frames (R0).
- *Who:* four personas (sceptic, optimiser, explorer, hurried reader) on Opus and Sonnet. A scripted no-read bot sets the floor.
- *Decides:* traces pay if the account score (0 to 100) in S beats S-off by 15 or more, in 7 of 8 pairs and within each model. Five or less: decoration. The world pays at all if S beats R0 by 15. A page arm is optional and never decides, since text favours the page; a screen win against that bias counts.
- *Bias:* pairs share an agent cell, so shared errors cancel. The coder is blind to the arm; traces count only where the perception probe passes.
- *Cost:* +1 day. One case-play per cell and arm; R0 about half.
- *Cannot show:* that people read traces as models do.

**2. Clue knockouts** (new: in neither study's sources; I know of no published use). Replays are exact and every trace belongs to a record, so one clue can be removed while all else stays identical. A *memory knockout* reruns the account step with one perceived clue struck from the journal. A *world knockout* forks the session from before the clue was first seen, with that record's layer off.
- *Decides:* a clue is decisive in a run if striking it moves the score by 10 or more. Pass: at least three key clues are decisive in half the runs that saw them, and one is a trace in the world, not a card. Cited but never decisive is decoration; decisive but rarely seen is hidden.
- *Bias:* a counterfactual inside one agent's reasoning, with no judge. Knockouts remove what can be seen, never a fact.
- *Cost:* +1.5 days. About 40k tokens per memory knockout; half a case-play per world fork.
- *Cannot show:* what a human's reasoning rests on.

**3. The cross-examined account** (new). A script traces every account claim to the moment it was perceived: the frame where its record was on screen (with its pixel share), or the card. A claim with nothing perceived behind it is invented by definition; no model judges. The paper round's leaps become a count.
- *Who:* every agent, and Øystein.
- *Decides:* a funnel per key fact: reachable on screen, in view, looked at, noted, cited, right. Pass: unsourced causal claims at most 10% of claims in S, none held "sure". False histories in S-off no more than in R0: the screen plants no history in texture.
- *Bias:* mechanical. A coder on a model other than the case's builder only splits the retelling into claims.
- *Cost:* +1 day; about 60k tokens per account.
- *Cannot show:* that seeing was understanding.

**4. Two seeds and the carried journal.** Each S player plays two cases on different seeds, half A then B and half B then A, carrying only its own journal into the second.
- *Who:* the eight S players; Øystein plays seed B second, its key sealed from him.
- *Decides:* mastery if case two beats case one by 10 or more in 6 of 8 players, and sourced citations don't fall. If four S-off players carrying journals score within 5 of S on the second seed, the shape is learned without reading (Shadows of Doubt's staleness).
- *Cost:* +1.5 to 2 days for the second case. Two case-plays per player.
- *Cannot show:* human learning, or staleness over twenty cases.

**5. The maker's sessions:** RITE, watched in silence, debriefed over the replay. Øystein plays three times: seed A, thinking aloud; seed B silent, before any agent report on it; then trying to win without reading. A script picks three replay moments (the longest look, the longest deliberation, the first claim the key marks wrong) for a ten-minute debrief with two prompts.
- *Decides:* a zero-tolerance list: reach three places in under a minute each; open a card; say the goal and how to lose; tell "you saw" from "Ruth said"; commit an account. He names one trade-off unprompted; his break session scores 15 or more below his reading.
- *Bias:* a maker's liking counts for nothing (Dell: 2.5-fold), and the curse of knowledge rules out his readings. Only his behaviour and challenge-card bets count. Agent findings he contradicts are logged.
- *Cost:* +1 day; three hours of his; about 150k tokens.
- *Cannot show:* a stranger's experience.

**6. The breaker and the stall map** (is it a game?). A no-read bot on the interface text, a random walker and the optimiser try to win without reading, beside a weak anchor: the case with no fail state and free questions. Telemetry maps stalls, stretches with no new information.
- *Decides:*
  - of 16 S case-plays, at least 3 fail and 3 win;
  - the bot wins under 1 in 10 of 20 runs;
  - asking everyone everything scores 15 or more below the median S run;
  - no option at the costly decision takes over 80% of runs on a seed;
  - stalls over 15 moves come in at most 1 run in 4.

  If the anchor doesn't fail two of these, the measures are blind.
- *Bias:* the bots are our only players outside the Anthropic family.
- *Cost:* +1.5 days. Bots cost no tokens; the anchor, two case-plays.
- *Cannot show:* that a structure that passes is fun.

#### 2. The strong option, point by point

#### 3. The recommended programme: the ladder, the breaker and the maker

1. **Gate (scripts only).**
   - Three sessions replay with hashes matching every second.
   - On both seeds, the solver reaches every key fact from what can be perceived, no single clue settles the account, and every key trace is visible from a reachable pose.
   - The perception probe: 40 crops with known answers. A trace counts in play only for models that get 80% right; the rest go to the cold readers.
2. **Round 1, RITE:** three case-plays (an Opus sceptic, a Sonnet optimiser, a Haiku hurried reader) and Øystein's first session, with fixes checked on the next run.
3. **Round 2,** committed in advance, about 29 case-plays:
   - 8 S players, two counterbalanced cases each (16);
   - 8 fresh players in S-off;
   - 4 in R0;
   - 2 on the anchor;
   - memory knockouts on every S account (world forks only if budget allows);
   - 40 bot runs;
   - Øystein's second and third sessions.

**Pass lines:**
- *A game:* pitch 6's lines, with the anchor failing two.
- *Reading pays:* S beats S-off and R0 by 15 or more (S-off in 7 of 8 pairs, within each model). Three decisive clues, one a world trace. Better readers win more: Spearman 0.4 or more between reading and outcome over the 16 S case-plays, positive within each model.
- *Mastery:* pitch 4's 6-of-8 line (the staleness check waits for budget). *Honest reading:* pitch 3's.
- *The maker:* the list passes by session 3.

Gaps between 5 and 15 are unsettled. Fable judges from a lean packet: the scores, funnels, stall map and three replay moments.

**What would change our minds:**
- *S near S-off but above R0:* people and routines carry the case; the traces need a role first.
- *S near R0:* the interface carries it, a book; change the case.
- *A bot or the optimiser wins like a reader:* make the costs bite.
- *The anchor passes:* the measures are blind.
- *Agents pass, Øystein never deliberates or fails:* his behaviour wins.
- *Everything passes:* strangers are Øystein's call.

### Greybox testing: pitches from an experimental designer (T3)

All seven build on the strong option's session file, id-render record lists, notes log and sealed key. Several add **the branch point**: replay the world exactly to frame T, then hand a fresh agent its own transcript up to T. The world replays exactly but the agent does not, since Claude Code agents can't be seeded, so every branch test carries a re-roll control: the unchanged branch, rerun from T. Costs are Phase 2 days beyond the strong option. A run is perhaps 60 to 100 turns; keeping only the latest frame, I'd guess 2 to 4 million tokens, mostly cache reads (unmeasured).

#### 1. The frame budget

**Idea.** Ration looking. The same persona plays the same case with an allowance of 0, 3, 12 or unlimited frames; the interface text stays on and the traces' accessibility description stays off.

**How it runs.** Past its allowance, the port returns text only. "Go to" works by named place, so a player without frames can still move. A fifth arm gets the engine's page for the case's people instead of frames. Two personas on two models in five arms make 20 runs. Logged: key score per arm, and where frames are spent.

**What it answers.** Does reading the world pay, as a dose-response curve. If 0 frames win as often as unlimited ones, the picture is decoration and the case is the page. The slope is the value of looking. Where scarce frames go shows salience. No human playtest can run one person blind.

**Precedent.** None I know. BALROG's text-versus-image arms rated models, not a game's picture.

**Cost.** Half a day.

**Failure mode.** Models read pixels badly (45 per cent on what changed between two frames, VideoGameQA-Bench), so the curve understates frames' worth to a person: a flat curve is a warning until Øystein's play agrees. If text alone wins, look first for interface text that tells what the picture should show.

#### 2. Sliding doors

**Idea.** Fork the player's mind at every costly choice. Replay to the decision, force the other option on a fresh agent with the same transcript, and play on.

**How it runs.** The case's three or four costly decisions each get three branches: as chosen, the other option, and nothing (that one choice's twin: the player stays home). Each runs twice, with the chosen branch re-rolled as the noise floor. That is about six full runs per trunk. The trunk can be Øystein's own session file, with agents continuing from his notes.

**What it answers.** Is it a game. A decision whose branches score the same is not a decision. One branch winning for every persona is a solved choice. A good one splits by what the player read. On Øystein's trunk it prices each of his choices, which one play can't.

**Precedent.** Partial. Restricted play (Jaffe and colleagues, 2012) and tree search fork simulated players. Forking a reasoning player's transcript, or a human's session, against an exact world: none I know.

**Cost.** 1 to 1.5 days: the branch point, then a runner.

**Failure mode.** A forced agent rationalises a choice it didn't make, so a branch measures the option, not the player who would take it. Agents on Øystein's trunk are not Øystein. Cap it at four decisions.

#### 3. The planted fact

**Idea.** Mutation testing for evidence. Flip one scored fact in a test-only fixture and see whether any account moves. A fact whose flip changes nothing carried nothing.

**How it runs.** The key's 6 to 10 load-bearing facts each get a mutant. The fixture is edited (a blockout at the Anchor that Friday or not, a step's wear, an account's line reversed), labelled `mutant`, with dependent traces regenerated. A recorded session replays to the first frame where the mutant differs, which the id render gives. Then the agent continues from its transcript. A mutant is killed if the account moves its way. Survivors split into never seen (placement) and seen but ignored (decoration).

**What it answers.** Does reading pay, fact by fact, and how far a false trace misleads. Paired visual and text mutants show whether the picture is used at all.

**Precedent.** Mutation testing in software (DeMillo, Lipton and Sayward, 1978). In games, none I know.

**Cost.** 1 day with pitch 2's branch point, 1.5 alone.

**Failure mode.** A mutant is what the standing rule forbids: a scene claiming a false history. It stays in flagged test fixtures, never in Øystein's build or a capture. Weak pixel readers let visual mutants survive, overstating decoration.

#### 4. Devil's counsel

**Idea.** Before anyone plays, a red-team agent with the sealed key writes the wrong accounts a fair player could hold. Each that survives everything the screen can show is an ambiguity found before a player falls into it.

**How it runs.** An Opus agent gets the key and the reach list: every record that reached a frame or card in any run, plus the interface's text. It writes up to five false accounts. Each must assert something the record denies, citing on-screen items that seem to support it. A second agent, without the key, tries to refute each from the reach list. A survivor is fixed with a separating fact put on screen, or kept as a named trap in the key. After play: how many wrong accounts did it predict?

**What it answers.** Is it fair, is it misleading, before players pay.

**Precedent.** Detective fiction's fair-play rules (Knox, 1929) and uniqueness checks in puzzle generators. A red team searching a world's visible evidence against its ground truth: none I know.

**Cost.** Half a day to a day.

**Failure mode.** Absence of evidence permits anything ("a visitor no one saw"); assert-and-cite limits it. Same-maker blindness: it finds the misreadings Claude makes, not people's.

#### 5. Show me where you saw it

**Idea.** Every claim in the final account cites a note, and every note is pinned to a frame and the records then on screen. A script then says, claim by claim, whether the screen could have told the player that.

**How it runs.** "Note" stores the frame's records and any open card. "Choose" requires citations, like Ace Attorney's present-evidence. Each claim is seen and right; seen and wrong (the screen misled: fix the scene); unseen and right (a leak: check the persona prompt and cards); unseen and wrong (the player's own leap); or uncited. An Opus coder scores claims against the key, as now; the join is a script.

**What it answers.** Is it misleading, and whose fault. The paper playtests found 100 of 115 misreadings were players' own leaps and 7 came from a screen. This makes the split automatic, so agent invention can't drown the scene's faults.

**Precedent.** None I know, as an audit.

**Cost.** Half a day.

**Failure mode.** Citing everything becomes a ritual, so count citations whose records don't support the claim. For Øystein, who won't cite, use the note key's timeline. Citations also change the game: a mechanic, or a chore.

#### 6. Reincarnation

**Idea.** Agents can do what no human tester can: forget completely. A finished player passes a 150-word note to a fresh instance of itself; nothing else carries.

**How it runs.** On the same case, the gain is what the note carried. On another case from the record, it is method only; the export has four natural deaths between December 1989 and October 1998. Notes are coded as answers ("the second account is wrong") or methods ("check a step's wear before trusting a route").

**What it answers.** Does it hold a player through mastery. A gain on the same case and none on a new one means the game teaches answers, not reading: a one-shot.

**Precedent.** Outer Wilds' knowledge-gated loop, as design; our Telephone measures drift. Splitting knowledge from skill with clean-slate minds: none I know.

**Cost.** Half a day on one case. A second case is 1.5 to 2 days of fixtures, likely after Phase 2.

**Failure mode.** 150 words test note-writing as much as learning; a model reads its own phrasing easily. With one case, the skill half goes unmeasured.

#### 7. The item ladder

**Idea.** The record grades each question before play; players' accuracy should climb the same ladder.

**How it runs.** Every scored item gets a rung. Free: the town's frequencies answer it, as the engine's base rate does (58.0 per cent). Earned: a local or public event that reached a frame supports it. Hidden: only private events do (1,271 of the export's 2,635 events are private). A fair score rests on free and earned items. After play: if earned items sit at chance while free ones score high, players guess from priors; a hidden item answered right is a leak. Once Understory publishes its readers' answers per decision, earned becomes exact: the calibrated witness (64.6) right, the base rate wrong. It joins the request bundle.

**What it answers.** Is it fair, and does reading pay, within each run.

**Precedent.** Pre-calibrated test items (item response theory). A simulation's own readers as calibrators: none I know.

**Cost.** Half a day from the export; exact rungs wait on the engine.

**Failure mode.** Who could know is not what the screen showed, so rungs need the reach list. With 15 to 20 items, rungs are noisy.

#### Verdict on the strong option

Facts in code end the game master's slips: four of five lives-off masters saw real lines, and NCP-Bench narrators contradicted their ledger in 40 to 68 per cent of sessions. The real build means the tested frames are the shipped ones. Exact sessions, telemetry and Øystein at the keys are right. Personas across Anthropic models are one opinion with spread.

The one thing it misses: it uses determinism to rewatch, never to intervene. A straight playthrough is one sample of one path, with the agent's noise mixed into the game's signal. It can't say whether the picture, a trace or a choice made the difference: nothing was held fixed while one thing changed. Two or three agent players can't make "better readers win more" a correlation. This world's rare power is the controlled counterfactual: same world, same mind up to turn T, one thing changed. Agents should be instruments in controlled arms, not stand-ins for players.

#### The two I would add

1. **Sliding doors.** The standing rule's first test, decisions that cost, can't be read from one path. On Øystein's trunk, his single play shows what each choice was worth. It builds the branch point the planted fact needs next.
2. **The frame budget.** It puts Overstory's founding question, the screen against the page, to the build in half a day in the same port. Its page arm is a small version of criterion 4's blind test.

Together: 1.5 to 2 days, one runner.
