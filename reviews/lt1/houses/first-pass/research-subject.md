# Harbour-street houses, Stavanger, as they stood in 1989

Subject research, first pass, 27 October 2026. **[M]** is a figure from Mandal, a comparable south-coast wooden town, used where Stavanger gave none. **(inf.)** is my own inference.

**The place.** Gamle Stavanger: small working people's houses of 1770–1900, 1½ storeys, low plinth. The 1946 plan meant to clear them; 154 were protected by 1975.

## 1. Cladding and trims

- **Horizontal boards** are the west-coast norm because they shed driving rain.
  - Classical houses (c. 1790–1875) have **enkeltfalset** boards, single-rebated so each laps the one below.
  - **Dobbeltfalset** (double-rebated) was common from 1850 and is the sveitser cladding (c. 1865–1910). It was often fitted to the street front only, while gables and backs kept the old boards [M].
  - **Pløyd staffpanel** ("pølsekledning", beaded) ran 1860–1930. On an old house it is usually the newest skin [M].
- **Boards** are 25–32 mm thick, slow-grown pine, laid heart side out, with the best boards on the weather wall. Widths vary within a wall. Ends meet in sloping, water-shedding scarfs, staggered so no vertical band forms. I found no board width in mm for Stavanger.
- **Trims** (Stavanger 1860–1915). Classical trims are flat and plain. From 1885 a firm (Øgreid) sold trims by catalogue, so profiles repeat along a street.

  | Part | Size |
  | --- | --- |
  | Side trims | 120–125 mm wide (140–150 on big houses), about 25 mm thick, plain or 2–3 flutes |
  | Drip board over the window | About 30 mm thick at about 20°. The upper one stands 180 mm proud, the lower 120 mm. |
  | Frame to upper drip board | About 250 mm |
  | Brackets | 480 × 125 × 50 mm |
  | Blocks | 180 × 125 × 50 mm |
  | Parts per window | 15–20 |
  | Corner boards | Wider than the window trims |
  | Old drip boards | 5/4" at 25–28°, against 17° today [M] |
  | Barge boards | 28–34 × 195–220 mm; a thin one is a post-1940 replacement [M] |

- **Overhangs.** Almost none on classical roofs. Sveitser roofs have a deep one with rafter ends showing. Some were later sawn short.
- **Later.** Battened cladding came about 1950, plastic cladding in the 1980s.

## 2. Paint

- **Systems by date.**
  - Linseed oil paint was the rule until about 1960.
  - Alkyd oil paint (Drygolin) came in 1948, and pure alkyd in the early 1960s.
  - Acrylic was used beside alkyd from the 1960s. No source dated when it became normal on exterior wood.
- **How each ages.**
  - **Linseed** mattes, chalks and thins to the grain, then scales in small flakes. It was repainted every 8–15 years, the south wall more often.
  - **Alkyd over linseed** forms a thick, tight film that blisters and peels in sheets. On shaded walls it crazes.
  - **By 1989**, a typical wall has linseed at the bottom and alkyd, maybe acrylic, on top. It cracks and flakes through the layers.
  - **Colour drift.** Light colours drift greyer, yellower or pinker, and dark ones fade. At Breidablikk each repaint matched the faded coat, so the colours grew stronger over a century.
- **Repainting.** Loose paint is scraped along the grain with a steel scraper, the wall is washed, then repainted. A new or spot-painted board therefore reads flatter, cleaner and whiter than its neighbours, whose scraped edges show stepped islands of old paint (inf.).
- **Colour by period.**
  - Before about 1850 white was dear: white fronts, red or ochre backs, dark windows.
  - 1850–84: ochres, browns, white and grey, dark windows, contrasting trims.
  - 1884–1910: whites and greys. 1910–40: green trims, white with blue.
  - 1945–55: alkyd brought pastels, dark red and blue.
- **1989.**
  - **Gamle Stavanger was all white**, with white windows and white picket fences. This was the preservation scheme's choice from 1956, which also removed outhouses and plank fences. Its one critic says the area shows no age, wear, old repairs or decay.
  - **The trehusbyen beyond** is mixed: many post-war coats of blue, green or yellow with white windows. "Stavangerblå" is NCS S 7020-R90B.
  - **Doors** were most often green, otherwise blue-black, red or brown [M]. Plinths kept their own material colour.
  - **Who chose.** Inside Gamle Stavanger a rule chose the colour. Elsewhere the owner chose within the fashion of the last repaint, so colour tracks the repaint date, not the building date (inf.).

- **One documented front.** Øvre Strandgate 90 (1836) has sand cladding, white trims, grey windows and a black door.

## 3. Windows and doors

- **Originals.**
  - Classical windows have two sashes on a centre post, with 3 panes per sash in the empire type. Larger houses have krysspost windows.
  - Sveitser windows are krysspost or T-post, with 4–6 panes.
  - Old glass is uneven, with thin bars and linseed putty.
  - Storm sashes came about 1900, then coupled sashes, then sealed units in the mid-1950s.
  - Doors were paired, with square panels and an overlight, and opened inward. In 1930–50 many became one leaf with wide side panels.
- **1960s–80s replacements.** The **husmorvindu** is one big pane on a pivot hinge, sold as easy to wash.
  - One often replaced two windows, and openings were often made smaller.
  - The trims were usually lost.
  - Window replacement with loss of trims is the most common change in the trehusbyen.
  - On an old front it reads as a dark, glassy blank in a thin white frame, smaller than its neighbours, with a board patch or paint ghost showing the old opening (inf.).
- **Not found.** No Stavanger source described aluminium or teak-look frames, or factory doors.

## 4. Roofs, chimneys, plinths

- **Roof coverings.**
  - Roofs pitch 38–45° [M].
  - Classical roofs carry red **enkeltkrum** pantiles on battens about 30 cm apart, with at least 6 cm overlap. The tiles last 100–150 years; frost spalls their undersides.
  - Sveitser roofs carry slate or flat tile.
  - In the 1960s–70s concrete tiles replaced slate and clay. They wear out sooner and wash out to grey.
  - Outbuildings carry corrugated iron, rusting red-brown. Tar paper and Eternit: not confirmed for Stavanger.
- **Gutters.** They were rare before sveitser. They were zinc, later galvanised, and PVC from the 1960s. The local rule: zinc gutters painted the wall colour, hung high on hooks, with straight-bend downpipes.
- **Chimneys.** A classical house has one big square chimney at mid-ridge. A sveitser house has two or more with corbelled crowns. Chimneys are brick, rendered where required, with lead flashing and a zinc or slate cap.
- **Plinths.** Grey fieldstone, dry-laid or in lime mortar, low on classical houses; concrete in the 1900s. Bare or rendered, some later painted grey.

## 5. Kept and neglected in the 1980s

- **Kept houses.** Oil money from 1969 paid for "upgrades", not restoration. A kept house in 1989 typically had husmorvinduer, concrete tiles, sometimes plastic cladding, and fresh alkyd. Tidegeilen 9 (1865) got husmorvinduer and concrete tiles in the 1980s.
- **Neglected houses.** Klinkenberggata was cleared about 1960–65. Pedersgata 81 was "falleferdig" (near collapse) in September 1989.
- **What fails first:**
  1. Paint on the lowest boards and under the sills.
  2. Window bottom rails and lower sashes rot, and the putty cracks out.
  3. The lowest boards, the bottom 10–20 cm of vertical panel, and boards at downpipes, joints and end grain rot. North walls stay damp longest.
  4. Leaking gutters stain and rot the wall below.
  5. Tiles slip and spall.
- **How repairs show:**
  - A board is cut out and a new piece scarfed in.
  - The cladding is lowered and a new board added under the cornice.
  - Boards are turned round.
  - New trims are thinner and plainer.
  - Fresh paint sits on new wood beside scraped old layers.
